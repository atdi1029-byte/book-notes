# Winning the Loser's Game — Charles D. Ellis (7th Edition, 2017)
# Raw Notes

## Structure
- 28 chapters + 3 appendices, 278 pages
- Practical finance / investment philosophy book
- Core thesis: index investing beats active management

## Preface (pp. 15-16)
- N001 [MUST KNOW] Ellis's core message: the principles of successful investing NEVER change — companies, markets, economies change, but core principles don't
- N002 [SHOULD KNOW] Written from 50+ years of learning from outstanding practitioners and expert theorists
- N003 [NICE TO KNOW] Dedicated to wife Linda Lorimer — learned that maximizing quantitative results is not as important as assuring financial security and freedom

## Introduction by David F. Swensen (pp. 17-20)
- N004 [MUST KNOW] Book stands alongside Malkiel's "A Random Walk Down Wall Street" and Bogle's "Common Sense on Mutual Funds" — pantheon of individual investor books
- N005 [MUST KNOW] The crux: mutual fund managers fail fiduciary responsibility by not limiting assets under management. They succumb to bloated funds that generate bloated profits for the MANAGER, not the investor
- N006 [MUST KNOW] Why size kills performance: larger fund needs more positions (50-100 instead of 20 best ideas). The 50th best idea is far worse than the 20th. Larger funds must invest in heavily researched, efficiently priced large companies. Smaller nimble funds have an advantage picking less-researched securities
- N007 [SHOULD KNOW] Mutual fund managers breach fiduciary duty with 60-80% annual portfolio turnover — creates tax bills for investors in rising markets. A true fiduciary would use longer horizons and close high-turnover funds to taxable investors
- N008 [MUST KNOW] Swensen (Yale CIO) initially indexed half Yale's endowment. But found some inherited active managers were genuinely adding value. Over years, Yale moved from index to active — returning 12.9% vs 8.8% for passive 60/40 portfolio over 30 years, adding $28.2 billion in value
- N009 [MUST KNOW] KEY CAVEAT: Yale has 30 full-time investment professionals, access to extraordinary managers not available to ordinary investors, and conducts extreme due diligence (one partner contacted a manager's 3rd grade teacher). Without substantial resources and dedicated staff, "it is nearly impossible to succeed in the cutthroat world of active management"
- N010 [MUST KNOW] Swensen's conclusion: the correct approaches are at the EXTREMES — either index everything (right for almost all individuals and most institutions) or manage everything actively (right only for wealthy individuals/institutions with extraordinary resources). The "sloppy middle" of paying high fees for mediocre active management is the worst place
- N011 [SHOULD KNOW] Swensen's two disagreements with Ellis: (1) Ellis underestimates diversification beyond equities — Swensen would add Treasury bonds and TIPS; (2) Ellis suggests young investors might use modest leverage — Swensen worries this exacerbates chase-performance behavior

## Chapter 1: The Loser's Game (pp. 21-28)
- N012 [MUST KNOW] Core statistic: 70% of mutual funds underperform their benchmarks over 1 year; over 10 years, over 80% underperform. Very few beat market averages over the long haul, and nobody can tell in advance which ones will
- N013 [MUST KNOW] The tennis analogy (from Dr. Simon Ramo's "Extraordinary Tennis for the Ordinary Tennis Player"): Tennis is TWO different games. Professional tennis is a WINNER'S game — outcome determined by winner's brilliant shots (80% of points WON). Amateur tennis is a LOSER'S game — outcome determined by loser's mistakes (80% of points LOST). Strategy: don't try to win points, just avoid losing them
- N014 [MUST KNOW] Why investing became a loser's game: Institutions now do 98%+ of all trading (up from 10% in the 1960s). They ARE the market. Professional investors collectively determine prices, making it nearly impossible for any one of them to consistently outperform the consensus they collectively create
- N015 [MUST KNOW] The cost math: With 80% annual portfolio turnover, trading costs of 1% to buy/1% to sell, plus 1.25% in fees = 3.25% annual operating costs before taxes. To match the market's 7% expected return, a manager must earn 10.25% gross — outperforming the consensus of experts by more than 46%
- N016 [SHOULD KNOW] Winner's games self-destruct by attracting too many players — like gold rushes. Investment management evolved from winner's to loser's game because professionals flooded in with superb info, computers, and determination
- N017 [SHOULD KNOW] Admiral Samuel Eliot Morison (Strategy and Compromise): "In warfare, mistakes are inevitable... the side that makes the fewest strategic errors wins the war." Same principle applies to investing
- N018 [MUST KNOW] Active investing is ALWAYS a negative-sum game at the margin — it would be zero-sum except for the large costs (fees, expenses, commissions, market impact) that must be deducted. These costs total billions annually
- N019 [SHOULD KNOW] Past performance is NOT predictive: managers with superior past results are not particularly likely to have superior future results. Only the WORST decile has predictive power — chronic high fees and incompetence persist. "The past is not prologue except for the grim finding that those who have repetitively done badly are likely to stay in their slough of despair"
- N020 [MUST KNOW] The hardest part of investing is NOT figuring out the optimal policy — it's STAYING COMMITTED to that policy through bull and bear markets. Disraeli called it "constancy to purpose." The cost of infidelity to your own commitments is very high
- N021 [SHOULD KNOW] Peter Drucker distinction: working EFFICIENTLY = doing things the right way; working EFFECTIVELY = doing the right things. Indexing = doing the right thing
- N022 [NICE TO KNOW] Day trading is "the worst of all: a sucker's game. Don't do it, ever."
- N023 [NICE TO KNOW] The "liquidity trap" — investors buy stocks they wouldn't buy if markets weren't liquid, creating a hidden cost of transactions

## Chapter 2: The Winner's Game (pp. 29-40)
- N024 [MUST KNOW] Investment management has two parts: the PROFESSION (doing what's best for clients' returns) and the BUSINESS (doing what's best for managers' profits). There is a continuing struggle between the VALUES of the profession and the ECONOMICS of the business — and the business is winning
- N025 [MUST KNOW] Three errors of commission/omission by investment professionals:
  1. ERROR OF COMMISSION #1: Falsely defining the mission as "beating the market" — this had good prospects 50 years ago but not today. Of the top 20 pension fund managers 40 years ago, only 1 is still in the top 20. In the UK, only 2 of the top 20 from 30 years ago remain
  2. ERROR OF COMMISSION #2: Letting the business dominate the profession — assets managed rose tenfold, fees as % of assets multiplied 5x. "Asset gathering" displaces investment performance as the priority
  3. ERROR OF OMISSION: Losing sight of effective investment COUNSELING — helping clients understand risks, set realistic objectives, choose right asset classes, stay the course
- N026 [MUST KNOW] The most valuable thing investment professionals can do is the LEAST difficult: investment counseling (helping clients set realistic goals, understand risk, stay the course). Price discovery (beating the market) is fascinating but extraordinarily difficult and getting harder
- N027 [SHOULD KNOW] Performance data is misleadingly reported: (1) time-weighted not value-weighted, so doesn't reflect actual investor experience; (2) "losers" underperform 1.5x as much as "winners" outperform; (3) no tax adjustment for 60-80% turnover; (4) buy-after-the-best, sell-after-the-worst behavior obliterates roughly one-third of long-term returns
- N028 [SHOULD KNOW] Consultant data is flawed — removing backdating and survivor bias often shifts consultants' reported "better than market" records to "below market"
- N029 [MUST KNOW] The skiing analogy: At Vail/Aspen, everyone has a great day because each skier chooses trails matched to their skills and interests — bunny slopes to expert runs. Investment counseling should do the same: match each investor to the program right for their skills, experience, financial situation, and risk tolerance. When the match is right, everyone wins
- N030 [SHOULD KNOW] Changes that made markets a loser's game: NYSE volume up 1000x (3M to 5B shares/day); 120,000+ CFA charterholders (from zero 50 years ago); Reg FD commoditized corporate information; 320,000+ Bloomberg terminals; algorithmic trading; hedge funds and PE as major forces; globalization

## Chapter 3: Beating the Market (pp. 41-42, beginning)
- N031 [MUST KNOW] The only way to beat the market: discover and exploit other active investors' mistakes. In a liquid professional market, prices are set by those most confident they know more than consensus and willing to commit substantial money
- N032 [MUST KNOW] Four theoretical approaches to beating the market: (1) market timing, (2) stock selection, (3) timely changes in portfolio structure/strategy, (4) developing a superior long-term investment concept
- N033 [MUST KNOW] Why market timing fails: the first weeks of a market recovery produce a SUBSTANTIAL proportion of all gains. At the crucial market bottom, a timer is most likely to be OUT — missing the best part of recovery gains. Stock market gains occur in very brief periods when investors are captive to conventional consensus
- N034 [MUST KNOW] Missing best days data (Fig 3.1, 1980-2016): US equities returned 11.4% annually. Remove just the 10 best days (out of ~10,000 trading days = 0.1%) → return drops to 9.2% (19% cut). Remove 20 best → 7.7%. Remove 30 best → 6.4%. ALL total returns on stocks in 20 years were achieved in the best 35 days — less than 1% of 5,000 trading days
- N035 [SHOULD KNOW] Missing best days over 112 years: missing just the 10 best days out of 49,910 days = missing two-thirds of total gains. Removing 5 best DAYS from 72 YEARS would reduce cumulative returns by nearly 50%
- N036 [SHOULD KNOW] "Just as there are old pilots and bold pilots, but no old, bold pilots, there are no investors who have achieved recurring successes in market timing"
- N037 [MUST KNOW] Stock picking doesn't work either: stocks managers sell typically do about as WELL as those they buy. Terrance Odean ("Why Do Investors Trade Too Much?") found retail investors' buys underperformed market by 2.7% while stocks they SOLD outperformed by 0.5%. Professional fund managers' trading subtracted 0.78% from returns (Lakonishok/Shleifer/Vishny). Plexus Consulting: typical purchase added 0.67% short-term but typical sale subtracted 1.8%
- N038 [SHOULD KNOW] "Nifty Fifty" example: 1970s growth stock investors had great results as two-tier market developed, but by late 1970s same stocks produced exceptionally negative results. Same pattern with oil stocks in '80s, pharma in '90s, tech in 2000, financials in 2008
- N039 [MUST KNOW] The antique fair analogy for market efficiency: Scenario 1 (alone for 2 hours) = easy pickings. Scenario 2 (24 expert shoppers) = harder. Scenario 3 (1,000 shoppers after experts) = slim pickings. Scenario 4 (50,000 shoppers, day 3, all also sellers, all prices known) = no mispriced bargains. The stock market is scenario 4
- N040 [SHOULD KNOW] Key distinction: the key to market success is not your skill compared to other individuals, but the skill with which EACH SPECIFIC TRANSACTION is made. If 90% of the pros trade with more skill, your transactions will be in the bottom quartile
- N041 [MUST KNOW] Four powerful truths wise investors understand: (1) Most important decision = long-term asset mix (stocks, RE, bonds, cash). (2) Asset mix determined by purpose, time horizon, and ability to stay the course. (3) Diversify within and among asset classes. (4) Be patient and persistent — good things come in spurts, usually when least expected. "Plan your play and play your plan"
- N042 [SHOULD KNOW] Market's apparent irrationality explained: prices reflect PERCEPTIONS of future value — estimates of estimates of estimates. The market's pricing mechanism is neither stable nor consistent, like the "butterfly of chaos." Economists joke the stock market has anticipated nine of the last three recessions

## Chapter 4: Mr. Market and Mr. Value (pp. 55-60)
- N043 [MUST KNOW] Two characters every investor must understand: MR. MARKET (short-term, emotional, unpredictable — from Benjamin Graham's "The Intelligent Investor") and MR. VALUE (long-term, stolid, consistent, does the real economic work). Mr. Market gets all the attention, Mr. Value does all the work. Mr. Market is an "economic gigolo" — his only objective is to be attractive
- N044 [MUST KNOW] Weather vs. climate analogy: daily weather ≠ long-term climate. Similarly, daily market changes ≠ long-term investment reality. Don't choose where to build a home based on last week's weather; don't choose investments based on recent market conditions
- N045 [MUST KNOW] The biggest risk in investing is NOT Mr. Market or Mr. Value — it's the emotional incapacities of the investor. "Know thyself" is the cardinal rule. The hardest work in investing is not intellectual; it's emotional
- N046 [SHOULD KNOW] "Investing is NOT entertainment — it's a sober responsibility. It's not supposed to be fun or 'interesting.' It's a continuous process, like refining petroleum or manufacturing cookies. If anything in the process is 'interesting,' it's almost surely wrong. That's why benign neglect is, for most investors, the secret to long-term success"
- N047 [SHOULD KNOW] Tom Wolfe's "The Right Stuff" analogy: young test pilots never recognized that "unique events" causing "inexplicable accidents" were actually integral dangers of flying beyond comfort zones — same as investors surprised by "six sigma events" that are actually within the normal bell curve of market experiences
- N048 [SHOULD KNOW] Regression to the mean dominates the long term — unusually high stock prices are NOT good for you because you'll eventually give back every increment above the long-term central trend

## Chapter 5: The Investor's Dream Team (pp. 61-62, beginning)
- N049 [MUST KNOW] The dream team thought experiment: imagine having Buffett, Munger, Swensen, Soros, Einhorn, plus all analysts at Goldman, Morgan Stanley, Merrill Lynch, all hedge fund managers — ALL working for you. You CAN have them all, for free. Just index — because the index fund replicates the market, which reflects the accumulated expertise of all these professionals making their best judgments on pricing
- N050 [MUST KNOW] Indexing benefits beyond performance: (1) lower fees, (2) lower taxes, (3) lower operating expenses, (4) peace of mind — no regret about past mistakes or worry about future ones. These persistent costs of active investing "do as much harm to portfolios as termites do to homes"
- N051 [SHOULD KNOW] Efficient market defined: not perfectly efficient, but sufficiently efficient that wise investors cannot expect to exploit inefficiencies regularly. "Random walk" — price changes follow unpredictable patterns. Even quarterly earnings that aren't immediately discounted present opportunities too small for large portfolios to exploit
- N052 [MUST KNOW] Warren Buffett endorses indexing: "Most investors, both institutional and individual, will find that the best way to own common stocks is through an index fund that charges minimal fees"
- N053 [SHOULD KNOW] The freedom of indexing: it gives you the FREEDOM to play active investing only when and where you want — not as the default. This carries the reciprocal responsibility to invest away from index funds ONLY when the incremental reward fully justifies the incremental risk
- N054 [MUST KNOW] Table 5.1 — Active management assertions vs reality: (1) "Tracking error in small caps/emerging" → yes but far smaller than active managers' errors. (2) "Index funds stuck with overpriced stocks like GE" → they also held them when cheap. (3) "Settling for average is un-American" → matching the market IS the winning strategy because the average fund/investor falls SHORT of the market. (4) "Active managers can go defensive" → most don't time it right, random patterns offset each other. (5) "Active must work or managers would disappear" → casinos are jammed with continuous losers who keep returning. (6) "Index funds underperform after fees" → by only ~0.10%, and Morningstar's star ratings have NO predictive value while low fees DO predict outperformance
- N055 [MUST KNOW] Advantages of indexing never mentioned by active advocates: (1) TAXES — turnover 5-10% vs 60-80%, zero short-term gains; (2) HIGH FEES — unrelenting drain; (3) TRADING COSTS — much lower; (4) PEACE OF MIND — no worry about manager style drift, being acquired, or overloaded with money
- N056 [SHOULD KNOW] Flip the argument: imagine an experienced indexer being pitched to switch to active — "Fees 10x higher, 80% of managers fall short long-term, underperformers do worse than winners do better, no known way to identify winners in advance, deteriorating managers hard to spot." Nobody would switch
- N057 [MUST KNOW] Two categories of risk: INVESTMENT RISK (market-driven, can't control much) and INVESTOR RISK (self-inflicted, can control a lot). Focus should be on investor risk because you can actually reduce it. "The market is what it is. As with weather, we can choose an agreeable climate, but we must accept day-to-day changes"
- N058 [SHOULD KNOW] Morningstar finding: star ratings based on past performance have little or NO value in predicting future performance. BUT low-fee funds in every category outperform other funds. Fees are the only reliable predictor
- N059 [SHOULD KNOW] Thomas Kuhn (Structure of Scientific Revolutions): change is hard for those who built careers on old assumptions. Active managers resist indexing for the same reason — their incomes depend on it. Upton Sinclair: "It is difficult to get a man to understand something when his salary depends on his not understanding it"
- N060 [SHOULD KNOW] ETF warning: 100+ single-country ETFs, 150+ single-commodity ETFs, 200+ leveraged/reverse-leverage ETFs. Unless you're an expert with specific reason, don't touch specialized ETFs

## Chapter 6: Investor Risk and Behavioral Economics (pp. 69-78)
- N061 [MUST KNOW] Pogo: "We have met the enemy and he is us." The true order of importance in investing (Fig 6.1): WRONG concept = Manager biggest, Market middle, Investor smallest. RIGHT concept = INVESTOR biggest, Market middle, Manager smallest. The investor has by far the most important role today
- N062 [MUST KNOW] Behavioral biases list: (1) Fail to appreciate regression to mean. (2) Ignore base rates. (3) Believe in "hot hands"/streaks. (4) Anchoring to first impressions. (5) Illusion of control, underestimate bad events. (6) Hindsight/outcome bias. (7) Overrely on experts. (8) Halo effect. (9) Overweight dramatic/media events. (10) Overestimate own skills. (11) Overly impressed by short-term results. (12) Confirmation bias. (13) Anchor to early estimates. (14) Self-serving perception distortion. (15) Confuse familiarity with understanding. (16) Overreact to news. (17) 80% think they're above average — as drivers, listeners, dancers, parents, investors. 80% also believe their children are above average
- N063 [MUST KNOW] Investor risks to avoid: (1) Trying too hard/too much market risk. (2) Not trying hard enough (too much cash/bonds). (3) Being impatient — 10% annual = <1% monthly, check prices max once per quarter. (4) Changing funds after <10 years — "investing should be marital, not dating." Average investor return is sharply lower than fund returns because of buy-high-sell-low switching. (5) Borrowing too much — 3 of 4 fortunes lost used borrowed money. (6) Being naively optimistic. (7) Being proud — "The stock doesn't know you own it." (8) Being emotional
- N064 [MUST KNOW] The Venn diagram (Fig 6.2): every investor has a ZONE OF COMPETENCE (real skill) and a ZONE OF COMFORT (where you stay calm/rational). Your sweet spot is the OVERLAP. "Sell down to the sleeping point." Don't go outside competence (costly mistakes) or comfort (emotional decisions)
- N065 [SHOULD KNOW] "Don't lose" advice: Wall Street training program anecdote — trainee asks wealthy partner how to get rich, answer is "Don't lose!" Seemed dumb at first, but 50 years later they all understood: when you're down 50%, you need a DOUBLE just to get back to even
- N066 [SHOULD KNOW] Captain Sullenberger analogy — use simple checklists to guide behavior because we don't realize how irrational we are. JFK: "At least, let's not do it to ourselves"
- N067 [SHOULD KNOW] Four ways to reduce investor risk: (1) avoid operational mistakes, (2) determine realistic objectives, (3) design sensible long-term strategy, (4) stay committed to your program. When designing, consider how you'd be tempted to react to unusual markets (both favorable and unfavorable) — unusual events WILL come again

## Chapter 7: Your "Unfair" Competitive Advantage: Indexing (pp. 79-80, beginning)
- N068 [MUST KNOW] Three ways to beat the market — physically difficult (work harder), intellectually difficult (think deeper — used by very few), emotionally difficult (maintain calm rationality, never get excited or upset — hardest at extremes when most needed). The EASY way: index. "If you can't beat 'em, join 'em"
- N069 [MUST KNOW] Indexing frees you to focus on the one role where you have a DECISIVE advantage: knowing yourself and your own objectives. Accept markets as they are, concentrate on designing a long-term portfolio that meets two tests: (1) you can live with the market risks even at extremes, (2) long-term expected results will meet your objectives
- N070 [MUST KNOW] Unfair advantages of indexing: (1) Higher returns — 80% of active managers fall short long-term. (2) Lower fees — 10 basis points vs 100-120 bps, compounding year after year. (3) Lower taxes — turnover <10% vs 60%+, saving ~1% of assets/year = 15% of today's expected 7% returns. (4) Lower commissions — turnover <10% vs 60%+. (5) Lower market impact costs. (6) Convenience. (7) Freedom from errors — no timing, stock picking, or manager selection decisions that can go wrong. (8) Freedom to focus on truly important decisions
- N071 [SHOULD KNOW] Fired managers typically OUTPERFORM newly hired ones — data shows investors consistently get hiring/firing wrong. Most great winners aren't accepting new money anyway
- N072 [MUST KNOW] Ideal index fund = worldwide total market. Best plain-vanilla mix is about HALF domestic, HALF international. Home-country bias is common everywhere — British invest in UK, Canadians in Canada, Japanese in Japan. May be OK if your country has a large, complex, dynamic economy AND you have large financial obligations in that currency
- N073 [MUST KNOW] Buffett's estimate of annual "horrendous costs" of active investing: $40B trading Fortune 500 stocks + $35B management fees/expenses + $25B spreads/options/annuities = $100B total. This was "just" 1% of Fortune 500 market value, but cut a big chunk from the $334B the Fortune 500 actually earned. Investors got <$250B — just 2.5% return on $10 trillion. "Slim pickings"
- N074 [SHOULD KNOW] Index funds strongest argument in most efficient markets (US large cap, UK, Japan). Even in smaller/less efficient markets, index funds still beat most active managers. But argument for active is relatively stronger in small caps, emerging markets, frontier markets where replication is harder

## Chapter 8: The Paradox (pp. 82-87)

- N075 [SHOULD KNOW] The paradox: funds with very long-term purposes are managed to meet short-term objectives that may be neither feasible nor important. Long-term investments managed by short-term priorities.
- N076 [MUST KNOW] Four steps investors should actually focus on: (1) understand your real investment objectives, (2) define realistic risk/return policies, (3) establish the right asset mix, (4) develop self-discipline to stay on plan through market gyrations.
- N077 [SHOULD KNOW] Ski resort analogy: trails are marked by difficulty so each skier goes to the right slope. Investing should work the same way — 10 factors make each investor unique: age, assets, income, time horizon, dependents, experience, risk tolerance, likely inheritance, intended bequests, philanthropic aspirations.
- N078 [MUST KNOW] Investment counseling is MORE important to long-term success than investment management itself, yet most investors don't do the disciplined work of formulating long-term policies. This is the investor's fault, not the manager's.
- N079 [SHOULD KNOW] Six questions every investor should answer: (1) What are the real risks of an adverse short-term outcome? (2) What are your probable emotional reactions to adversity? (3) How knowledgeable are you about market history? (4) What other capital/income resources do you have? (5) Any legal restrictions on your investments? (6) Any unanticipated consequences of interim portfolio fluctuations?
- N080 [NICE TO KNOW] Suggestion to build market resilience: go to a library and spend hours reading daily newspapers from summer/fall 1929, fall 1987, dot-com era, or fall 2008. Getting "up close and personal" with past crises helps you remain calm in the next one.
- N081 [SHOULD KNOW] Avoiding market risk has a real opportunity cost. You should understand the gains and losses at each incremental level of market risk taken AND the opportunity cost of each level of risk NOT taken.

## Chapter 9: Time (pp. 88-92)

- N082 [MUST KNOW] Time transforms investments from least attractive to most attractive and vice versa. The average expected return is not affected by time, but the RANGE of actual returns around that average is greatly affected. Given enough time, seemingly unattractive investments become highly desirable.
- N083 [MUST KNOW] One-day return illustration: a $40 stock with 3.1% daily range and 0.04% daily expected return (8% annual / 250 days) — annualized, that one-day range becomes +/- 387.5%. The annualized return for a one-day hold could be anywhere from +395.5% profit to -379.5% loss. Short-term holdings in stocks are rank speculations, not investments.
- N084 [MUST KNOW] Figure 9.1 data — range of real returns by holding period: 1-year stocks range from +53.4% to -37.3%. Shifting to 5-year periods: far fewer losses, more consistent gains. 10-year: only ONE 10-year loss period, most show 5-15% annual gains. 20-year: NO losses at all, gains cluster around the long-term average.
- N085 [MUST KNOW] Weather vs. climate analogy: short-term market returns are like weather (unpredictable day to day), but long-term returns are like climate (sensible and reliable over time). The patient observer sees true underlying patterns.
- N086 [MUST KNOW] Conventional time horizons drive suboptimal asset allocation. 5-year horizon → 60:40 stocks:bonds. 10-year → 80:20. 15-year → 90:10. But most individual investors have 30-50+ year horizons — they'll be living and investing for much longer than the conventional 10-20 years. Using truly long-term thinking would lead to higher equity allocations and higher long-term returns.
- N087 [SHOULD KNOW] The single most important dimension of investment policy is the asset mix (ratio of fixed-income to equity). The trade-off between risk and reward is driven by one key factor: time.

## Chapter 10: Returns (pp. 93-100)

- N088 [MUST KNOW] Two forms of returns: (1) predictable cash from interest/dividends, (2) unpredictable short-term gains/losses in market price. Investors who focus on capitalizing on price changes by outsmarting others are making a big mistake.
- N089 [SHOULD KNOW] Two main areas dominate stock valuation: (1) consensus on probable amount and timing of future earnings/dividends, (2) consensus on the discount rate to capitalize that stream into present value. Both change continuously based on estimates of other investors' estimates — ad infinitum.
- N090 [MUST KNOW] For long-term investors, what matters is not today's consensus about the distant future but the consensus that will prevail WHEN we actually get to that future. As holding period lengthens, the discount factor decreases in importance and corporate earnings/dividends increase in importance.
- N091 [SHOULD KNOW] For a very long-term investor, earnings and dividends are overwhelming in importance. For a short-term speculator, everything depends on day-to-day investor psychology. The climate (long-term) is never surprising; the weather (short-term) is frequently surprising.
- N092 [MUST KNOW] Three components of average rate of return: (1) the real risk-free rate, (2) a premium to offset expected inflation's erosion of purchasing power, (3) a premium over the inflation-adjusted risk-free rate to compensate for market risk.
- N093 [MUST KNOW] Treasury bills: appear safe in nominal terms (positive returns almost all years), but inflation-adjusted returns are positive less than 60% of the time. The average annual real return on T-bills after inflation is ZERO. T-bills are just a match for inflation — return OF your money, not return ON your money.
- N094 [SHOULD KNOW] Figure 10.1 data (1900-April 2016): $1 invested grew to — Equities: $38,932.18 (9.51% annual compound). Bonds: $619.68 (5.68%). Cash: $81.81 (3.86%).
- N095 [SHOULD KNOW] Bond return components: maturity premium estimated at 0.9%, default premium on high-grade corporates about 0.5%. Inflation-adjusted real return on long government bonds is slightly above 1.0%, on long-term high-grade corporates nearly 1.5%.
- N096 [MUST KNOW] Inflation-adjusted expected real return on stocks is about 5-6%. Over 25-year periods, real returns are instructive: 6.6% for stocks, 1.8% for bonds.
- N097 [SHOULD KNOW] "Beware of averages": stocks returned an average of 10% per year over 75 years, but actually returned exactly 10% just ONCE (1968). Returns came close to that only three times total. Investors must embrace "average" across delightful AND dreadful markets.
- N098 [MUST KNOW] Inflation impact example: when expected inflation rose from ~2% (1960) to ~10% (1980), the required nominal return on stocks rose from ~9% to ~17%, producing a major reduction in stock prices. After factoring in inflation, the losses during that "adjustment" period were the worst in half a century.
- N099 [SHOULD KNOW] Compound interest table (Figure 10.2): $1 at 8% grows to $4.66 in 20 years; at 10% to $6.73; at 12% to $9.65. Small differences in return rate create enormous differences over time.
- N100 [NICE TO KNOW] Ellis's personal admission: in nearly 50 years of continuous involvement with many of the world's best investors, he found only TWO major opportunities that weren't obvious to many others. That's a discovery rate of once every 25 years.
- N101 [SHOULD KNOW] Four questions to ask when you think you've found a great investment: (1) What could go really right, and how likely? (2) What could go wrong, and how likely? (3) Am I confident enough to invest a significant part of my portfolio? (4) If the price goes down, will I really want to buy a lot more?
- N102 [NICE TO KNOW] Near the millennium, Buffett used the interest rate/corporate profits framework to show why he expected inflation-adjusted returns of only 4% annually — while consensus investors expected nearly 13%. He was right; they were wrong.

## Chapter 11: Investment Risks (pp. 102-109)

- N103 [MUST KNOW] Risk vs. uncertainty distinction: Risk describes expected payoffs when magnitudes and probabilities are KNOWN (like actuarial tables). "Riskiness" in investing is actually uncertainty — what academics mean by beta and market risk. The terms are imprecise.
- N104 [MUST KNOW] Two locations of risk: (1) Market risk — price fluctuations that over time tend to cancel each other out. (2) Investor risk — euphoria at peaks and panic at lows that provoke permanent harm. The great risks of wrong actions at the wrong times are imposed on investors BY themselves.
- N105 [MUST KNOW] Four types of risk active investors face: (1) Price risk — buying at too high a price. (2) Interest rate risk — rates rise more than expected, stocks fall. (3) Business risk — company blunders, earnings don't materialize. (4) Failure risk — company fails completely (Penn Central, Enron, WorldCom, Polaroid).
- N106 [MUST KNOW] Real risk is simple: not enough cash when money is really needed — like running out of gas in the desert. For 401(k) investors especially, the grave risk is running out of money, particularly too late in life to go back to work.
- N107 [MUST KNOW] Three kinds of investment risk from academic research: (1) Market risk — cannot be avoided, investors must be rewarded for taking it. (2) Individual-stock risk (specific risk). (3) Stock-group risk (extra market risk). The last two CAN be avoided or eliminated; investors are NEVER rewarded for accepting them. This is why we should diversify.
- N108 [MUST KNOW] Figure 11.1 — how diversification reduces nonmarket risk: Single stock = 60% specific-issue risk, 15% market-segment, 25% general market. Typical portfolio = 4% specific, 2% segment, 94% market. Several managers = 0.5%/0.5%/99%. Many managers (index) = 0%/0%/100% general market risk.
- N109 [SHOULD KNOW] Optimal market risk level for very long-term investors is MODERATELY ABOVE the market average, because most other investors can't take the long view. A portfolio with 20% more market risk than average is feasible; much more is hard to achieve while staying diversified.
- N110 [MUST KNOW] Total equity return has four components: (1) risk-free return after inflation, (2) equity market risk premium, (3) potential extra return from stock-group exposure, (4) potential extra return from individual stock selection. Components 3 and 4 carry risk you're NOT rewarded for — manage market risk (component 2) instead.
- N111 [MUST KNOW] Portfolio return comes from three sources in order of importance: (1) the level of market risk assumed or avoided, (2) the consistency with which that risk level is maintained through cycles, (3) the skill with which specific-stock and stock-group risk are eliminated through diversification.
- N112 [SHOULD KNOW] True investment risk vs. apparent riskiness is a function of TIME. Stocks are very risky short-term but the apparent riskiness fades if time is long enough. If not confident the market is low, use dollar-cost averaging.
- N113 [SHOULD KNOW] Recovery time data: it took 16 YEARS for the S&P 500 to get back to its 1966 peak, and even longer to regain the 1929 peak. But if you don't need to sell, nominal fluctuations shouldn't matter — they're like stormy weather in faraway places.
- N114 [SHOULD KNOW] Risk tolerance is NOT about how you behave when calmly considering normal markets — it calibrates your behavior when driven by real worries during market extremes, particularly after you've recently been proven wrong.

## Chapter 12: Building Portfolios (pp. 110-114)

- N115 [SHOULD KNOW] Investing is neither art nor science — for most managers it's a problem of ENGINEERING. The key lesson of engineering: define the real problem correctly and you're well on the way to the correct solution. Portfolio management is "investment engineering."
- N116 [SHOULD KNOW] An efficient portfolio has greater expected returns than any other feasible portfolio with equal risk, and less risk than any other feasible portfolio with equal expected returns.
- N117 [SHOULD KNOW] Bond portfolio insights: rating agencies' biggest errors are in estimating GROUP risks, not individual issuer risk vs. peers. Proven again in 2008 with triple-A rated subprime mortgage securities. Credit rating mistakes have happened many times — triple-A street railways in the 1920s all went bankrupt when people switched to automobiles.
- N118 [MUST KNOW] Individual investors should NEVER buy individual corporate bonds — diversification is an absolute necessity in bond investing. Use well-managed bond funds instead. How much to invest in bonds (and whether to invest at all) matters more than which particular bonds.
- N119 [SHOULD KNOW] Portfolios of medium- to lower-grade bonds, after absorbing all defaults, provide higher net returns over time than portfolios of higher-grade issues. Portfolio managers with proprietary credit research can increase risk-adjusted returns by focusing on mispricing in lower-grade bonds.
- N120 [MUST KNOW] The great secret to success in long-term investing is AVOIDING serious, permanent loss. Investing should be primarily DEFENSIVE. Leverage is all too often the instrument of self-destruction. Remember the difference between maximization and optimization — Icarus was a maximizer.

## Chapter 13: Whole-Picture Finance (pp. 115-117)

- N121 [MUST KNOW] Most investors think of their investment portfolio as a standalone entity, separate from other assets. This is a major framing mistake. A 30-year-old MBA shouldn't put 30% in bonds just because "invest your age in bonds" — the net present value of her future earnings (35-45 years of salary) is over 95% of her total financial portfolio and is bond-like (stable income stream).
- N122 [MUST KNOW] When you include human capital (future earnings NPV), a young person's "whole picture" portfolio is already overwhelmingly bond-like. Her securities portfolio should be at least 100% stocks. She might even use moderate margin leverage for an all-equity portfolio.
- N123 [SHOULD KNOW] The whole-picture portfolio also includes capitalized Social Security benefits, pension fund value, 401(k), anticipated inheritance, and home value. When stocks go up or down, the change is only to a part of the whole picture — reducing anxiety from compartmentalized thinking.
- N124 [SHOULD KNOW] Approaching retirement: consider the substantial financial advantages of (1) not claiming Social Security until age 70, and (2) continuing to work. Both are compelling from a whole-picture perspective.
- N125 [NICE TO KNOW] Bonds in a young person's portfolio serve as "anxiety insurance" with a real cost: the lower rate of return. It may be cheaper to invest time and effort into understanding markets and learning to live with volatility than to pay for that insurance.

## Chapter 14: Why Policy Matters (pp. 118-121)

- N126 [MUST KNOW] The principal reason to articulate long-term investment policy explicitly and IN WRITING is to protect yourself from yourself. Misdemeanors in investing are almost all caused by inadequate understanding of our own short-term emotions (internal realm) in response to external market changes.
- N127 [SHOULD KNOW] Without a written policy, investment decisions get resolved in haste during distressing markets — wrong decision, wrong time, wrong reasons. Investors sell AFTER drops (missing the recovery) and buy at market highs (when the record looks most compelling).
- N128 [SHOULD KNOW] Jason Zweig quote: "If we shopped for stocks the way we shop for socks, we'd be better off." We feel good when stocks go up (wrong — they're more expensive now) and bad when they go down (wrong — it's a necessary first step to buying low).
- N129 [SHOULD KNOW] Four characteristics that make people overestimate risk (from anxiety research): large-scale consequences, lack of personal control, unfamiliarity, and sudden occurrence. This is why we fear air travel (30 killed/year) more than driving (45,000 killed/year) — and why sudden market drops cause outsized panic.
- N130 [SHOULD KNOW] Sharp market losses are expected and considered NORMAL by those who have studied stock market history. Drops are predictable in their magnitude and suddenness — just not in their timing. Investors who aren't informed in advance experience unnecessary anxiety.

## Chapter 15: Playing to Win (pp. 122-123, continues)

## Chapter 15: Playing to Win (pp. 122-126)

- N131 [MUST KNOW] Five separate levels of investment decisions: (1) Long-term objectives and asset mix. (2) Equity mix — growth vs. value, large vs. small, domestic vs. international; same for bonds. (3) Active management vs. indexing. (4) Which specific funds/managers. (5) Selecting specific securities and executing trades. Level One is the LEAST costly and MOST valuable. Levels 4-5 are the MOST expensive and LEAST likely to add value.
- N132 [MUST KNOW] The ultimate irony: investors are dazzled by Level Five (stock-picking, trading) — Mr. Market's favorite territory — where costs are high and rewards small. This distracts from Level One (asset mix), where costs are low and rewards are very large.
- N133 [SHOULD KNOW] Separate a "cushion of caution" reserve for current spending from the long-term investment portfolio. The reserve amount should be carefully determined and should NOT influence the much larger long-term portfolio. Cash within the investment portfolio should be minimized — ideally zero.
- N134 [MUST KNOW] Spending decisions should NEVER influence investment decisions — it must be the other way around. Setting portfolio return targets to fund a desired lifestyle is "nonsense" — the market doesn't give a damn what you want to spend.
- N135 [SHOULD KNOW] Four tests for any investment policy: (1) Would it achieve your long-term objectives? (2) Is it written clearly enough that a competent stranger could manage the portfolio? (3) Could you sustain commitment during the worst markets of the past 50 years? (4) Is it realistically designed for your real needs as a long-term investor?

## Chapter 16: Challenges with Performance Measurement (pp. 127-134)

- N136 [MUST KNOW] Barr Rosenberg's statistical finding: it would take 70 YEARS of observations to show conclusively that even a large 2% annual incremental return (a 20% increase over a 10% base rate) was the result of skill rather than chance. Performance stats in ads are drawn from far shorter periods.
- N137 [MUST KNOW] In a typical 12-month period, about 40% of mutual funds beat the market (30% even after taxes). But can they repeat over 10-20 years? Historical data says "No. Not likely." Apparently superior active managers seldom stay superior for long.
- N138 [SHOULD KNOW] Performance measurement is LEAST useful when most needed (short-term, where data is insufficient) and most accurate when least timely (long-term, by which time it's too late to act). By the time performance data are good enough for confidence, the optimal time for action is long past.
- N139 [MUST KNOW] Performance reports are statistical ESTIMATES, not "results." Two-decimal precision ("7.53%") gives the appearance of legitimacy but is just a small sampling from a long-term continuous process. Should be treated with plus-or-minus boundaries.
- N140 [MUST KNOW] Two major biases in performance data: (1) Survivorship bias — poor performers get quietly deleted from databases as if they never existed. (2) Incubation/backdating bias — new funds are "incubated" privately, only the winners get launched publicly with pre-existing track records. Together these biases can add 100+ basis points of distortion — often equaling or exceeding the apparent superiority of selected managers.
- N141 [SHOULD KNOW] "Gee whiz" performance charts can become ordinary by simply adding or subtracting 1-2 years at the start or end of the period shown. Starting point manipulation is a major tool of deception.
- N142 [MUST KNOW] Three factors mixed together in performance data: (1) Sampling error — portfolio may not be representative of the manager's true work. (2) Market environment — may have been favorable/unfavorable for the fund's style (e.g., small caps). (3) Skill (or lack thereof). In the short run, sampling error usually has a MUCH larger impact than skill.
- N143 [MUST KNOW] Figure 16.1 — quartile transition matrix: first-quartile managers in one year had only 29.2% chance of staying first-quartile in the next three years, and 20.6% chance of falling to fourth quartile. The pattern across all quartiles is essentially random — past performance does NOT predict future performance.
- N144 [MUST KNOW] Morningstar ratings: the firm candidly says its star ratings have little or no PREDICTIVE power, yet 100% of new money flowing into mutual funds goes to 4- and 5-star funds. Research shows "there is little statistical evidence that Morningstar's highest-rated funds outperform medium-rated funds." In the months after ratings are handed out, 5-star funds generally earn less than half as much as the broad market index.
- N145 [MUST KNOW] Figure 16.2 — top 20 bull market performers vs. same funds in bear market (out of 3,896 funds): the #1 bull market fund ranked #3,784 in the subsequent bear market. Of the top 20, most ranked in the 3,000s-3,800s in the bear market. Performance reversals are near-total.
- N146 [MUST KNOW] Figure 16.3 — long-term performance of active funds vs. market: 16% were -2% or worse, 57% were between 0 and -2%, 26% were between 0 and +2%, and only 2% were +2% or better. Nearly three-quarters of active funds achieved LESS than market performance. (And this is favorably biased by failed fund deletions.)
- N147 [MUST KNOW] Investor behavior gap: from 1997 to 2011, the average mutual fund investor earned only ONE-HALF the returns earned by the average mutual fund. From 1984-1998, the S&P 500 gained 17.9% annually but the typical stock fund investor gained only 7%. Reason: chasing performance, frequent switching, holding funds less than 3 years.
- N148 [MUST KNOW] Figure 16.4 — percentage of funds outperforming declines with time: 1 year = 35%, 10 years = 20%, 25 years = 10%, 50 years = only 5%. One long-term study found only 13.25% of surviving mutual funds beat the S&P 500.
- N149 [SHOULD KNOW] Quality control insight: any unexpected and unexplained deviation from realistic expectation — whether ABOVE or BELOW — signals a problem. A ship 10 miles west of its objective is just as off-course as one 10 miles east. Don't confuse good luck with skill.
- N150 [SHOULD KNOW] Heisenberg principle applied to investing: the process of measuring performance almost certainly influences the phenomenon being measured. Performance measurement stimulates counterproductive short-term thinking.

## Chapter 17: The Dark Matter of Investing (pp. 135-140)

- N151 [MUST KNOW] Two "dark forces" making active management increasingly difficult: (1) Fees have increased to surprisingly high levels when correctly calculated. (2) The huge influx of skillful professionals with equal access to information has compressed the distribution around the mean — much harder for anyone to break out of the pack.
- N152 [MUST KNOW] Figure 17.1 — active fund "success rates" by category over 10 years (Morningstar data, after fees): Large Blend 16.6%, Large Growth 12.2%, Small Blend 24.7%, Small Growth 23.2%, Emerging Markets 43.2%. In three out of four categories, less than 35% of funds keep up with their benchmark. More than 3 out of 4 actively managed funds are losers in the competition they chose.
- N153 [MUST KNOW] Nearly half of mutual funds didn't even SURVIVE for 10 years. Closure/merger rates for poor performance: 48% of large growth funds, 47% of small growth, 40% of large value, 32% of small value.
- N154 [MUST KNOW] Figure 17.2 — how closed funds declined before death: -1.03% in last 6 months, -2.47% in last 12 months, -3.98% in last 18 months. These "abandoned orphans" and their losses never get reported — they're essential parts of the truth that gets buried.
- N155 [MUST KNOW] No law requires mutual fund organizations to report funds they no longer manage, how many funds have been closed/merged, or the poor performance preceding closure. Poor performers get deleted from databases as though they never existed. Adding failures back flips the conclusion from "active usually works" to "active usually does NOT work."
- N156 [SHOULD KNOW] Consultant deception: when consultants drop a manager from their "recommended" list, they delete all past data. When they add a new manager, they add that manager's favorable prior results retroactively. The resulting chart claims their picks outperformed — but it's not an accurate measure of selection skill.
- N157 [MUST KNOW] Figure 17.3 — percent of mutual funds failing to match their OWN benchmarks (S&P/CRSP data, 2000-2015): the trend over time is for larger and larger percentages to underperform. In the 10 years ended December 2015: 82% of large cap funds, 94% of large cap growth, 61% of large cap value, 88% of small cap funds underperformed their benchmarks.
- N158 [MUST KNOW] Figure 17.4 — survivorship and style consistency over 10 years: of 2,110 domestic funds, only 60% survived, and only 35.9% maintained consistent style. Only about 1 in 3 funds both survived and stayed true to their stated investment approach.
- N159 [SHOULD KNOW] The deception of reporting enhanced performance data — often to the second decimal — may be good for current sales but is hardly professionally responsible. The mutual fund industry needs higher standards for measuring and reporting performance.

## Chapter 18: Predicting the Market — Roughly (pp. 141-145)

- N160 [MUST KNOW] Two big factors dominate market valuation: (1) corporate profits (and dividends), and (2) the P/E ratio at which earnings are capitalized. P/E is determined by interest rates, expected inflation, equity premium, plus a speculative factor reflecting investor optimism/pessimism.
- N161 [MUST KNOW] Historical real returns by era: 1901-1921 = 0.2%, 1929-1949 = 0.4%, 1966-1986 = 1.9%. For 60%+ of the 20th century, the third-best-performing stock market in the world delivered real returns under 2%. And at year-end 1964 AND year-end 1981, the Dow was at 875 — 17 years of ZERO nominal return.
- N162 [MUST KNOW] Anatomy of the 1982-1999 bull market: In 1988, dividends yielded 3.5%, earnings grew 7.1%/year for 11 years. Fundamental return = 10.6%. But total return was a stunning 18.9% — the extra 8.3 percentage points came from the P/E ratio doubling from 12 to 29 (speculative return). Regression to the mean made this unsustainable.
- N163 [SHOULD KNOW] How the 1982-1999 bull was driven by objective forces: corporate profits rose from 3.5% of GDP (below the 4-6% normal range) to almost 6%. Interest rates on long-term government bonds plunged from 14% to 5% — this single change multiplied bond values eightfold (13% compounded annually). Subjective sentiment went from deeply pessimistic (1970s) to very optimistic (late 1990s).
- N164 [SHOULD KNOW] Historical parallels for "this time it's different": canals in Britain (1830s), railroads in Europe and America (1850s), automobiles (1920s), real estate in Japan (1980s), internet stocks (2000s).
- N165 [SHOULD KNOW] Simple framework to estimate future nominal returns: dividend yield (currently ~1.5%) + earnings growth (long-term average ~4.5%) = ~6% fundamental return, before adjusting for P/E changes. Average P/E has been about 15.5.
- N166 [SHOULD KNOW] Benjamin Graham caution: "Long-term investors must be careful not to learn too much from recent experience." Markets always overreact to recent events — both positively and negatively.
- N167 [NICE TO KNOW] Forecasting study: in a review of over 80,000 expert forecasts predicting rates of change in their own fields, you could beat the experts by simply saying: 1/3 of the time change will increase, 1/3 it will decrease, 1/3 no significant change.

## Chapter 19: Individual Investors (pp. 146-157)

- N168 [SHOULD KNOW] Individual investors differ from institutions in key ways: (1) taxes — active managers with 60%+ turnover generate heavy tax obligations; declared performance is before taxes. (2) Mortality — individuals have finite time horizons, unlike endowments.
- N169 [SHOULD KNOW] "My money is me" syndrome: many investors feel their money represents them and the worth of their lives. Particularly common among elderly. If it happens in your family, be tolerant — it's probably another way of expressing fear of death.
- N170 [SHOULD KNOW] Individual investors buy and sell for reasons OUTSIDE the stock market (inheritance, bonus, house purchase, tuition). The amateur's "scoop" is usually already well known and factored into prices by professionals. Market researchers correctly call most individual trading "informationless" trading or "noise."
- N171 [MUST KNOW] The trading ratio has completely reversed: in the 1960s, individual amateurs did 90% of NYSE trading. Now 99% is done by professionals. 75% is done by the top 100 institutions, and 50% by just the top 50. The very largest institutions pay their leading stockbrokers as much as $100 MILLION apiece for the best research.
- N172 [MUST KNOW] Inflation is the most underestimated risk for individual investors, especially retirees. At 2% inflation, purchasing power is cut in half in 36 years. At 5%, in just 14 years (and halved again in the next 14 — to one-quarter). For someone expecting to live to 86, this is serious.
- N173 [SHOULD KNOW] Saving comes before investing — you can only invest what you've saved. Savings has one special characteristic: you can decide to do it and MAKE it happen. Limit spending to last year's income. Always max out tax-sheltered retirement contributions.
- N174 [SHOULD KNOW] Rule of 72: divide 72 by the return rate to get years to double. At 7%, $1 saved becomes $2 in 10 years, $4 in 20, $8 in 30.
- N175 [MUST KNOW] Elderly investors may not live long, but their investments — after being inherited — may have a very long-term mission (children, grandchildren, alma mater). Don't limit the time horizon to the owner's lifetime when the true objectives have a much longer horizon.
- N176 [MUST KNOW] The right answer to "would you prefer stocks go UP or DOWN?" is DOWN — 90% of investors get this wrong. If you're a buyer of shares (as most investors are for years), lower prices mean more shares per dollar invested and greater future dividends as a percentage of investment. You buy cows for milk and hens for eggs — wouldn't you prefer low cow prices?
- N177 [MUST KNOW] Figure 19.2 — power of reinvesting dividends: from 1927 to 1999, a $1 investment grew to $106 from price appreciation alone, but to $2,592 including reinvested dividends. Dividends provided the vast majority of total return.
- N178 [SHOULD KNOW] Senior Wall Street partner's maxim when asked his secret to success: "Don't lose!" Seems simplistic but is very sound. Don't risk serious, irrevocable losses. Don't invest with borrowed money. "There are no old, bold pilots." Benign neglect is the secret to long-term investing success.
- N179 [MUST KNOW] Ellis's 10 Commandments for individual investors: (1) Save. (2) If you must "play the market," limit it to Las Vegas money and keep records. (3) Don't invest for tax reasons. (4) Don't think of home as an investment. (5) Don't do commodities. (6) Remember stockbrokers make money FROM you, not FOR you — a typical broker "talks to" 200 customers with total assets of $5M, needs $300K in commissions (6% of assets) to earn $100K. (7) Don't invest in "new, interesting" products — they're designed to be sold TO investors, not owned BY them (fishing lure analogy). (8) Don't invest in bonds for "safety" — bonds are a poor defense against inflation. (9) Write out long-term goals and estate plan, review investments annually, estate plans each decade. (10) Distrust your feelings — euphoria means bruising ahead, gloom means dawn is near. Activity is almost always in surplus supply; less is better.
- N180 [MUST KNOW] NEVER invest 401(k) money in your own company's stock — your income is already concentrated in that one company. Read about Polaroid and Enron: employees lost retirement savings AND their jobs simultaneously.

## Chapter 20: Selecting Mutual Funds (pp. 158-163)

- N181 [SHOULD KNOW] Stockbrokerage commissions can easily add up to a HIGHER total annual cost (as % of assets) than mutual fund fees. Investing is not a hobby — every major study shows average self-directed individual investor results are poor.
- N182 [MUST KNOW] Cambridge Associates (endowment advisory firm) candidly reports: "There is no sound basis for hiring or firing managers solely on the basis of recent performance." Divide managers into deciles by past results and the data have almost zero predictive power. The one exception: the worst losers do tend to keep losing.
- N183 [SHOULD KNOW] If you must pick active funds: don't try to find THE best fund. Instead find the best fund ORGANIZATION — one with professional culture, low fees, where professionals invest their own money. Look for 30-40 year career retention, consistency in "how we do things here." Organizations named: American Funds (Capital Group), T. Rowe Price, Wellington, Vanguard.
- N184 [MUST KNOW] Never choose a fund you wouldn't confidently "double up" on if it underperformed for 2-3 years. Most long-term successful funds have stretches of subpar performance within their records. Staying the course shows "client prudence."
- N185 [SHOULD KNOW] "Comfortable to live with" is the key to long-term mutual fund success. Change is the investor's enemy: (1) switching costs add up, (2) investors systematically sell after losses and buy after gains — industry money flows confirm this. The self-inflicted pain of performance-chasing is enormous.
- N186 [MUST KNOW] For over 50 years, mutual funds in aggregate lost 180 basis points compounded annually to the S&P 500 — returning 11.8% vs. 13.6%. Over 20 years, index fund returns exceeded 80%+ of all U.S. mutual funds.
- N187 [MUST KNOW] Burt Malkiel's finding: the best estimate of a mutual fund's future performance relative to peers is based on just two factors — portfolio turnover and expenses. In both cases, LESS IS MORE.
- N188 [NICE TO KNOW] Ellis's personal John Neff/Gemini Fund anecdote: in the mid-1970s bear market, he bought Gemini Capital shares (closed-end dual-purpose fund managed by Neff) on margin. He stacked six layers of benefit: Neff's skill + market recovery + value stock outperformance + discount-to-premium shift + dual-fund leverage + margin leverage. The following 10 years were very well rewarded.

## Chapter 21: Phooey on Phees! (pp. 162-169)

- N189 [MUST KNOW] Fee reframing: stated as % of assets, fees look low (~1% for individuals, ~0.5% for institutions). But stated as % of RETURNS (the only thing managers produce), at 7% expected returns those same fees are 14%+ for individuals and 7%+ for institutions. Even this vastly understates the true cost.
- N190 [MUST KNOW] The ultimate fee calculation: since index funds deliver market returns at 0.03-0.10%, the true cost of active management is the INCREMENTAL fee as a percentage of INCREMENTAL risk-adjusted returns above the index. By this correct measure, active management fees are OVER 100% — all the value added (and then some) goes to the manager, leaving nothing for the investor who put up all the money and took all the risk.
- N191 [MUST KNOW] History of fee inflation: 50 years ago, banks managed pension assets for little or no explicit fee (they monetized via reciprocal brokerage commissions). In the 1960s, new boutique firms charged full 1% fees but offset them entirely by recapturing brokerage commissions — real fees were ZERO. When Morgan Bank started charging 0.25%, Wall Street predicted disaster — but clients accepted it and fees only went up from there. Fee schedules tripled or quadrupled over 50 years.
- N192 [SHOULD KNOW] The number of professionals devoted to "price discovery" grew from perhaps 5,000 fifty years ago to 500,000-1,000,000 today. Yet fees kept rising because during the great bull market, investors assumed fees were unimportant relative to their high returns.
- N193 [MUST KNOW] Fees are uniquely invisible: nobody writes a check — they're quietly and automatically deducted by managers, stated as % of assets rather than in dollars. This framing suppresses awareness of their true magnitude.
- N194 [SHOULD KNOW] SEC's Reg FD (Regulation Fair Disclosure) requires all material information be simultaneously available to ALL investors. The "secret sauce" of active management in the last century is now a universal commodity — everyone has almost all of what anyone has. Yet fees remain high.
- N195 [SHOULD KNOW] "Talent congestion" — the worldwide increase in highly trained professionals working for any competitive advantage has been phenomenal. Today's stock market prices are an aggregation of expert estimates from extraordinary numbers of hardworking, experienced professionals. Beating this consensus with high portfolio turnover is deeply challenged.
- N196 [SHOULD KNOW] Few indexers would ever consider switching to active management. Why pay 10x the fees (plus more in taxes) to get more uncertainty, lower long-term returns, PLUS the risk of costly manager disappointment?
- N197 [NICE TO KNOW] Fee comparison across countries: Australia 1.60%, Canada 2.68%, France 1.13%, Germany 1.22%, Switzerland 1.42%, UK 1.32%, US 1.4%. Plus 12b-1 fees of ~25 bps commonly added in the US.
- N198 [SHOULD KNOW] The impact of "only 1 percent" compounded: two investors each start with $100K and add $14K/year for 25 years. One pays 1.10%, the other 0.10%. After 25 years: a $255,423 difference ($1,400,666 vs. $1,145,243). Over a quarter-million dollars lost to a "small" fee difference.

## Chapter 22: Planning Your Play (pp. 170-180)

- N199 [MUST KNOW] Your investing horizon may be far longer than your own life expectancy. If you plan to leave capital to children, grandchildren, or charities, the time horizon extends to THEIR lifetimes. A 40-year-old with a 5-year-old son should plan for 80+ years (the son's life expectancy), not just 45. Even a 75-year-old with young grandchildren has a very long effective horizon.
- N200 [SHOULD KNOW] Don't change your investment strategy just because you've retired. If you could afford fine paintings, you wouldn't change which ones you loved because you turned 70. Same with investments — "The stock doesn't know you own it."
- N201 [MUST KNOW] Figure 22.1 — trade-off between market risk and inflation risk (1926-2006): Nominal returns — T-bills 3.8% (never negative), Bonds 5.2% (negative 9% of years), Stocks 10.5% (negative 30%). But REAL returns — T-bills 0.8% (negative 35%), Bonds 2.1% (negative 38%), Stocks 7.2% (negative 35%). Inflation-adjusted, T-bills and stocks have negative returns about equally often. Real stock returns are 9x T-bill real returns.
- N202 [MUST KNOW] Purchasing power of $1 shrank from $1.00 (1913) to under $0.10 (2011) — Figure 22.2. From 1977-1982, the inflation-adjusted Dow took a 5-year loss of 63%. From the late 1960s to early 1980s, the unweighted stock market adjusted for inflation plunged about 80% — the 1970s were actually WORSE for investors than the 1930s. In 1993, the Dow finally equaled its inflation-adjusted 1929 peak — 64 years to get even.
- N203 [MUST KNOW] $1 million invested in 1964 over 35 years — the four layers of destruction: Nominal value: Stocks $55M, Bonds $15.5M, T-bills $10.7M. After taxes: Stocks $30.2M, Bonds $6.6M, T-bills $4.4M. After inflation: Stocks $5.4M, Bonds $1.2M, T-bills $0.8M. After fund expenses: Stocks $1.8M, Bonds $755K, T-bills $589K. Taxes and inflation are "fiscal pirates" — T-bills ended 20% BEHIND the starting line in real terms.
- N204 [SHOULD KNOW] Retirement income target: 75-80% of preretirement spending plus 2-3% compounded annually to offset inflation. 80% of lifetime healthcare expenditures come in the last 6 months of life. Women live longer and are often younger than husbands — plan especially for the wife's widow years.
- N205 [MUST KNOW] Ideal retirement withdrawal rate: limit to 4% of a three-year moving average of portfolio value. This protects against both inflation and overspending. If you need 5%, put a rolling 5 years of expenditures in medium-term bonds and the rest in equities. Each year, convert one more year from equities to bonds UNLESS the market is high and prospects poor — in that case, convert two years (a mild form of market timing that leans against the wind).
- N206 [SHOULD KNOW] If you draw 6% annually, you WILL deplete your capital after inflation. You owe it to yourself to cut spending to a sustainable level.
- N207 [SHOULD KNOW] Dividends on a portfolio of common stocks have almost never gone down and generally rise slightly more than inflation — making them more reliable income than T-bill interest, which fluctuates substantially year to year.
- N208 [SHOULD KNOW] Five investment planning unknowns to manage simultaneously: (1) rate of return, (2) inflation, (3) spending, (4) taxes, (5) time. Like algebra with five unknowns, each changing.
- N209 [MUST KNOW] "Day away for investing" — pick one day per year (birthday, New Year's, Thanksgiving) to quietly and systematically answer in writing: How much income in retirement? How long will I be retired (ask doctor about "genetic envelope")? What spending rule? How much capital per year of retirement? Healthcare savings needed? Capital for family? Capital for philanthropy? First year takes hours; subsequent years take about one hour updating the prior plan.
- N210 [SHOULD KNOW] High-yield bonds paying 8-10% are partly returning your own CAPITAL — the extra yield compensates for occasional defaults. The income is not all "profit."
- N211 [SHOULD KNOW] Investment counseling is the best bargain available — costs less than $5,000 (paid once per decade), yet most investors pay $10,000+ per million every year in operating expenses while refusing to pay for the more valuable planning service.

# Winning the Loser's Game — Notes Batch 3 (Chapters 23-28, Appendices)

## Chapter 22 (continued from batch 2, pp. 181-182)

- N212 [SHOULD KNOW] Effective federal tax rate on stock returns is under 15%, about half the tax rate on bond/bill income, because stock returns include capital appreciation (taxed at lower cap gains rate and deferred until sale).
- N213 [SHOULD KNOW] In all but one year since 1950, investing in tax-free municipal bonds and holding for 20 years lost money after inflation — even before management/custody expenses. The one favorable year yielded just 0.01% real annual return.
- N214 [MUST KNOW] Social Security benefits increase by 76% (inflation-protected, for life) if you wait to claim at age 70 instead of 62. Waiting 7.5 years is one of the best guaranteed returns available.

## Chapter 23: Disaster Again and Again (pp. 183-190)

- N215 [SHOULD KNOW] Madoff Ponzi scheme: 8,000 investors, $50 billion vaporized. Dec 11, 2008. Claimed "split-strike conversion" strategy using derivatives. Only 11 monthly losses from 1992-2008 — consistency that should have been a red flag.
- N216 [MUST KNOW] If it seems too good to be true and "this time it's different," it IS too good to be true. Madoff's 10% consistent annual return was good enough to attract but not beyond plausibility — the hallmark of effective fraud.
- N217 [SHOULD KNOW] Iceland crisis (Oct 6, 2008): population 320,000, all three banks nationalized. Banks had borrowed $75 billion abroad — many times national GDP, and $250,000 per person. 500,000 British/Continental savers lost $15 billion ($30,000 avg per loser).
- N218 [MUST KNOW] The Madoff and Iceland losses were PERMANENT — money gone forever. Markets, by contrast, recover. The greatest risk for long-term investors is NOT that markets will fall, but that investors will panic-sell at the bottom, turning temporary market losses into permanent capital losses.
- N219 [SHOULD KNOW] The 2008 crisis anatomy: leverage was the central culprit. Easy credit, high property appraisals, low rates, derivatives, SEC authorizing Wall Street dealers to increase debt, ninja loans (no income, no jobs, no assets), inappropriately rated securitized mortgages — all combined into a cascading failure.
- N220 [SHOULD KNOW] In 14 months during 2008, the U.S. stock market lost HALF its value — more than $7 trillion. Yet inflation-adjusted, this was not as severe as the 1970s stock market collapse.
- N221 [MUST KNOW] Risk in its classic and most powerful definition is a function of unacceptable PERMANENT loss. Madoff, Iceland, Lehman were permanent losses. Selling stocks in a panic is also permanent loss. For long-term investors, the worst mistake is "locking the barn door after the animals had run off."

## Chapter 24: Getting Right on 401(k) Plans (pp. 191-200)

- N222 [MUST KNOW] The 401(k) problem is not plan design but IMPLEMENTATION. Workers make mistakes at 8 stages: (1) not participating, (2) not matching the match, (3) not increasing contributions with pay raises (ideally to 12%+), (4) borrowing/withdrawing between jobs, (5) buying high/selling low, (6) retiring too soon, (7) withdrawing too much in retirement, (8) not anticipating late-life healthcare/assisted living costs.
- N223 [MUST KNOW] Retirement age 65 is an artifact of Bismarck's Germany in the 1880s when life expectancy was 45. When Social Security was enacted in 1935, life expectancy at 65 was only 6 years. Today it's 20 years for men, 22 for women. If we kept the same working-to-retirement ratio as 1935, today's retirement age would be 70+.
- N224 [MUST KNOW] Working to age 70 vs retiring at 62 creates a triple benefit: (1) 76% higher Social Security benefits, (2) 8 more years of 401(k) contributions, (3) 8 fewer years of withdrawals. Together these can DOUBLE a 401(k) balance before investment returns. With returns, the balance can TRIPLE to an average ~$360,000, providing $12,000-$15,000/year additional income.
- N225 [SHOULD KNOW] Median 401(k) balance for a 65-year-old is only $120,000. With 4% drawdown minus 2% inflation, that's just $4,800/year. Combined with Social Security, a serious drop from median earned income of ~$63,000.
- N226 [MUST KNOW] Opt-in vs opt-out data (Table 24.1): switching 401(k) defaults from opt-in to opt-out dramatically increases participation. Join plan: 75% → 95% (+20). Match the match: 70% → 95% (+25). Escalate contributions: 30% → 80% (+50). Sensible investing: 30% → 90% (+60).
- N227 [SHOULD KNOW] Tax-deferred investing chart: $5,000/year at 8% over 45 years = $2,087,130 tax-deferred vs $755,512 taxable. Tax deferral nearly triples the outcome.
- N228 [SHOULD KNOW] Don't confuse loyalty to employer with investment wisdom — don't invest 401(k) in your employer's stock. You already have major economic dependency on that company through your job.
- N229 [SHOULD KNOW] Fee differences compound enormously: $100,000 at 6.5% return over 30 years yields ~$500K with 1% fee, but drops sharply at 2% and 3% fees. Low-cost index providers (BlackRock, Northern Trust, State Street, Vanguard) charge <0.1%. Some charge 10x more (0.15% plus 0.35% 12b-1 fee plus other expenses totaling >1%).

## Chapter 25: Endgame (pp. 201-208)

- N230 [SHOULD KNOW] Maximizing lifetime financial success has 5 stages: Earning, Saving, Investing, Contributing, Estate planning. Most investors focus only on stages 2-3 and neglect the endgame.
- N231 [MUST KNOW] "Too much of a good thing can be wunnerful" (Mae West), but a giant inheritance is usually NOT wonderful for children. "Poor little rich kids" are miserable. Warren Buffett: leave "enough money so they would feel they could do anything, but not so much that they could do nothing." Bill Gates: "I don't think it would be good for them."
- N232 [SHOULD KNOW] Tax-free gift: $14,000/person/year ($28,000 for married couples). Over 20 years, $14,000 annually invested at child's lower tax rate can grow to $500,000+; $28,000/year from both parents can produce $1 million+.
- N233 [SHOULD KNOW] Generation-skipping trust: up to $1 million into a tax-free family fund. Assets can grow tax-free for 80-100 years across several generations. At 7% annual after-tax return, $1 million doubles every 10 years — potentially reaching $1 billion in 100 years (before inflation).
- N234 [SHOULD KNOW] Qualified personal residence trust: transfer house to children, live rent-free for set period (e.g. 15 years). Home is valued as taxable gift at only 20-30% of current market value because IRS values the gift as the children's right to take possession at end of trust term.
- N235 [NICE TO KNOW] Charitable trust (20-30 year term): interim annual distributions to charity, then the corpus goes to your beneficiaries. Gift tax only on the discounted present value of the corpus — a fraction of its future market value. Useful if worried about distorting young heirs' values.
- N236 [SHOULD KNOW] It takes more capital to deliver $1 million after taxes as a bequest ($2.2 million) than as a gift ($1.55 million). Estate taxes are assessed on the whole estate before deductions; gift taxes are assessed on the gift amount alone.
- N237 [SHOULD KNOW] Best assets for charitable giving at death: tax-exempt retirement funds (401k, IRA, 403b). These are subject to BOTH estate tax and income tax when distributed to beneficiaries. Both taxes are avoided if donated to charity.
- N238 [NICE TO KNOW] Reframe estate taxes: not a tax on wealth but a tax on CAUTION — your reluctance to make irrevocable decisions about wealth distribution while living. If you make those decisions now, compound interest works for the maximum period.

## Chapter 26: Thoughts for the Wealthy (pp. 209-218)

- N239 [SHOULD KNOW] For $20M+ fortunes (<100,000 Americans): consider organizing an investment committee or hiring a personal expert. Alternative to paying 1-2% annually: engage an advisor once every 5-10 years for a thorough fee-for-time evaluation. Tip: best advisors work at large foundations/endowments and may consult on weekends.
- N240 [MUST KNOW] If you've won the "money game," focus on DEFENSE (lose less) not offense (win more). Be cautious of "asset gatherers" — charming, articulate people who win trust of wealthy people. Check references carefully.
- N241 [SHOULD KNOW] Hedge fund break-even math: if stock market returns 7%, a hedge fund must return 11.25% just to cover the 2-and-20 fee structure. That requires 4.25% alpha — over 60% MORE than the expected market return. Over 10% of hedge funds disappear each year.
- N242 [MUST KNOW] Venture capital: median return of TOP QUARTILE VC funds was 28%, but median of ALL VC funds was less than 5% (below Treasury bonds). The 10 most successful VC organizations captured more total excess returns than the entire rest of the VC industry combined. You can't get into the best funds — they're overbooked.
- N243 [SHOULD KNOW] Best VC investors are NOT passive backers — they actively help build companies. Key to VC success is the entrepreneur who has a compelling need to succeed AND is a skillful risk manager (not a risk-taker). Best VCs know effective entrepreneurs always change their products/plans as they learn what works.
- N244 [SHOULD KNOW] Private equity: on average, PE funds have UNDERperformed market averages after adjusting for their substantial leverage. Investors would be better off with publicly traded stocks and more liquidity.
- N245 [SHOULD KNOW] Commodities are economically inert — no increasing economic value. Trading is zero-sum minus costs. Gold: inflation-adjusted price in early 1980 was over $2,250/oz — a reminder that gold can lose real value for decades.
- N246 [NICE TO KNOW] Maslow's hierarchy extended: beyond self-actualization are "transcendences" — fulfillment from serving others' needs and hopes. Philanthropy can reach this level.
- N247 [SHOULD KNOW] Vonnegut/Heller anecdote: At a billionaire's party, Vonnegut asks Heller how it feels that the host made more in one day than Catch-22 earned in its history. Heller: "I've got something he can never have — the knowledge that I've got enough."
- N248 [NICE TO KNOW] Ellis's personal philosophy: "I prefer to make errors of commission (giving to causes that later disappoint) than to make errors of omission (giving too little or too late)."

## Chapter 27: You Are Now Good to Go! (pp. 219-222)

- N249 [MUST KNOW] The right question is NOT "Can we find brilliant, hardworking managers who would do their very best?" (answer: yes). The right question is "Can we find a manager who can outperform the CONSENSUS of experts by enough to cover fees, costs, and offset risks?" That's far harder.
- N250 [SHOULD KNOW] Two problems with trying to beat the market: (1) it's extraordinarily difficult and easy to do much WORSE, (2) the attempt diverts attention from the truly productive task of establishing long-range objectives and policies matched to your needs.
- N251 [SHOULD KNOW] Three characteristics needed for investment success: (1) genuine interest in understanding your own values and objectives, (2) basic appreciation of capital markets including Mr. Market's tricks and institutional dominance, (3) personal discipline to work out and hold to basic policies over time.

## Chapter 28: Parting Thoughts (pp. 223-224)

- N252 [SHOULD KNOW] Feynman quote: "Genuine doubt is the necessary first step toward creativity." Ellis has double-checked his main arguments with experts and is confident certain structural realities won't change: (1) the number of brilliant investment professionals won't decrease, (2) institutional dominance of transactions won't decline, (3) even if someday most investors index, that day hasn't come yet — focus on asset mix policy now.

## Appendix A: Serving on Investment Committees (pp. 225-232)

- N253 [SHOULD KNOW] Investment committee's primary responsibility is not investment MANAGEMENT but investment GOVERNANCE. Main task: determining appropriate goals for both interim risk and long-term returns, then setting long-term policies.
- N254 [SHOULD KNOW] Committee-manager meetings: the review of operations and reaffirmation of the manager's mission should take just 5 minutes. If it takes longer, "Houston, we have a problem" — either the mission is unclear or results are off-mission.
- N255 [SHOULD KNOW] Performance measurement purpose: not to provide answers but to identify QUESTIONS. Ask "Why?" repeatedly. Soft/qualitative signals of trouble (manager's judgment, consistency of words and actions) surface long before hard quantitative data shows problems.
- N256 [MUST KNOW] Counterintuitive manager decision: when an underperforming manager is "up for review," often the best decision is to ASSIGN MORE MONEY to that manager. A well-chosen active manager is probably underperforming only because their style is temporarily out of favor. Committees too often drop managers they should keep and switch to managers who have just finished their best period.
- N257 [SHOULD KNOW] Ideal meeting: about 5 minutes with no actions taken. Like a well-run continuous-process factory, a well-run investment process should be "boring" — anything "interesting" is a problem.
- N258 [SHOULD KNOW] Committee composition: majority should have substantial investing experience. Optimal average tenure 6-8 years (staggered 5-6 year terms, renewable once or twice). Shorter tenure = members too new to work as a team. Longer = members stop listening carefully.
- N259 [SHOULD KNOW] Spending rule for endowments: two cardinal principles: (1) set at a level sustainable through bull AND bear markets, (2) the difference between return and spending must be enough to absorb inflation. Never let spending wishes determine investment policy.
- N260 [SHOULD KNOW] The biggest hidden advantage of indexing for committees: it frees them from the distraction of manager selection/evaluation and lets them focus on what really matters — asset mix and long-term policy.

## Appendix B: Murder on the Orient Express (pp. 233-240)

- N261 [MUST KNOW] Agatha Christie analogy: In the "murder" of investment performance, no ONE suspect is guilty — ALL are guilty. Active managers, investment consultants, investment committees, and individual investors all contribute to systemic underperformance. None recognizes their own role in the crime.
- N262 [SHOULD KNOW] Underperformance data: over 12 months, ~60% of mutual funds underperform. Over 10 years, >70%. Over 20 years, >80%. After risk adjustment, 24% of institutional funds have significantly negative alpha, 75% have zero alpha, and less than 1% achieve statistically significant positive alpha.
- N263 [MUST KNOW] Goyal and Wahal study (3,000+ institutional funds): managers hired had outperformed by 12.5%, 7.8%, and 4.3% annually over the 3 years BEFORE hiring. After hiring, fired managers actually achieved slightly HIGHER returns than the newly hired managers. The hiring/firing cycle destroys value.
- N264 [SHOULD KNOW] Investment consultants' conflict: their business model incentivizes keeping accounts (90%+ annual profitability per incremental account) over giving truly independent advice. Priority becomes "do not lose an account." They recommend diversifying across multiple managers — which protects the advisor's business but approximates an expensive index fund.
- N265 [SHOULD KNOW] Consultants present only managers with compelling recent records and never defend "disappointing" managers. Evidence shows their clients systematically hire managers AFTER best years and fire AFTER worst years — the opposite of buy low, sell high.
- N266 [SHOULD KNOW] Active management may be "the only service ever offered that costs more than the value delivered." True incremental fees as a percentage of incremental value added are actually over 100% of the value active managers deliver.
