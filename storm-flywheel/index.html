The Storm Flywheel

What holds up, what bounces first, and how to buy when the whole market is on sale at once.

The Sector Flywheel is built for the normal case, when one corner of the market is on sale and the rest carries on. This volume is for the other case: the storm, when everything falls together. In a storm the Flywheel's cheapest-eight ranking stops being useful, because every name is cheap at the same time. The questions change. Which names will fall least, so you can hold them without panic? Which will bounce first and hardest, so they are worth buying on the way down? What actually protected a portfolio in past storms, and what only seemed to? And how do you buy into a falling market without running out of money, or getting a margin call, before the bottom?

Everything here is built from the record. The guide measures every S&P 500 decline of 10% or more since 1950, how twelve storms since 2000 treated each sector and each kind of hedge, and how all 151 names in the Flywheel and the Sector Flywheel behaved in the seven storms since 2018. It closes with a step-by-step storm plan and ten new "storm anchor" stocks, companies that held up unusually well in the recent storms and that none of the other volumes cover. Prices and data run through 25 September 2026.

How Often Storms Come

Storms are not rare events that happen to other generations. Since 1950 the S&P 500 has fallen 10% or more from a high 27 times, about once every two years and ten months. Eleven of those declines went past 20%, the usual definition of a bear market, roughly once every seven years. Five more stopped just short of 20% (1990, 1998, 2011, 2018 and 2025), which is a reminder that the 20% line is a convention, not a law. Anyone who holds stocks for thirty years should expect around ten corrections and four or five bear markets along the way.

Every S&P 500 decline of 10% or more since 1950, placed at the year it bottomed. Bar length is the depth of the fall, measured on daily closing prices.

The more useful pattern is in how long they last. Most storms are short. A typical correction of 10–20% bottoms within a few months and gets back to its old high in another few, and even some deep crashes healed fast: the 1998 Russia and LTCM scare recovered in three months, COVID's 34% crash in five, and the 2025 tariff crash in under three. The long ones are the ones tied to a recession or a bubble. The 1973–74 bear took almost six years to regain its old high, the dot-com bust more than four and a half, and the financial crisis four. That difference, between a storm that passes in months and one that lasts years, is the most important thing to figure out when one starts, and the next section is about how to tell them apart.

Three things stand out in the table. First, the causes rhyme: rising rates and inflation, recessions, bubbles bursting, and outside shocks account for nearly every storm. Second, the fastest falls often recover fastest, because they are driven by panic and forced selling rather than by a collapse in earnings. Third, every single one of these storms ended, and a buyer at the bottom of any of them did well. The hard part was never knowing that storms end. It was having the money, the nerve and the plan to buy while they were happening.

Five Kinds of Storm

Storms look alike on a price chart and are nothing alike underneath. What protects you and what you should buy depends on which kind you are in. Five kinds cover almost every decline since 1950. Many real storms mix two of them, but one usually dominates, and it is worth deciding which one early.

1. The panic

A panic is a sudden shock that forces a lot of selling at once: a crash with no obvious cause (1987), a pandemic (2020), a fund blowup (1998's LTCM, 2018's volatility funds), a currency trade unwinding (August 2024's yen carry trade) or a policy surprise (the April 2025 tariffs). Prices fall faster than the economy can possibly change. On October 19, 1987, the S&P 500 fell 20.5% in one day. In 2020 it fell 34% in 23 trading days, the fastest bear market on record. On April 3 and 4, 2025 it fell almost 11% in two days, then rose 9.5% in a single day on April 9 when the tariffs were paused.

Panics are driven by forced sellers: funds meeting redemptions, traders meeting margin calls, strategies that sell when volatility rises. Once they are done selling, prices snap back, often before the news improves. What holds up is the classic defensive group (staples, utilities, health care) and long Treasuries, which rally as money runs to safety. What to buy is quality companies that fell for no business reason, and the high-beta leaders that fell hardest, since they rebound hardest. Speed is the defining feature on both sides, so a panic plan has to be written in advance: there is no time to think once it starts.

Shock → forced selling (margin calls, redemptions, volatility funds) → everything falls at once, fast → forced selling ends → sharp rebound led by the hardest-hit names.

2. The recession bear

A recession bear is the late-cycle story from the Sector Flywheel's clock playing out in full. Rate hikes bite, orders and hiring fall, earnings drop, and the market prices in a real contraction. These are slower and deeper than panics: 1957, 1969–70, 1973–74 (with an oil shock on top), 1981–82, 1990 and 2007–09. The market usually falls in waves, with sharp rallies in between that fool people into thinking it is over, and it bottoms months before the recession ends.

What holds up: staples and health care (they lose the least), long Treasuries (yields fall as the Fed cuts), and cash. What gets crushed: banks, industrials, materials, consumer discretionary and anything with heavy debt, because defaults rise and credit dries up. In 2008 financials fell 82% while staples fell 29%. The buying rule is patience. Buy slowly in steps as the market falls, lean toward quality and strong balance sheets, and save the most cyclical names for late in the storm, when credit spreads have peaked and the first signs of improvement appear.

Fed overtightens → orders, hiring and earnings fall → defaults rise, credit tightens → slow, deep decline in waves → Fed cuts hard → bottom comes months before the economy turns.

3. The bubble bust

A bubble bust is a valuation collapse in whatever the market overpaid for. The dot-com bust of 2000–02 is the model: the Nasdaq-100 fell 83% from peak to trough and technology stocks as a group fell 82%, while consumer staples finished that same stretch about 1% higher. The broad index fell 48%, but that average hid a split market. Boring, cheap companies did fine; expensive favorites were destroyed and took fifteen years to recover. The Nifty Fifty bust of 1973–74 had a similar flavor.

What holds up is whatever the bubble ignored: value stocks, small companies, defensive sectors and bonds. What to buy is not the fallen bubble leaders, at least not early. A stock that falls 80% first falls 50%, and then halves again. The rule is to own the unloved side of the market going in, and to wait for fallen leaders to prove their earnings can support a lower price before buying them. A bubble bust usually looks like a sector storm at first and only later spreads.

Excitement → extreme valuations in one group → growth disappoints → that group collapses over years while unloved stocks hold up or rise.

4. The rate and inflation shock

This is the storm that breaks the usual hedges. When inflation surges and the Fed raises rates hard, stocks and bonds fall together, because higher rates lower the value of both (the discount-rate math from the Sector Flywheel's machinery section). 2022 is the textbook case: the S&P 500 fell 25%, long Treasuries fell 29%, and a traditional mix of stocks and bonds had one of its worst years in about a century. 1994 (a bond crash with a milder stock decline), 1966, 1969 and the 1970s were earlier versions, and the 2023 decline, when the 10-year yield reached 5%, was a small one.

What holds up: energy and commodities (in 2022 energy stocks rose 45% during the bear), the U.S. dollar, cash in short-term T-bills (which earn the rising rates), and trend-following managed-futures funds, which rode rising yields and a rising dollar to gains of 30–50%. What gets crushed: long-duration assets of every kind, from long bonds to fast-growing tech to REITs and utilities. The buying rule is to watch inflation and the Fed rather than the economy. These storms end when inflation turns down and the market starts to expect the Fed to stop, which is often well before the economy weakens.

Inflation surges → Fed hikes hard → bond yields jump → stocks and bonds fall together → commodities, energy, dollar and cash hold up → the storm ends when inflation peaks.

5. The war and oil shock

Wars and supply shocks hit through energy prices and fear. Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in 1990 doubled oil prices and helped tip the economy into recession; the S&P 500 fell 19.9%. Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 added an energy shock to the inflation storm. And in 2026 the Iran war and the disruption in the Strait of Hormuz took the S&P 500 down 9% from late January to late March, while energy stocks rose 26% and even gold fell. Markets have usually recovered from the military events themselves within weeks or months. The lasting damage, when there is any, comes from the oil shock feeding inflation and recession.

What holds up: energy producers, defense, the dollar, and short-term T-bills. What gets hurt: airlines, cruise lines, chemicals and other oil consumers, and anything exposed to the region. The buying rule is to separate the headline from the economics. If the shock fades (a ceasefire, a reopened shipping lane), the sell-off in oil-sensitive names reverses fast and energy gives back its gains. If oil stays high long enough to push inflation up, it turns into a rate and inflation shock.

War or supply cut → oil spikes → fear selling and an energy rally → either the shock fades (fast recovery) or it feeds inflation (a rate shock) or recession (a recession bear).

Four lessons come out of that table, and they shape everything else in this guide.

Nothing works every time. Consumer staples or utilities were the best sector in nine of the twelve storms, and energy in the other three, all of them rate or war storms. Long Treasuries rose in ten of the twelve and were the best asset of all in the financial crisis and 2011, but lost 29% in 2022 and 15% in 2023. Gold rose in the financial crisis and fell in 2022 and 2026. The dollar is the most consistent, rising in most storms, but it was flat or down in three. Any plan that depends on one hedge working is a plan that will eventually fail.

The kind of storm decides the winner. Staples and utilities win panics and recessions. Energy wins inflation and war storms. Treasuries win everything except storms caused by inflation. Knowing which kind of storm you are in, from the checks in the last section, is worth more than any single hedge.

Defensive doesn't mean safe. Staples fell 29% in the financial crisis and 24% in COVID. Utilities fell 35% in COVID, worse than the S&P 500, because the panic hit everything with debt and a dividend. "Defensive" means "falls less, usually", not "doesn't fall". A plan that assumes staples are cash will be caught short.

The hardest hit bounce hardest. Look at the last column. After the financial crisis, financials (the worst sector on the way down, at −82%) doubled in three months. After COVID, energy (−56% on the way down) rose 69%. After 2025 and 2026, technology (the worst both times) led the rebound by a wide margin. The best-performing names during a storm are rarely the best names to own after it. That is the central tension of storm investing: the names that let you sleep during the fall are not the ones that make the money on the way back up.

The Hedges

A hedge is anything that rises, or at least holds its value, when stocks fall, so that you have money to spend when prices are low. Every hedge has a cost, either in lost return during good years or in the storms where it doesn't work. Long Treasuries (TLT, EDV, ZROZ)

The classic hedge, and a very good one in panics and recessions: when fear spikes and the Fed is expected to cut, investors pile into long government bonds, and their prices jump (TLT rose 35% in 2011 and 25% in the financial crisis). The weakness is inflation. When a storm is caused by rising prices and rising rates, long bonds fall with stocks, as in 2022 (−29%) and 2023 (−15%). The Bond Flywheel covers how to trade them. As a storm hedge, the rule is: own long Treasuries when inflation is tame and falling, and don't count on them when it isn't.

Cash and T-bills (SGOV, BIL)

The most underrated hedge. A fund of 0–3 month Treasury bills never moved more than a fraction of a percent in any of these storms, and when rates are high it pays a real yield while you wait. It doesn't rise in a crash, but that is not its job. Its job is to be there, at full value, when everything else is down 30% and on sale. Every other hedge has to be sold (hopefully at a gain) before the money can be used; cash can be spent immediately. For a buy-the-dip strategy like the Flywheel, a cash reserve is the single most useful storm tool, and the plan later in this guide is built around it.

Gold (GLD, GLDM)

Gold is a hedge against the financial system and against currency debasement more than against stock market falls. It rose 24% in the financial crisis and 6% in 2011, but fell in COVID (when investors sold everything liquid), in 2022 (when real interest rates rose) and in 2026 (when it was coming off a record high in January). It is a good long-term diversifier and a poor storm-timing tool. The Flywheel already holds GLD and the miners, so there is no need to add more for storms.

The dollar and the yen (UUP, FXY)

The U.S. dollar rises in most storms, because global investors and companies scramble for dollars to pay debts and because U.S. Treasuries are the world's safe asset. It was up 18% in 2022, the storm where bonds failed. The Japanese yen used to be the other safe haven, but it fell 22% in 2022 as Japan kept rates at zero while everyone else hiked. Currency funds are a specialist tool; for most investors the dollar's strength shows up indirectly, as foreign stocks falling more than U.S. ones.

Anti-beta (BTAL)

BTAL owns low-volatility U.S. stocks and sells short high-volatility ones in equal dollar amounts, so it makes money when calm, boring stocks beat jumpy ones, which is exactly what happens in most storms. It rose in every storm since it launched in 2011 for which there is data here: 22% in 2015–16, 12% in 2018, 9% in COVID, 17% in 2022 and 18% in 2025. The cost is that it loses money in strong bull markets led by high-flyers, and the cost can be steep: it fell about 20% in 2025 and another 17% in 2026 through September as speculative stocks soared. It also charges about 1.4% a year once shorting costs are included. It is the most reliable storm hedge in this table and a reasonable small sleeve (a few percent of the portfolio) for anyone who wants insurance that doesn't depend on the bond market.

Tail-risk funds (TAIL, CAOS)

These funds hold mostly Treasuries plus a steady supply of put options on the stock market, which pay off when stocks crash. TAIL rose 28% in COVID and 27% in the 2025 tariff crash. The catch is the cost: the puts expire worthless most of the time, so the fund bleeds value in calm years, like paying an insurance premium. It also did poorly in the slow 2022 bear, when there was no single crash for the puts to catch, and it lost about 13% in each of 2022 and 2023. Tail funds are insurance against the fast panic, and they are expensive insurance.

Managed futures (DBMF, KMLM, CTA)

Trend-following funds buy whatever is rising and short whatever is falling, across bonds, currencies, commodities and stock indexes. They need a trend to last several months to make money. In the slow grind of 2022 they were superb (KMLM +48%, DBMF +32%) because rates, the dollar and commodities trended for most of the year. In fast panics like COVID and April 2025 they lost a little, because the crash happened before any trend formed. They are the natural partner to Treasuries: they tend to work in exactly the inflation storms where bonds fail.

Low-volatility funds (USMV, SPLV)

Funds of the least volatile stocks usually fall less than the market, but not always, and not by as much as their name suggests. In COVID USMV fell 33%, almost as much as the S&P 500, because its heavy weights in utilities and real estate were hit by the panic. They are a smoother way to own stocks, not a hedge.

A sensible storm kit

Putting it together, a practical kit for a Flywheel-style portfolio has three layers. The main layer is a cash reserve in T-bills, sized so you can keep buying through a 30–40% decline (the plan section suggests how much). The second layer is the storm anchors in the pool: the names in the scorecard below and the new ones at the end of this guide, which fall less and can be sold to fund cheaper names near the bottom. The third, optional, layer is a small insurance sleeve that doesn't depend on any one kind of storm: some mix of long Treasuries (for panics and recessions) and managed futures or anti-beta (for inflation storms). Insurance that covers every storm doesn't exist; insurance that covers different storms, held together, comes close.

Signals Near the Bottom

Nobody can call the exact bottom of a storm, and trying to is how people end up buying nothing. But the record shows which signals have been worth acting on and which haven't, and it is more useful than most rules of thumb.

How far down: the drawdown ladder

This is the simplest signal and the most reliable. Since 1950, here is what happened after the first close at 10%, 20% and 30% below the S&P 500's record high:

First close below the high byTimes since 195012 months later: medianUp after 12 monthsUp after 3 yearsWorst 3 years later
Any time (all days, for comparison)—+10.6%75% of the time——
10%24+11.4%16 of 2418 of 23−35%
20%11+22.9%8 of 1110 of 11−5%
30%6+17.9%4 of 66 of 6+8%

The pattern is clear. A 10% decline on its own is not much of a signal, because some of those turned into bear markets (2000, 2007). By 20% down, three-year returns have been positive in ten of eleven cases, and the one exception lost only 5%. By 30% down, every case was higher three years later. That is the logic behind buying in steps: each step down has, historically, been a better entry than the one before, but no single step is guaranteed to be the last.

The fear gauge: VIX

The VIX measures how much investors are paying for protection against a fall. It is popular as a bottom signal, but the record says the popular version is weak. The first time VIX closed above 30 in each storm since 1990 (25 cases), the S&P 500 was higher a year later in only 13 of the 24 with a full year of data, with a median gain of 4%, worse than an average day. VIX 30 often comes early in a bear market, as in 2000, late 2007 and early 2022, when the worst was still ahead.

Higher readings are different. The first close above 40 (9 cases since 1990) was followed by a median one-year gain of about 21%, higher in 7 of 9 cases. Above 50, it has happened only three times: October 2008, March 2020 and April 2025. March 2020 came two weeks before the bottom; October 2008 was five months early, though already far below where that storm started; and April 2025 was the exact bottom. The practical rule: VIX above 30 means the storm is real; VIX above 40 means forced selling is peaking, and it is time to be buying.

Other signals worth watching

Credit spreads. When the extra yield on junk bonds over Treasuries stops widening and starts to narrow, the bond market is saying the worst is priced in. Stocks and spreads have tended to turn around the same time, within weeks to a few months of each other. Breadth. When nearly every stock is below its 50-day average (90% or more), selling is usually exhausted. The first day afterwards when 90% of volume is in rising stocks has often marked the start of strong rallies. The Fed. In recession and inflation storms, the turn usually comes when the Fed signals it is done hiking or starts cutting. Bad news that doesn't hurt. The most reliable sign of all is a terrible headline (a bad jobs report, a bank failure, a war escalating) that the market shrugs off. It means the sellers are gone.

The Leverage Problem

The Flywheel matches dividends with borrowed money, one for one. In normal markets that is a modest boost. In a storm, borrowed money changes the math, and it is worth understanding exactly how before one arrives.

A margin loan is secured by everything in the account. The broker requires the account's equity (its value minus the loan) to stay above a minimum share of its value, typically 25–30%, and it can raise that minimum for volatile stocks at any time, which brokers did in 2020 and 2021. If equity falls below the minimum, you get a margin call: add cash or the broker sells for you, usually at the worst moment.

The number that matters is the loan compared with the whole account (loan-to-value). With a 30% maintenance requirement, the account can fall this far before a call. The last column assumes the account falls as much as the S&P 500 did; a concentrated account can fall more.

Loan as % of account valueLeverageFall that triggers a margin callStorms that would have triggered it
10%1.1×about 86%None since 1950
20%1.25×about 71%None since 1950 for a diversified account
33%1.5×about 53%2007–09
50%2×about 29%1970, 1974, 1987, 2000–02, 2007–09, 2020

Two things make this more dangerous than the table suggests. First, individual stocks fall much further than the index. In COVID, the S&P 500 fell 34%, but many of the names in the scorecard fell 45–80%. A leveraged position concentrated in a few high-beta names can hit its limit even when the account as a whole looks safe. Second, stuck trades pile up. A Flywheel trade bought with borrowed money that doesn't work becomes a position you hold through the storm, paying margin interest the whole time, and if several of them stack up, the loan grows while the collateral shrinks.

The rules follow directly. Keep the loan small relative to the whole account: the Buy Borrow Die guide's 20% ceiling is a sensible hard limit, and lower is better going into a late-cycle market. Don't put borrowed money into the names at the bottom of the scorecard, the ones that routinely fall twice as much as the market. Keep enough cash to meet a call without selling. And in a storm, pay down margin before adding new positions: buying with borrowed money at the start of a bear market is how a correction becomes a disaster.

The Storm Plan

A plan only works if it is written before the storm. Once prices are falling 3% a day, nobody thinks clearly, and the default human response is to sell what is falling and freeze. Here is a plan built from everything above, in three stages.

Before: build the kit in calm weather

1. Hold a cash reserve. Keep a reserve in T-bills (SGOV or similar) earmarked for storms, separate from any emergency fund. A useful size is enough to make four purchases of equal size, one at each step of the ladder below. It earns interest while it waits, and it is the only hedge that is always worth exactly what it says. 2. Know your anchors and your rebounders. Use the scorecard to mark which names you'd hold through a storm and which you'd buy near the bottom, and write the list down. 3. Cap the leverage. Keep margin well under 20% of the account in late-cycle markets, and keep it off the most volatile names. 4. Decide on insurance. If you want a hedge sleeve, set it up now: insurance bought during a storm is expensive.

During: buy in steps, in the right order

Buy on a ladder tied to the S&P 500's decline from its high, not to headlines or feelings:

S&P 500 below its highDeployWhat to buy
10%First quarter of the reserveQuality names that fell for no business reason, especially the Flywheel's regular cheapest-eight picks
20%Second quarterStart adding the strongest rebounders from the scorecard; begin selling anchors that held up well to fund them
30%Third quarterLean into the rebounders and the hardest-hit quality cyclicals; this has been a good 3-year entry every time since 1950
40% or VIX above 40Last quarterEverything on the buy list; this is where the forced sellers are done

Adjust the ladder to the kind of storm. In a panic, the steps come fast and the bottom is often near the 20–35% level, so move quickly. In a recession bear, go slowly, space the purchases out in time as well as price, and save the most cyclical names for when credit spreads peak. In a rate and inflation shock, don't buy long bonds as a hedge, and favor energy and short-duration names until inflation turns. In a war shock, buy the oil-sensitive names hit by fear, and sell energy's gains once the shock fades. Throughout, run the "which kind is this?" checks every week or two, because storms can change type.

After: rotate and rebuild

Once the market has recovered half its losses, the storm has usually been over. Rotate the anchors and hedges that held up back into the Flywheel's normal pool, refill the cash reserve from gains, and take stock of which names behaved as the scorecard predicted. Then go back to the Sector Flywheel's normal routine. On the record since 1950, storms of 10% or more come every two to three years on average, so the kit is worth keeping ready at all times.

Storm Anchors: New Names

Ten companies that held up unusually well in the seven storms since 2018 and that none of the other volumes cover. They were found by testing every uncovered S&P 400 company and a list of smaller defensive names, then checking each one for business quality, trustworthiness and 2026 news.

A storm anchor is a stock that falls much less than the market in a storm, so you can hold it calmly and, near the bottom, sell it to buy the names that fell hardest. The best anchors have a business that doesn't depend on the economy, or one that actually does better when times are hard: pawn loans, tax preparation, water, gas heat, insurance that isn't tied to the stock market, and restructuring advice. Both are needs, not wants, which is why the combination has been defensive. Chemed has paid a dividend for more than 220 consecutive quarters and raised it 17% in August 2026.

2026 has brought scrutiny. Earnings recovered strongly and guidance was raised, and the activist fund Barington is pushing for a strategic review that could include spinning off Roto-Rooter. But reports in August and September alleged that VITAS admitted dementia patients to hospice who didn't qualify, which the stock took badly, and the CEO sold shares outside a preset plan.

Shines when: Hospice admissions grow, Roto-Rooter demand holds, and a breakup unlocks value.

Dangers: Hospice fraud allegations and Medicare scrutiny. Medicare rate changes. Governance questions.

Watch: Reports late Feb/Apr/Jul/Oct. VITAS admissions and any regulatory action, Roto-Rooter revenue, and the activist campaign.

How to use it: A defensive business with an activist catalyst and a regulatory cloud. Keep it small until the hospice allegations are resolved one way or the other.
