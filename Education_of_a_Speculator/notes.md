# The Education of a Speculator — Notes
## Victor Niederhoffer (1997)

## The Old Trader and the Yen (Prologue)
[pages 26-35]

- N001 [MUST KNOW] The prologue is written as literary fiction in the voice of a broken-down old yen trader — a fictional composite of Niederhoffer himself. It opens with a Hemingway epigraph from The Old Man and the Sea. The old trader was once rated #1 in his field, Soros called him to trade for his own account, but he got in over his head, bought dollar:yen at 93, it went to 88 in hours and he was "eaten alive." Banks cut his credit, customers left. He has a young apprentice named Lopez, an 18-year-old Mexican student who worked for free to learn from him.

- N002 [MUST KNOW] Central lesson of the prologue: the lonely, ego-driven speculator fighting massive institutional forces (Bank of Japan, U.S. Treasury, central banks) who all want the opposite. Short $300 million against the world. Understands the forces but can't stop fighting. "The risk creates opportunity. Still, I am humble because I have lost many times."

- N003 [SHOULD KNOW] The old trader is philosophical about his edge and disadvantages: he trades through brokers who signal his presence to banks; the banks trade ahead of him. The powerful use Reuters dealing machines, direct lines — he uses brokers and starts behind every trade. "I pay them points to trade, so I always start behind."

- N004 [MUST KNOW] The prologue describes a 52-hour sleepless vigil watching yen. His only hope was Bank Negara (Malaysia's central bank) — a "pirate" that always went against the dollar, had already lost $10 billion. The trader prays for Bank Negara to stampede the market at 7 PM New York time. Key insight: "A man should never give up hope. But it is better to have science. I know what happens to the yen when the soybeans are up and gold is down." — intermarket relationships as edge.

- N005 [SHOULD KNOW] Moon cycles and markets: "The yen is my friend, and there is a full moon out. The trends often change when the moon is full. The moon affects the markets just as it affects women, crops, crime, and the tides." — Niederhoffer genuinely considers astronomical factors in trading.

- N006 [MUST KNOW] The great reversal: The Chairman of the Eminent Persons Council speaks in Japan calling for equilibrium dollar at 80. Dollar drops from 94 to 92 instantly. Trader buys $375 million in seconds using three phones simultaneously — pressing speaker, speaking to multiple brokers at once: "What's your dollar bid and offer for 12.5 yards of yen?" — then "I buy dollars." Bought $375 million against yen in one transaction. Made ~$3 million.

- N007 [MUST KNOW] The lesson of the prologue's ending: He added a "fun" $4 million position after the win, the sharks attacked (Bank of Japan bought dollars), and he lost $1 million. "Why did I do something for fun? I never have fun trading. It is too serious." After he covered, Bank of England, Bundesbank, and Federal Reserve all intervened to buy the dollar. Dollar rose to 98. Had he not covered, he would have lost $40 million — 100% of his capital.

- N008 [SHOULD KNOW] Soros contrast: "Soros is long the dollar. He is always with the force when governments and businesspeople want something to happen." Niederhoffer notes Soros's father taught him survival skills during the Holocaust. Soros is described as always aligning with government forces — the opposite of the old trader.

- N009 [NICE TO KNOW] The old trader's personal deterioration: wife gone to Maine, can't urinate for fear of missing a move, gnats biting through window screens, hungry. Ulcers violently painful. Leaves only after trading to hit racquetball balls on his court.

## Chapter 1: Brighton Beach Training
[pages 37-64]

- N010 [MUST KNOW] Brighton Beach as the "Harvard of his education as a speculator." Born 1943 at the southern edge of Brooklyn. "The games, bargains, music, sex, and fauna taught me to appreciate the earthy and nitty-gritty. That's the proper foundation for buying low and selling high, the trade of a speculator."

- N011 [SHOULD KNOW] Brighton Beach culture: a town of second fiddlers, underdogs. The Brooklyn Dodgers won zero World Series for 52 years, finally won in 1955, then moved to LA two years later. Ebbets Field replaced with a housing project. Niederhoffer bet on the Dodgers in 1951 when they were up 13 games — lost when Bobby Thompson's homer decided the playoff. His first losing bet.

- N012 [SHOULD KNOW] Brighton was once the horse racing capital of the world — three tracks: Sheepshead Bay, Gravesend, Brighton Beach. Well-known speculators (Diamond Jim Brady, Vanderbilts, Belmonts, Leonard Jerome) frequented it. Gambling declared illegal 1910, tracks converted to housing developments. By the Roaring '20s, 30 bathhouses on boardwalk. Immigration cycles created 3-year store vacancy cycles: 100% occupancy to 90% vacancy.

- N013 [MUST KNOW] Grandfather Martin Niederhoffer's cautionary tale: Wiped out in the Depression. Speculated in real estate and stocks during the '20s with 5% equity on his positions. Survived the first 200-point crack (Dow below 200, Nov 1929), then the Dow fell another 75% to 50 in May 1932 — buried him alive. Became a "dead duck" searching for a stake, ended up in a $25/month 400-sq-ft apartment. "With this exposure to changing fortunes ingrained in me by my heredity and environment, I guess it's natural for me to tend to play a defensive game."

- N014 [MUST KNOW] Contra-indicator: Buy on blood, buy on panic. "I try to protect myself from sucker plays by waiting to buy until things look really grim. Blood in the streets isn't enough. Nathan Rothschild said he liked to buy when the cannons are firing, and sell when the trumpets are blowing. That's good enough for him but not for a weak hand like me."

- N015 [MUST KNOW] Taiwan missiles example (March 1996): Chinese lobbed missiles before Taiwan presidential elections. 7.8% one-day decline in HK stocks, 5% drop in Taiwan. Niederhoffer bought Chinese mutual funds immediately. Within a week, Taiwan ROSE whenever missiles were launched. Taiwan was best-performing market in the month after the missiles. Classic panic-buy setup.

- N016 [SHOULD KNOW] Brokerage house as contra-indicator: When Barings collapsed and Nikkei slid toward 15,000 (mid-1995), a magazine ran cover story forecasting Nikkei fair value at 8,000. Niederhoffer knew it was time to buy. "I knew then that it was every man for himself, and I rushed into the fray." Barings' failure marked the Nikkei bottom.

- N017 [SHOULD KNOW] Gerald Loeb's brokerage house theory: Sell stocks when brokerage house profits are at highs; buy when brokerage houses are in the red. Loeb recounts a story of opening a stock trading office on a luxury ocean liner Bremen in October 1929 — just before the crash.

- N018 [MUST KNOW] Niederhoffer TESTED Loeb's theory quantitatively. Collected Merrill Lynch monthly prices from NYSE listing (1972) through 1995. Correlation between Merrill Lynch excess return in a month and S&P return in subsequent months: POSITIVE 0.05 — Merrill doing well predicts S&P doing better, not the reverse. The monthly contra-indicator theory is WRONG.

- N019 [MUST KNOW] BUT: Annual data shows opposite. Merrill Lynch excess return in one year vs S&P change next year: correlation of -0.3. Five years when Merrill Lynch excess return was 30+ percentage points: S&P declined 3 out of 5 subsequent years. Odds of decline after Merrill's outsized year: 3:2 vs 2:15 in other years — odds ratio of 11:1. Annual brokerage house fortunes ARE a negative harbinger. Monthly ≠ annual signal.

- N020 [MUST KNOW] Jesse Livermore ("The Boy Wonder") taught the core philosophy that Wall Street maxims must be TESTED, not accepted. Martin Niederhoffer (grandfather) traded with Livermore at bucket shops on New Street, then they'd go to Waterson & Berlin (Irving Berlin's firm, where Martin was CFO). Key Livermore quotes: "The only thing a man should do when he is wrong is cease to be wrong." "There is a time for all things." "You can beat the market in grains but not the grain market."

- N021 [MUST KNOW] Livermore's fatal flaw: Went bankrupt at least 3 times BEFORE 1929 crash. Put last chips in during early 1930s, wiped out. Spent 10 years trying to scare up a stake, tried to sell a book of insights, failed — penned an 8-page suicide letter at the Sherry-Netherland Hotel and shot himself in the hatcheck room in 1940. Lesson: Even geniuses with timeless wisdom can be destroyed. Track record with full context required.

- N022 [MUST KNOW] Wyatt's three rules (Brooklyn Dodger pitcher Whitlow Wyatt), which Niederhoffer applies to speculation: 1) Never let down for a moment — pitch to every batter as if facing him for the first time. 2) Pitch to every hitter as though trying to keep him from getting a long hit — be careful and watch weakness. 3) No matter how low a batter's average, you cannot afford to show him too much of your Sunday pitch or he'll hit it. Applied: don't take calls, break for lunch, or allow visitors during trading day. No distractions.

- N023 [SHOULD KNOW] Ted Williams trick: When fuss about his August birthday distracted him from batting .400+, he simply changed the date of his birthday to October. Niederhoffer applies this to trader focus — extreme concentration is a competitive advantage.

- N024 [SHOULD KNOW] Squash training under Jack Barnaby, "the greatest coach in the history of racquet sports." Niederhoffer practiced against himself 4 of every 7 days. Would hit one stroke over and over — forehand finesse shots, then backhand, then off back wall. Played 3,500 consecutive days without a break. Kept detailed diaries. This deliberate practice model applies directly to trading research discipline.

- N025 [SHOULD KNOW] Lacoste ("The Crocodile") focus story: At Wimbledon, Lacoste was serving while opponent bowed and 18,000 spectators stood — Queen Mary had just entered. Lacoste "thought only of his serve." Never noticed the Queen. The 20th-century equivalent: Niederhoffer doesn't take calls, sign checks, or shuffle regulatory forms during trading hours.

- N026 [MUST KNOW] Athletic background for traders: Hank Shatkin (veteran Chicago Board of Trade clearing firm owner, employed 100+ floor brokers) believed competitive athletics is the best training for speculators. The exchange floors were "studded with former professional athletes." At least 8 members of CBOT grain pits were former professional baseball or football players.

- N027 [SHOULD KNOW] The Brighton handball/paddle tennis scene: All the players had working-class nicknames — "Bitter Irving," "Bookie," "the Animal," "the Martian," "the Ganef," "the Barber," "the Butcher," "the Milkman," "the Refugee." These were Niederhoffer's early teachers in competitive game theory.

- N028 [MUST KNOW] Father Artie's sportsmanship lesson — "Give the benefit of the doubt to the other side and you'll get the best out of them. You'll feel better also." Niederhoffer applies this to business: give all calls to the other side, don't participate in promotions. The irony: when he told Soros this story about his own honesty, Soros immediately launched an audit of all trades Niederhoffer had done for him. Soros's response (translated from Hungarian): "The more he talks about his honesty the faster I count my silver."

- N029 [MUST KNOW] Soros's skepticism principle: The mere fact that Niederhoffer told a self-serving honesty story triggered Soros's antennae. "When someone starts a sentence with 'Quite honestly...' or 'In all frankness...,' I put my hand firmly on my wallet." Applied: protestations of honesty by counterparties in markets = red flag.

- N030 [SHOULD KNOW] Temper control as a competitive weapon: Niederhoffer had a terrible temper as a kid, threw racquets, grabbed money and ran. Artie's lesson: "In addition to being a disgrace, you just helped your opponent along by losing your temper because he'll play harder now." Niederhoffer later watched countless opponents lose by losing their temper, dissipating their energy. Applied to trading: emotional traders make worse decisions and signal weakness.

- N031 [SHOULD KNOW] Jack Kramer temper story: As a boy, Kramer threw his tennis racquet at a referee. His father walked over — Kramer thought his father was defending him. Instead the father accepted the default ("Game and match, Smith"), broke the racquet over his knee, and banned Kramer from tournaments. Kramer said it was the last time in his career he argued with a referee. Lesson in external discipline creating internal discipline.

- N032 [NICE TO KNOW] Water main break anecdote (April 13, 1992): Niederhoffer holding a short bond position, planning to exit at close. Chicago Board of Trade flooded — trading canceled for the first time ever. When the exchange opened 3 days later, he was down 100% on his equity. "Sometimes you win and sometimes you lose, and sometimes you get rained out." External force majeure events can wipe positions regardless of correctness of the trade.

- N033 [NICE TO KNOW] "Economy of motion" — Artie's bedtime lesson: to be consistently the best at something, you must be economical, using the least amount of energy and motion to achieve maximum effect. The Danish from Sea Breeze Bakery and Breyer's banana ice cream ritual preceded nightly sports discussions.

## Chapter 1 (continued) + Chapter 2: Panics and Hoodoos
[pages 65-102]

- N034 [SHOULD KNOW] Economy of motion as champion's trait: Marty Reisman (ping-pong), Ken Rosewall (tennis), Michael Jordan (basketball), Sam Snead (golf), Sugar Ray Leonard (boxing) — all described with same terms: "effortlessly," "never breaking a sweat," "smooth as silk." Josh Gibson quote by Judy Johnson: "There was no effort at all." Josh Gibson hit like Babe Ruth with zero visible exertion. Artie also studied Emily Dickinson's poetry for economy of expression. Father's lesson: economy of motion = "the least amount of input to accomplish the greatest output."

- N035 [MUST KNOW] Niederhoffer's margin rule in squash, applied to speculation: "I always aim at least six inches above the tin, unlike my adversaries who love to shoot an inch above the tin in a do-or-die effort to win outright or lose." Applied: "I never buy a market up on the day or sell one down on the day." Never play with razor-thin margins; always have enough cushion for error.

- N036 [MUST KNOW] Willie Sutton principle: "Success in any endeavor requires single-minded attention to detail and total concentration." Sutton spent weeks casing banks, dressed as a cop so perfectly he answered citizen complaints and directed traffic while en route to rob the bank. A police captain once stopped him for a loose button and bawled him out — Sutton "felt just awful... not because a police officer had stopped me right across from the bank I was about to rob but because I was being censured by a superior." Applied: prepare like Sutton — total immersion, total concentration.

- N037 [SHOULD KNOW] Three-person partnership bottle collection at Brighton Beach — young Vic, Stevie, and Rene collected soda bottles for the 2-cent deposit on national holidays, earning $15-20 a day ($300 in today's money). Partners planted stolen sodas in Vic's box — police arrived, arrested Vic. Artie's "Judas steer" story: a steer who always led others up the slaughterhouse ramp but "somehow always managed to get lost in the shuffle and slide back to the bottom" before others met their doom. Applied: markets play the "Judas steer" — move a few ticks in one direction to induce wrong positions, then reverse. Also: beware tax-shelter investments where your "friend" backs out after you've committed funds.

- N038 [MUST KNOW] Damon Runyon's maxim (from the dumping anecdote): "Someday, somewhere, a guy is going to come to you and show you a nice brand-new deck of cards on which the seal is never broken, and offer to bet that the jack of spades will jump out and squirt cider in your ear. But, son, do not bet him, for as sure as you do you are going to get an ear full of cider." — If a deal seems absolutely certain, beware; someone has rigged the deck.

## Chapter 2: Panics and Hoodoos
[pages 75-102]

- N039 [MUST KNOW] Scott Joplin's "The Wall Street Rag" (1908) — written with the notation "Panic in Wall Street, Brokers feeling Melancholy." Captures the 1907 Panic, when call loan rates reached 150% per year, bank runs swept the country, cash redeemable at 5% premium, NYSE closed. Dow fell from 96.37 to 53 — a 45% decline. Niederhoffer notes Joplin (through his publishing connection with grandfather Martin) knew: 16 measures in, the music shifts to major key with the notation "Good times have come." Panics are followed by recovery — Joplin encoded this musically.

- N040 [MUST KNOW] Niederhoffer's definition of a panic: Any day when the daily Dow closing price fell by at least 10% from the highest prevailing close during the preceding 30 calendar days. Key findings: Panics peaked at 38 in the 1930s, were 4 in the 1980s, have all but vanished in the 1990s. Statistically: starting from 1940, the Dow was up 1% one month after panics and 3% three months later — buy after panics.

- N041 [MUST KNOW] Post-1987 panic quantification: After an S&P decline of 7.50 points, average move ONE DAY LATER = +1.39 S&P points, vs +0.12 after a random day and +0.88 after a daily RISE of 7.50. The market mean-reverts after large drops more strongly than random. But results compatible with randomness given high variability and market's overall rise since 1987. March 8, 1996 (largest bond decline since 1987): Niederhoffer bought, doubled his position return in one day. Stocks rose 173 Dow points the next day — largest one-day Dow rally ever.

- N042 [MUST KNOW] The old men with canes quote (19th century Wall Street wisdom): "These old veterans of the Street will be seen in Wall Street, hobbling down on their canes to their brokers' offices. Then they always buy good stocks to the extent of their bank balances, which have been permitted to accumulate for just such an emergency... quickly realize [profits], deposit their profits with their bankers... and retire for another season to the quietude of their splendid homes." — accumulate cash during good times, deploy in full during panics.

- N043 [SHOULD KNOW] Counterpoint — panic buying is not always right. Niederhoffer acknowledges that buying after panics in BONDS is NOT profitable — "almost brought me under." The 1931-1932 period had 16 non-overlapping 10% panic declines as the Dow fell from 163 (April 1931) to 43 (July 1932). His father's warning: "They're completely unpredictable — a war, a change in Fed policy, an earthquake, a brokerage house failure, an assassination."

- N044 [MUST KNOW] Soros lost more money selling short than in any other speculative activity. Niederhoffer concurs: "The advice that comes down the pike from authors of stock market doom books — that individual investors should sell short — is a ticket to the poorhouse." Short sellers pay: interest rate + dividend + risk premium. The costs of being short compound against you.

- N045 [MUST KNOW] Blue-chip permanence fallacy: Of the 12 companies in the Dow Jones at the end of the 19th century, ONLY General Electric survived into Niederhoffer's day. The others "passed into oblivion or merged out of existence." 3 out of 4 of grandfather Martin's favorites went bankrupt. Martin also steered Niederhoffer AWAY from Disney, Walmart, Tam Brands, Capital Cities, Merck, Noxell — which appreciated 4,000%-10,000% over the next 30 years. The blue chips of yesterday are the laggards of tomorrow. Never pay too high a premium for current Street favorites.

- N046 [MUST KNOW] "You can never go broke taking a profit" — Martin's favorite saying, and WRONG. He took 10-15% profits consistently to avoid another crash repeat. Good for profits 2 out of 3 times — but those profits were swallowed by losses from holding deteriorating old standbys. Niederhoffer first stock lesson: bought Benguet Mining at 50 cents (cheapest NYSE stock), paid 6 cents commission + 1/16 spread = DOWN 25% before started. Stock went to $1, sold immediately on grandfather's advice. Stock then went to $30. Has repeated this mistake of grabbing small profits at round numbers throughout career.

- N047 [MUST KNOW] Round number phenomenon (quantified): After a commodity or stock breakup through a round number and subsequent pullback, markets tend to be quite bullish. Many players set targets at round numbers — fast operators come in just ahead, ready to take the other side. If price overcomes this resistance and reaches the target, something BIG is about to happen. Markets that break through round numbers have momentum.

- N048 [SHOULD KNOW] Fading published academic trading systems: "Whenever one or the other of two competing approaches [momentum vs. mean reversion] seems to show superior returns, the other approach is a good bet. Fading the published technical analysis of academic systems is always a good horse to ride. Even a professor is shrewd enough to keep the good stuff, if any, private." — Published edges are already arbitraged away.

- N049 [MUST KNOW] The Hoodoo concept — one of Niederhoffer's most important practical ideas. A hoodoo is a former successful trader/investor whose luck has permanently turned. Classic definition from Garet Garrett: "There is about him an air of departed prosperity. He was once a member of the NYSE, or Gould's broker twenty years ago. He is most knowing of speed and would easily fool you if you were not warned. All the past he understands, and the why of everything, but for the present and future he is a source of fatal ideas and a borrower of money." The Rothschilds would never do business with a hoodoo regardless of references — knowing that bad luck often conceals excess of greed, rashness, cowardice, bad temper, or moral turpitude.

- N050 [MUST KNOW] Ivan Boesky as hoodoo example: Jim Lorie (Niederhoffer's mentor, father of his speculative business) arranged a breakfast meeting with Boesky in 1986 at Boesky's office. Boesky's walls were covered with photos of himself at commencement exercises, with political grandees, holding copies of "Merger Mania," in pink Rolls Royces. The phone rang and Niederhoffer overheard "We'll tender at 75..." — immediately recognized a hoodoo feel and quietly exited. Applied to trading: when you see a presentation "available because of an incredible series of unlucky breaks," one partner passes a note: "H-O-O."

- N051 [SHOULD KNOW] Anti-hoodoo self-assessment: Niederhoffer's claim — his business partners become millionaires, 5 former assistants became centimillionaires. Never had a client lose money with him. Won national squash doubles tournament with 3 separate partners. "Financial Trader" dubbed him "The Shoeless Trader."

- N052 [MUST KNOW] Friday the 13th tested scientifically: Martin said never buy stocks or bonds on Friday the 13th. Niederhoffer analyzed 17 years ending Friday, September 13, 1996. Result: Bonds went UP an average of one-third of a point on Friday the 13th. This would occur less than 1 in 1,000 times by chance. For stocks: too variable for reliable estimate (on October 13, 1989, stocks fell 190 Dow points; average for other 21 Friday the 13ths was +5 Dow points). Finding: Friday the 13th is a day to beware — for BOND BEARS, not bears generally.

- N053 [SHOULD KNOW] Richard Whitney cautionary tale: President of the NYSE in the late 1920s/30s, Morgan broker, Harvard Porcellian Club member, foxhunting estate in Far Hills NJ. Embezzled funds from the NYSE's Gratuity Fund (set up for members' widows and orphans) and the New York Yacht Club treasury — all to cover margin calls on worthless Distilled Liquors Corporation stock (he bet big that Prohibition's end would create demand for NJ applejack). Police came to his Penthouse: he insisted on teaching them the difference between "two-quart and six-quart bottles" vs "magnums and jeroboams." Inventoried: 47 suits, 12 walking sticks, 4 pink riding coats. Sentenced to Sing Sing for 5 years. Warning: even pillars of the establishment can become hoodoos.

- N054 [NICE TO KNOW] Wall Street graveyard / river geography: Trinity Church (1846) at Broadway and Wall — its parish still owns prime Wall Street real estate granted by Queen Anne in 1705. Alexander Hamilton's bones lie in its cemetery. Martin described it: "Wall Street has a graveyard at one end and a river at the other." Former rich operators end up begging outside Trinity. Martin's own ex-associate "Paulie" — former top bond broker — ended up rattling coins in a paper cup outside Trinity and then later (30 years on) under a Miami electric stock ticker, still a hoodoo.

## Chapter 3: Delphic Oracles and Science
[pages 104-130]

- N055 [MUST KNOW] The Delphic Oracle as the proto-model for all market forecasters. Key: out of 60 years of classified oracle responses, clear predictions = only 2% of oracles. The secret of the Oracle's success: rarity of predictions + difficulty of falsification. Most responses were commands, warnings, or deliberately ambiguous statements. The Croesus example: "Croesus crossing the Halys will destroy a great empire" — true, but it was HIS OWN empire that fell. When challenged, the Oracle blamed Croesus for failing to ask for clarification.

- N056 [MUST KNOW] Niederhoffer's 10-point guide for running a modern oracle business: 1) Impressive site far from clients (Jackson Hole, Santa Fe, New Haven, Cambridge). 2) Guru pushing age 100 as head. 3) Networking preferred over forecasting. 4) Forecasting should be rare — stick to statements, commands, warnings. 5) If a forecast is mandatory, make it conditional on events unlikely to occur. When cornered, make it ambiguous so you can claim success regardless. 6) Mystical ambiance precludes down-to-earth comparisons. 7) Evaluate your OWN forecasts before anyone else can. 8) When a forecast hits, hold a multimedia press conference. 9) When certain, make startling forecast only if answer is known in advance; otherwise obscure timing so it SEEMS to precede the event. 10) New forecasting technique at each public meeting so clients evaluate method not accuracy.

- N057 [MUST KNOW] Nine characteristics of pseudoscientific market analysis (Niederhoffer's list): 1) Appeal to authority. 2) Absence of counting. 3) Predictions framed untestably. 4) Tautological predictions guaranteed to be true. 5) No allowance for chance variations / sampling variation. 6) Paranoid mien. 7) Disregard of alternative explanations. 8) Self-evaluation of accuracy. 9) Retrofitted systems. He also cites Langmuir's five symptoms of pseudoscience: (1) effect produced by barely-detectable causative agent; (2) effect near limit of detectability; (3) claims of great accuracy; (4) theories contrary to experience; (5) criticisms met by ad hoc excuses.

- N058 [MUST KNOW] The Federal Reserve as modern Delphi: Marble-clad temples, fine food, art, evasive utterances, ascetic chairmen, infinite power, political intrigues, desperate independence protection. Fed refuses to release deliberations; when mole disclosed transcript-keeping, they agreed to release only after 5-year delay. A $1 trillion institution hides behind: "Increase slightly the degree of pressure on reserves." William Greider: "The Federal Reserve was not a sacred temple... yet it inherited all the resonant feelings that surround money... performed the ancient priestly function: the creation of money."

- N059 [MUST KNOW] Superbowl Indicator debunked via proper statistics: After NFL team wins, Dow averaged +259 points in following year; after AFL wins, average = -7.3 Dow points. Seems remarkable, odds against chance: 1 in 300. But: there were 5+ major annual sporting events, 10+ competitors, multiple possible predictors. Simulation shows: probability of finding such a correlation by chance in 5 sporting events = ~50%. Key lesson: When you look at many comparisons, you WILL find spurious correlations. Must apply Bonferroni Adjustment — multiply observed probability by number of comparisons made.

- N060 [MUST KNOW] Random runs in markets: 16 consecutive Friday yen declines in Q3 1995. Probability of 16 consecutive same-direction moves for 50-50 series = 1 in 65,536. But: over 10 years there were 2,500 different opportunities for runs, times 5 major markets, times 2 directions = 25,000 possible instances. The "impossible" probability becomes very likely. Markets that have 10 years of data will always show very long runs by chance alone. The longest run of consecutive days in major markets over 9 years was consistent with randomness.

- N061 [SHOULD KNOW] The spoon-bending dinner story: A publisher hired a spiritualist to pick stocks. Niederhoffer sent "Dr. Bo" Keeley (a hobo-veterinarian who walks backwards and writes in mirror-writing) to investigate. At the dinner, Bo warmed his spoon under the table and bent it himself — became the star of the session. 14 of 15 people were probably paid confederates. Applied: Market gurus and psychics use identical misdirection techniques. Vanderbilt (founder of NY Central Railroad) also relied on spiritualists for stock picks in his later days.

- N062 [MUST KNOW] Martin Gardner's "Fads and Fallacies" — required reading for all Harvard freshmen. Niederhoffer absorbed Gardner's technique for exposing pseudoscience. Key conclusion from the Oxford Companion to the Mind: after 50 years of research, "Not one demonstrable or repeatable, paranormal effect has been discovered, not one characteristic or law has been found which turns up in all these experiments that claim a positive result." The bogus scientist's techniques are "virtually identical to those deployed by the average market guru."

- N063 [SHOULD KNOW] The scientific method applied to markets: Niederhoffer at early age decided to "make my living by applying the scientific method" to an area "that had not advanced beyond the scientific techniques of the dark ages" — technical analysis. His training: collecting data on sporting events, formulating proper odds, betting, revising theories based on outcomes. Karl Popper (mentor to Soros): "A hypothesis is scientific only if it can be falsified. If a hypothesis explains everything and forbids nothing, it is not scientific." Richard Feynman: "If you want to know the essence of scientific method, don't listen to what a scientist may tell you, watch what he does."

- N064 [NICE TO KNOW] Taxonomy of market gurus: The Mystic (Fibonacci), The Unappreciated (bitter/angry), The Other World Person (reads parking lot data), The Mathematician (chaos theory), The Traditionalist (Gann/Livermore), The Washingtonian (ex-government connections), The Correlation Expert (soybeans before bond rallies), The Stand-Alone Artist (maverick), The Insider (Dream Team has a bullet bid in). Photographs and autographs of deceased/failed/incarcerated gurus hang in Niederhoffer's trading room "to provide a certain deflationary ambiance."

## Chapter 4: Losses, Comebacks, Trends, and the Weather
[pages 132-144]

- N065 [MUST KNOW] Rules for losing (inverted rules for winning) from Uncle Howie Eisenberg, "the greatest loser of all time." Howie's classic losses: won the first game consistently, then collapsed in the second and third when victory was in sight. Niederhoffer's 15-rule list of losing techniques, each with a market parallel: Tell your broker how much you like a trade; excoriate regulators (they will investigate you); cut gains early and refuse losses; when behind, chase with everything; enrage opponents/market by revealing your intentions (leave stops at round numbers); invite distractions; take intercourse breaks during trading day; use experimental techniques for crucial trades; officiate/committee-work during a tournament; adopt expensive habits (polo, yachting); stick to your guns when score goes against you; celebrate before the event.

- N066 [MUST KNOW] The Revenge Factor — after a loss, victory becomes more likely. Lacoste: "An unexpected defeat is generally followed by a series of successes; the best means of willing to win is to be beaten from time to time... a defeat causes the well-known reaction: the desire to regain what one has just lost." Capablanca (World Chess Champion 1921-1927): "Nothing is so healthy as a thrashing at the proper time, and from few won games have I learned so much as I have from most of my defeats." Applied: Markets that have made a large down move often have larger mean-reversion potential than markets that are already rising.

- N067 [MUST KNOW] Niederhoffer's own catastrophic losses: 1) Oct 19, 1987: Long bonds, wife walked through trading room noting Dow was already down 300. He "sexulated" (acted on her implicit fear signal) and bought 100 S&P futures. "Before I heard the word Filled, I was down $3 million." SEC chairman then said exchange might close. - N069 [MUST KNOW] Gold/Libya loss (Feb 10, 1983): Short gold (bearish trend for years, gold going down avg $3/oz from Friday close to Monday open for 3 years). Shorted heavily at noon. ## Chapter 4 (continued) + Chapter 5: Winning and Self-Reliance

- N072 [MUST KNOW] Mean reversion in markets after a loss (data): 18 major markets, 1985-1995. The worst performer among all 18 in one year: average change the next year = +20.2% (95% confidence interval: 4.5%-36%). Of 42 years in which a market went down 10%+ — average change in subsequent year = +11.5%. "Rank correlation between consecutive years = -0.28" — highly nonrandom. Bottom pickers are well-served by the data. Crude oil: up $10 total open-to-close after a previous-day decline vs -$9 total after a previous-day rise — the revenge factor is real.

- N073 [MUST KNOW] Trends vs. Reversals — the pivotal question. Contrarians vs. trend followers (MAR data, 1987-1996): Contrarians outperformed in 6 of 9 years, averaging 26% vs 15% for trend followers. But all contrarian superiority came in early years when the universe was smaller. Retrospectively reported results consistently higher than prospective (survivorship bias). The practical conclusion: "prices sometimes move like a wild beast, sometimes like a gentle lamb, and sometimes like a playful trout" — no universal answer, context is everything.

- N074 [MUST KNOW] Penumbra of fluctuation (Frank Taussig, Harvard economist, ~1920s): Prices tend to reverse within certain bands but give way to continuation when bands are broken. These are now called support/resistance levels. Paul H. Cootner and Sidney Alexander later tested this. Alexander's filter rules: small filters on the order of 5% showed significantly profitable results. "Medium filters uniformly yield profits, and the smallest filters yield the highest profits." Cootner synthesized: prices behave as a random walk with "reflecting barriers" — trending within the bands, then reflecting back.

- N075 [MUST KNOW] Niederhoffer's undergraduate Harvard thesis (1963): Positive breakthrough = two consecutive increases in price + two in volume. Prediction: stocks more likely to rise after positive breakthroughs. Result in original 1948-1961 data: 73% chance of a rise next month after positive breakthrough vs 39% after negative. BUT — when tested 30 years later on 1946-1991 data: the theory does NOT hold up. Regression of future price changes on previous price/volume changes explains LESS THAN 1% of variability. "No t-statistics of a univariate or multivariate nature above 1." His own early theory falsified by his own later testing. "How deflating."

- N076 [MUST KNOW] Volcano → wheat price link (quantified): Tested 14 major volcanic eruptions. Average change in wheat prices 8 months after a volcano: +11% — a "highly nonrandom and economically important event." The mechanism: ash clouds block sunlight, damage crops. Historical confirmation: Indonesia's Tambora volcano (1815) created Europe's "Year Without a Summer" in 1816 — wheat prices rose from 9s to 16s 6d per bushel in England. Applied: natural disasters are tradeable macro events, not just news stories.

- N077 [MUST KNOW] The specialist system and why prices cluster at round numbers: Exchange specialists have exclusive licenses to make markets. Limit orders tend to concentrate at multiples of 5 and 2.5 (larger caps) and 1/4 (smaller caps). Prices oscillate between these "magic numbers" until the concentration is broken. When market orders overwhelm the limit orders and break through: "There is likely to be strong underlying demand or supply." This explains the round number phenomenon Niederhoffer discovered empirically at the start.

- N078 [MUST KNOW] "Unchanged" stocks as market predictor: On a typical NYSE trading day, ~25% of 3,100 issues show no change. On the ~10 days per year when the percentage of unchanged stocks falls below 15%: this is highly BEARISH — the expected S&P change over the next 12 months is 10 percentage points BELOW norm. On ~10 days per year when unchanged stocks exceed 30%: BULLISH — S&P goes up 10 percentage points above norm. This would occur by chance fewer than 1 in 5,000 times. Applied: "Never sell a quiet market." Market makers love unchanged days — they profit from both sides of the bid/ask.

- N079 [MUST KNOW] Gold pyramid trade: Niederhoffer turned $40,000 into $22 million by buying gold at $290 and pyramiding (adding to holdings with profits) every time it rose $10. Then applied the Odysseus principle — knowing he would be tempted to hold too long, he gave Susan standing instructions: "Do not listen to my entreaties if I wish to double further. If losses reach 50% of winnings, reduce positions by half. If I beg to be released, sell everything out." When the Hunt brothers' rumored liquidation hit and his wealth dropped ~50%, he called Susan begging her to "untie" him. She followed original orders, sold 75% of his positions. Had she not done so, his wealth would have gone to zero. Final result: kept 5% of the high. "The goddess of losses does everything possible to prevent her worshippers from allowing friends to protect them from her clutches."

- N080 [MUST KNOW] Odysseus rule for speculation: To protect against your own weakness at a market peak, TIE YOURSELF TO THE MAST in advance. Pre-commit to exit rules with someone else who won't be moved by your emotional pleading at the time of decision. The instructions must specify: "If I beg you to release me, you must tighten and add to my bonds." This must be set up BEFORE the temptation arrives, not during it.

- N081 [SHOULD KNOW] Self-confidence as the foundation of speculation: Niederhoffer traced his self-reliance to father Artie's football career at Brooklyn College — team won 1, tied 1, lost 33 games, cumulative score against them 705-8. Despite this, grandfather Martin wrote an angry letter to the coach defending "All-American" Artie. Story of Soros playing tennis on October 19, 1987 while down at least $1 billion from the crash — played his best game ever, completely confident the market would open tomorrow and create opportunities "with the same regularity and predictability as in the past."

- N082 [MUST KNOW] "Niederhoffering" — applying contrarian/asymmetric thinking to get the best value. At Harvard: took graduate-level courses as an undergraduate to get graded on a high curve. Even with the worst grade in graduate class (B+), this pulled his overall ranking to 2nd of 150 economics majors. Later defined by Professor Leontief: "Here you are Niederhoffering the commodity markets by picking up the detritus of Soros's trades." And on the trading floor: buying at the low, selling at the high. "You Niederhoffered them," partners call out.

- N083 [SHOULD KNOW] Simple guesses often work best: "If someone asks the likely price of a stock at year-end, the best answer is 'Unchanged.'" When a commodity has a big move in the beginning of a week, best guess for second half is the opposite. Strategy: sell at-the-money option straddles — "Much more often than you would expect, you'll make money on both sides because expiration will close at exactly the same price you sold it."

- N084 [SHOULD KNOW] M.F.M. Osborne — Niederhoffer's mentor and hero of the efficient markets debate. A high-energy physicist at the U.S. Naval Research Laboratory who applied physics to stock prices. His paper "Brownian Motion in the Stock Market" (1959) is described as "still the seminal and most useful paper ever written in this field." Developed it by watching salmon in a river and noticing the similarity to stock price behavior. "He will someday be recognized as the Pasteur of the field of efficient markets." They collaborated for 15 years.

- N086 [SHOULD KNOW] Contrarian testing strategies from Adam Robinson (co-founder of Princeton Review) — applied to standardized tests and to markets: Questions arranged roughly by difficulty. - N087 [MUST KNOW] "Niederhoffering" the guts (Harvard poker): In 24-hour marathon poker games, he waited the entire length until he was dealt the perfect low hand. By then, opponents were so desperate to get even they would call any big bet. "The urge to get even is one of the most costly habits a speculator can have." The desperate "get-even" trade creates momentum after markets have retraced — they're ready to go much further once the get-even sellers are out.

- N088 [SHOULD KNOW] Deception via "fanny move" (Barnaby's squash tactic): After six shots down the wall in an even exchange, fake a three-wall nick move (twist fanny 2-3 inches) — opponent rushes forward, actual shot goes straight. Applied: "Six days in a row the market opens down and closes down. The seventh day it twists its fanny and goes up. Players buy, thinking it'll keep rising. Instead it ends down. The next time it opens up, players don't buy, but this time the move is real and closes limit up." Markets' deceptive "fanny moves" set up the real move.

- N089 [SHOULD KNOW] Harvard endowment as contrarian signal: As of June 30, 1995, Harvard endowment had $35.3 billion in derivative face value on $7.7 billion net equity — leverage far greater than Soros. Long Sears Roebuck, short J.C. Penney (pairs trading), $1 billion short in stocks. Niederhoffer notes: counterparties buy securities Harvard needs to borrow to "pressure Harvard to deliver them, presumably by jacking up the borrowing rate." Even $7 billion endowments can be front-run.

- N090 [MUST KNOW] "Speculare" — origin of speculation: The word comes from "speculare," men who stood at the back of a Roman ship during sea voyages to find good fishing areas. Joseph (Old Testament) noticed a 7-year cycle of good/bad harvests in ancient Egypt, convinced Pharaoh to buy/tax excess in good years and warehouse for lean years. Became immensely wealthy. Earliest recorded speculative model: buy low during abundance, sell high during scarcity. Cycle awareness is the ancient root of speculation.

- N091 [MUST KNOW] The game of speculation defined: Object = buy low, sell high. Board = organized exchange. Equipment = phone and money. Strategy = balance reward/risk; most players lose, so if you find yourself with the herd, change your plan. Combine cooperation with colleagues + competition with opponents. "Speculation combines chance and skill and includes elements of hunting, deception, cooperation, competition, creativity, rhythm, and physical strength — in short, all the elements of games we played as children."

- N092 [MUST KNOW] Trading from the middle = certain disaster: In "monkey in the middle" you're the public — with the herd, buying what everyone buys, selling what everyone sells. In markets: middlemen (asset allocators, trading managers) take 2-4% per year in fees, leaving the manager little room to outperform. "Trading from the middle is the surest ticket to disaster. You're with the public in the middle, bound to play the losing role." Galton's oxen study: incapability of relying on oneself + faith in others = compels brutes to live in herds. Same in markets.

- N093 [MUST KNOW] India discovery story: At a 1995 dinner with an Indian squash player and derivatives expert, Niederhoffer learned that office rental per square foot in Bombay was 10 times the comparable cost in New York, yet the Indian stock market was valued at only HALF the P/E multiple of U.S. markets. "Something had to give. I bought the Indian stock market with abandon." This is the "gap and bridge" — when two related valuations diverge dramatically, one must converge to the other. Seek valuation dislocations globally.

- N094 [SHOULD KNOW] Chain trading / intermarket connections: Classic chain: weak dollar → strong mark → German bonds and stocks up → pound and lira under pressure → sell their markets → bad for Mexico → sell peso + sell Indian GDRs + sell Telmex calls. Like DNA built from 4 simple units, good chains need few links. "If you add too many separate units to your position, it becomes unwieldy." Exploding links can be lethal. Markets can "go amok" when a Bundesbank announcement breaks the chain.

- N095 [SHOULD KNOW] Hunt brothers silver escape: Was short silver 20x his equity when Hunts started bulling it from $10 toward $45. Closed limit up two consecutive days. Found an escape as small as a "beaver lodge" — bought the spot month up $1, then switched out of shorts by selling spot and buying forward. Within a second, silver went from limit up to limit down (a planted rumor that Hunts were selling). "I have never been as furious with myself." Covered at $11 — had he not, his remains would be "in Boot Hill."

- N096 [SHOULD KNOW] Closed vs. open positions in markets (Soros): Markets are like chess board positions — either closed (prices move in narrow bands, strategic play) or open (prices move rapidly, tactical aggressive play required). Eurodollar market = closed (moves 5 points a week). S&P futures = open ($3,000/day). "One of the things that makes Soros great is that he can move from one to the other, deftly changing strategy. During open positions [like British pound devaluation] — fully leveraged, hastening inevitable adjustment. During closed positions — grinding out a profit each month, patiently playing the closed game."

## Chapter 8 — Gambling the Vig
[pages 247-264]
- N108 [MUST KNOW] The Vig principle: Artie Niederhoffer's reaction when his son told of winning a big paddle-tennis money match — "I don't ever want to see you gamble. All gamblers die broke and most turn into degenerates along the way." Real-life confirmation: every friend who had a gambler in the family responded with a moment of silence, then "Yes, my father was a gambler too, and he died broke." The key conceptual distinction: gambling risk is created by the casino for entertainment; speculating risk is inherent in the real economy (price discovery, capital raising, risk transfer). But emotionally and economically, they share the same machinery.

- N109 [MUST KNOW] Distinction between gambling and speculation: "I invest, you speculate, they gamble." Professional card counter in a casino is closer to investing than buy-and-flip new stock IPOs. The old tale of buying on speculation and holding as an "investment" when the price falls illustrates the infinitesimal difference in practice. All three are first cousins. "Gambling suited me for speculation."

- N110 [SHOULD KNOW] Three gambling downfall stories from Niederhoffer's youth: (1) Tennis instructor Jim Noriley — idolized for his classic Don Budge-style backhand; had taught the two best juniors in the East; Artie drove 2 hours for lessons. Noriley canceled one lesson to pick up a friend at Belmont (horses in the mud), and ultimately lost everything gambling at races, ending as a hand-to-mouth consultant at former students' tennis camps. (2) Count Mazarin who, on his deathbed, lost a coin toss on who would pay for his funeral, one second before dying. (3) The sugar broker on the Coffee, Sugar & Cocoa Exchange who had a strategy of buy below 5 cents, sell above 8 cents — worked many times, until sugar went to 40 cents/lb. He committed suicide. A 30x loss on margin.

- N111 [SHOULD KNOW] Andrew Beyer story: Harvard classmate who had a senior final exam but a "sure thing" running at Belmont. Figured the odds of hitting the winner were better than passing the exam. Went to Belmont. Never regretted it; became one of the most respected turf handicappers in the world, writing for the Washington Post. Illustrates that gambling intuition, when channeled constructively, can be a career.

- N112 [MUST KNOW] Marty Reisman table tennis hustling story: Former World Champion, master hustler. Played at seedy West 50th Street Table Tennis Club. His warmup included: shattering a cigarette at the far corner with his forehand (clocked at 112 mph), playing with the edge of the racquet, playing with his eyeglasses. Niederhoffer started playing lefty to hustle Reisman, then switched to righty. Won with 10-point spot while Reisman sat in a chair. Then bet everything on a "sure thing" — Reisman could score only if he hit a book at the end of the table. Reisman hit the book on all 10 first serves. Niederhoffer bought out for 99% of the bet and ended day up $1. Key trading application: even the world's greatest hustler lost $1+ million to vig. Reisman started with $4,000, ran penny stocks to $1.5M profit, then lost it all — his broker made $1M+ in commissions; bid/ask spread added another 2x that. "The house will grind through a sack of gold" — lesson from Reisman's bookie father, forgotten and relearned.

- N113 [MUST KNOW] The Vig defined and calculated: The house take — the mandatory payment to the dealer — destroys all but the best-capitalized, lowest-frequency traders. NASDAQ bid/asked spread on $10 stock = 2.5%; with 4x annual turnover, this erases the entire 10% secular trend in stocks. Futures bid/asked: silver/soybeans 0.1%, bonds 0.03%. But: casino roulette at 5% house edge for 10,000 bets → winning probability near zero. Options house edge typically 12-50%. The coin-flip calculation: in a fair coin game where broker takes 20 cents per toss, after 200 tosses the chance of ending a winner is ~1 in 100,000. The 1970s-80s round-trip futures cost: $80-100 in commissions + spreads. Key insight: bid/ask + commission + bad execution combine to a staggering load even when individual spreads look small.

- N114 [MUST KNOW] Gambler's ruin mechanics: The speculator bets against an infinitely rich adversary (the house). A speculator with $2,000, putting $1,000 on a $50,000 face value future fluctuating $400/day: daily fluctuation = 20% of bankroll. Three average daily moves against him = 100% margin loss, 50% of bankroll gone. Two forces grind the public: Mr. Vig (80% of ruin cases) and Mr. Leverage (20% of ruin cases). Classic gambler's ruin formula: with 60% win probability, starting with $1 aiming for $10 → 66.1% chance of ruin; expected gain $2.39. Starting with $4 → expected gain $4.17 (sweet spot: capitalizing adequately). With <50% win probability, the losing player should bet MAXIMUM (home run) — only way to win — which is why casinos have bet limits. Bottom line: to survive as speculator, (1) keep transactions low, (2) minimize vig, (3) have adequate capital relative to position size.

- N115 [SHOULD KNOW] Dostoyevski and the psychology of gambling: Freud's take on Dostoyevski's antihero: gambling replaces the "vice" of masturbation. Edmund Bergler extends this: gambling replaces guilt from Oedipal longing for mother; losing becomes self-punishment for early transgressions; the gambler wants to lose. Application: same psychology drives speculative compulsion — the flashing market screen creates the same focused-attention aura as a casino wheel. Compulsive speculator: a friend's brother made 18 consecutive losing trades shorting S&P futures after 1987 crash (down 80%), then 6 more losing trades in puts. When told to take a break, replied: "Something big might happen while I'm waiting. I don't want to be away when it happens." Niederhoffer asked him to take his business elsewhere. They haven't spoken since.

- N116 [SHOULD KNOW] N.M. Moore Jr. / Bo parable: Bo proposes to Junior that they together cheat Cleo. Bo tips Junior to Cleo's hand for 10% off the top. Then Bo secretly starts helping Cleo cheat Junior. After 5-6 rounds, Cleo and Junior each have 50% of their original money — and Bo has 100% of both their starting capital. Illustrates how the middleman/dealer extracts all value through the take, even when the players are trying to cheat each other. Direct application to brokers and dealers in financial markets.

- N117 [SHOULD KNOW] Fowler's 1870 classic on Wall Street danger: "These are the men whose sole business is stock speculation. When they have once entered the street, they never leave it except in a pine box or rosewood case." "Gambling in stocks, after following a legitimate business, is like quaffing brandy after sipping claret." "Men come into Wall Street with fortune, credit, reputation, hope, strength unbruised... they leave it without money, credit, or reputation; with shattered nerves, a blunted sensibility, a conscience seared." Key parallels: random outcomes + uncertainty about opponents' holdings; judicious blending of acceptable risk and return given the chips; importance of calculating odds while judging opponents' psychology; the Iron Law of House Take. ## Chapter 9 — Horse Racing and Market Cycles
[pages 274-284]
- N124 [MUST KNOW] The racetrack as a market education: Niederhoffer goes to the track when too stressed to trade. "Nothing is so good for the inside of a gambling man as the outside rail of a racetrack." Key analogy: favorites at short odds = nifty-fifty growth stocks. Long-shot high-odds nags = value stocks. Both show similar patterns: favorites win more often but payoff is lackluster when they do win; losses are disastrous when they disappoint. Value stocks lose occasionally but losses are small since expectations were already low. The track's 20% take = exchange's bid/ask + commissions + bad execution. "George Soros is the Sultan of Speculation; Sweep is the King of the Card."

- N125 [MUST KNOW] The ebb and flow of horses as markets: "Each market has its own running style: early speed, late closers, 'sitting chilly' off the pace." The trading day is the race; different issues maneuver for position; 15-minute calls give clues how the race will finish. Bonds influence stocks, stocks move currencies, currencies move crude oil — like horses in a race caught in speed duels, boxed in, or fanned wide. "Every day, the conditions change and the fate of each horse is directly affected by the others."

- N126 [MUST KNOW] Bookie — Niederhoffer's racing mentor: Born near Sheepshead Bay Racetrack. Shoveled manure as a kid; eventually given the trainer's bet placements. Found discrepancies between bookmaker odds and pari-mutuel pool (bookmakers allowed fixed odds before tracks monopolized). Could multiply and divide numbers in a flash. Clocked workouts in the morning; hired sentries for paddock body language. Never bet on a horse with an erection in the paddock (makes the horse lag). Found favorable market-maker bookmakers who opened with edge for underdogs/favorites and hit them before odds adjusted — identical to timing buy/sell orders around market opens. Spread bets among 8-10 bookmakers, keeping deposits small so none noticed. Favorite bet: trainer's horse sent out to win after poor recent performance; especially loved when trainer had just reclaimed a horse at higher price than it was claimed away from him (trainers unlikely to pay up for merchandise they intend to lose on). Direct application: insider buying after disappointing earnings.

- N127 [SHOULD KNOW] Bookie's three secrets of success (told during 5-mile swim to Rockaway): (1) Integrity — customers must believe in your fairness or won't return. (2) Guts — courage to take big bets even if can't lay off before the race. (3) Initiative — give customers something exciting; the parlay (bet on 2-3 races, all must win) is attractive: house takes cut from each leg, customer gets big win from small initial investment. And the meta-secret: "Live a good, balanced life. When I make this swim, I always take a partner. Something might happen. I don't drive at 90 mph. If I take bad risks in personal life, I might take too big a risk making the book. Develop the habit of doing the right thing in everyday things — it becomes habitual." Bookie offered customers 10% rebate on all race wagering at weekly settlement — unlike brokerage houses that simultaneously quote 200% profit margins while writing market letters recommending strategies.

- N128 [MUST KNOW] Dutch book concept: Bookie's method — lay off bets, wagering on one side at odds, then betting the other side at better odds, guaranteed profit regardless of which side wins. "That's called a dutch book. When bookies were allowed at the track, before the track monopolized the action, that's how I made all my money. Why take a risk when you don't have to?" This is the essence of arbitrage — the risk-free locked-in profit by simultaneously holding both sides at favorable prices.

- N129 [SHOULD KNOW] Elements of handicapping with market applications: (1) Horse bandages = first-time indicator of stiffness. (2) Bad disposition (fighting jockey, refusing post parade) = market that had a big move with volume drying up — buyers and sellers not happy with current price. (3) Beaten favorite rebetting: Bookie liked betting on horses ridden by top jockeys who performed miserably — professional pride would lead to strong next performance. Same with odds-on favorites that lost: public avoids them, improving odds. But this became such a cliché it's now one of the worst bets (Aqueduct winter 1996 data showed 0.20 win rate vs. 0.12 average — barely better). Market parallel: Steve Kagann buys stocks down 50%+ with highly unfavorable press; gains 15% above market. Buying stocks with huge declines AND insider buying showed highly superior performance in Niederhoffer's 1970s study.


## Chapter 9 — Horse Racing and Market Cycles (continued)
[pages 285-304]
- N130 [MUST KNOW] Bacon's "Secrets of Professional Turf Betting" — Niederhoffer's most referenced book: Mandatory reading for all new employees at his firm. "I consider Bacon's Secrets one of the best books I've ever read. I go back to it for a fresh supply of ideas when I get in a rut." The key principle — "Copper (bet against) the public's ideas at all times" — is the foundation of professional horse betting and by extension, professional speculation. A handful of handicappers at every track grind out consistent profits by doing the opposite of the crowd.

- N131 [MUST KNOW] The Take as the fundamental problem: Track take = 15-25% of all money bet on each race. A 20% return is considered above-average for a mutual fund — yet this is roughly what the racegoer must overcome just to break even. The public — OTB bettors, NASDAQ stock buyers, leveraged commodity traders — all face the same impossible mathematics. "No one doubts that the public must lose at the races in order to pay for prize money, stable care, and upkeep. The racetrack's 20% take pays expenses."

- N132 [MUST KNOW] Switch-itis as the killer of speculators: From Bacon's "Keep Out of Those Switches!" chapter. The amateur: bets to win, horse runs third. Bets to show, horse runs second. Bets second, horse runs third. Bets long shot to show — it wins at $44.80 but pays only $2.40 to show. The amateur lets greed or fear change bet size: plunges on the favorite (loses), bets lightly on fair-priced winner. "He is always one race behind the form of a horse and several races behind the rhythm of results sequences." Niederhoffer's own currency trading confession: sold yen (wrong), sold deutschemark (Fed intervenes), increased size to 200 contracts to catch up, bought Swiss franc (cut profits too early), reduced to 25 contracts (missed the big pound move), reversed bonds (opened against him). Switch-itis epitomized. Key insight: "It's not the losses that do speculators in — it's the switches."

- N133 [MUST KNOW] Principle of Ever-Changing Cycles — the single most important principle of speculation: From Bacon. The public constantly looks for a system or key using past performance. Once found, the public bets it until: (1) the odds on selections get driven down, eliminating the edge; (2) the owners/trainers, seeing their horse being overbet, tell the jockey to ride for exercise, not the win. What once won 2 out of 7 now wins 1 out of 10. Speed figures example: In the 1980s, Beyer speed figures were a profitable edge. When the Daily Racing Form began publishing them to the general public, a 1995 study at New York and California tracks showed betting on the highest Beyer figure produced a 38% net loss. "What once was a valuable profit-making tool is now a money loser." The cycle turns: bettors discard the Beyer number → horses with good figures go back to reasonable odds → the edge returns briefly. Bacon: in a 40-day meeting, at least one radical cycle change; 60 days = at least two.

- N134 [MUST KNOW] Ever-changing cycles applied to markets: Same principle governs everything. Trend followers had a great run → too many imitators → dealers notice and front-run the trigger points → trend-following fills deteriorate. Bob Prechter, Elaine Garzarelli, and Mario Gabelli called the 1987 crash → became prophets → their following drove their fills worse. "When I buy a mutual fund, I always look for the worst performing advisor in the previous period, not the best." For 1996 crown jewel: chose an Indian fund down 60% in 1995 (avg fund up 25%). "More people lost more money after the 1987 crash waiting for a repeat of the 1929 scenario than is pleasant to contemplate." Squash parallel: after playing deep, switch to short shots when opponent lays back; then deep again when opponent moves forward. Jack Barnaby: "You're not playing golf out there."

- N135 [SHOULD KNOW] Discipline — "iron ass" and selectivity: Emmanuel Lasker: "When you see a good move, wait — look for a better one." Track novelist William Murray calls discipline "an iron ass" — the ability to sit there and not bet. Professional handicappers spend 16 hours/day on research but bet only 2-3 races with the greatest profit potential. "You can beat a race but you can't beat the races." The professional's advantage: opportunity to be selective, unlike the market maker who must quote both sides. Imagining a golfer who could erase his worst holes, or a hitter who faced only his best pitchers. "The one advantage the price taker holds over the market maker is the opportunity to be selective."

- N136 [SHOULD KNOW] Emotion and the professional's detachment: Bacon: quiet professionals are inconspicuous among the emotionally charged losers who "jump from one crackpot idea to another betting on hope and fear." Niederhoffer enforces a ban against jocularity and temper tantrums in his office, avoids all phone calls including his wife's during trading. His mentor Irving Redel's trick: always answer "Fair" when asked how he's doing — regardless of whether he just made seven figures or is stuck in a rut. Niederhoffer augments this with "Not as badly as usual" on good days. The "Clock" hire story: Harvard student who subsidized school through racetrack betting, now hired by Niederhoffer, went to Saratoga training — filed a first-person narrative of contrarian horse picking that paid $32.80 on a $2 bet.

- N137 [SHOULD KNOW] Racetrack and markets in the cyberspace era: 1996 visit to track: nearly empty stadium, but $200,000 handle from OTB and simulcast. 90% of handles at tracks like this come from off-track and electronic transfer. Handicapping software, 900 number services, computer charts — "not principally different from 'High Quality Quotes at Lowest Cost' or 'Your Broker Is Now Obsolete' services in Investor's Business Daily." Professional still makes sharp contrast with public: professional has trip notes, trainer/jockey trends, tailored speed figures, past track biases, form cycles for each horse. One handicapper even tracked tidal patterns at tracks near the ocean; a Vegas bookie stays current on weather across the country.


## Chapter 10 — Deception and Charts
[pages 306-324]
- N138 [MUST KNOW] The chapter's core argument — deception is universal and charts are its tool: Niederhoffer was naive enough early in his career to believe technical analysis literature: "I find the use of long-term charts to be paramount... look for repetitive cycles." "Markets go through active and inactive periods, trending and nontrending. Pull out a ruler, draw the trendline." These are direct quotes from six best-selling technical analysis books. He fell "hook, line, and sinker" for this guidance. It took five major lessons to learn that things are seldom what they seem. The markets use deception as a survival mechanism, just like every organism from viruses to humans.

- N139 [SHOULD KNOW] Lesson 1 — Deception in nature (the Brighton Beach walk): Artie's lesson in the wetlands. Chameleon blending into green leaf. Praying mantis looking like a leaf, then grabbing butterflies. False eyes on the mantis to scare birds. Disruptive coloration (the beetle with 100 colors — enemies can't make heads or tails of it). Moths looking like dead twigs. Handball parallel: Red Kravitz wins despite no natural talent because his temper gives opponents nothing to bite into. Hershkowitz playing dead with lobs, then coming up for the kill. Charlie Conerly faking two hand-offs before throwing a touchdown pass. "Every great sports technique is learned from nature. Concealing, decoys, distraction, playing dead, Trojan horses."

- N140 [SHOULD KNOW] Lesson 2 — Chess and the deceptive move: Hypermodern chess shifted from center attacks to "Indian attack" — indirect pressure through bishop moves on the wings. Russian tactic: write moves on scorecard before moving to prevent blunders. Crafty opponents read the written moves for extra time. Super-crafty players deliberately wrote a blunder on the pad, then changed at the last second. Leopold (the "Loophole"), President of the Puzzle Club of America, sent a rare rebus book to a puzzle contest creator, retained the only other copy — always knew answers in advance. World Champion Mikhail Tal: "[My play is] silent till the time comes, apparently without serious threatening intentions. The board is dozing, the opponent is calm — and an imperceptibly prepared explosion proves extremely effective."

- N141 [SHOULD KNOW] Lesson 3 — Deception in handball (the hook serve): Artie Wolf had the greatest hook serve in handball history — half the pace of champions but just as effective. Ball would bounce toward you one second and be eight feet away the next. Key technique: disguise the swing so the motion is identical regardless of direction; hit the ball from in front so opponent can't read hands; then move hand in opposite direction to the spin at the end of the swing. Doubles partners call "in" or "out" to help. Jean Borotra (1925 Davis Cup) feigned death after being hit by a volley — officials and doctors rushed out, opponents took pace off all remaining shots. His team won 10-8. Borotra's career then tainted by serving as Vichy Minister of Sports.

- N142 [MUST KNOW] Lesson 4 — Primate deception and the big brain: Niederhoffer's Japanese stump-tailed macaque Lorie would steal float from his brother Roy only when Niederhoffer left the room, then hide under the bed to avoid being caged. Research by Richard Byrne and Andrew Whiten: young baboon Paul yelled (falsely signaling danger) to scare away female Mel who was digging for a bulb, then stole the bulb when Mel fled. The chimp that closed the metal food box and waited for the second chimp to leave, then reopened it — whereupon the second chimp had been hiding behind a tree and came back to steal the food. Byrne and Whiten's conclusion: "Machiavellian intelligence" — the ability to defeat one ruse with another — is likely the adaptive behavior that favored development of the enormous human brain. The only primate without a single case of trickery: the Lemur family, our smallest-brained cousins.

- N143 [MUST KNOW] Lesson 5 — The visit to John Magee: Niederhoffer visited John Magee (author of "Technical Analysis of Stock Trends," "the definitive work on pattern analysis") in Springfield, Illinois, in March 1964. Office: decaying building, men with green eye shades over drafting tables, windows boarded up to eliminate influence of weather on objectivity, newspapers two weeks old so as not to "becloud the timeless nature of conclusions." Magee believed patterns were real, repeating, and predictable. Niederhoffer's realization: "I was talking to a revered figure — 'the Dean,' 'the Fountainhead,' 'the Seminal Pioneer,' 'the Preeminent Practitioner' — and yet even a novice could see that if moths, monkeys, and men practiced deception as a regular survival technique, speculators should pay more attention to it than to Magee's naive extrapolations." He decided to study deception before losing another dollar speculating.

- N144 [MUST KNOW] Deception as universal adaptive strategy: Niederhoffer's study progressed from viruses to ants to generals: Viruses invade bodies by deception. Ants enslave other ants. Moths evade birds. Generals defeat brilliant generals. Con men profit. Great financiers break suckers they don't even remember the next day. Speculators use deception to shake weak hands out of positions at just the wrong time. Economic theory: Oliver Williamson's transaction cost economics — organizations exist largely to manage opportunism (the use of guile in pursuit of self-interest). James Madison in the Federalist Papers: "As there is a degree of depravity in mankind which requires circumspection and distrust..." Arms race of deception: both predator and prey constantly evolve to counter each other. The earlier the interruption in the predation chain (concealing coloration), the less risk and energy expended.

- N145 [SHOULD KNOW] B.H. Liddell Hart and the indirect approach: "When I came to perceive the superiority of the indirect over the direct approach, I realized that the indirect approach had a much wider application — it was a law of life in all spheres. The direct assault of new ideas provokes a stubborn resistance. Conversion is achieved more easily by unsuspected infiltration of a different idea... In commerce the suggestion that there is a bargain to be secured is far more potent than any direct appeal to buy. And it is proverbial that the surest way of gaining a superior's acceptance of a new idea is to persuade him that it is his idea!" Three historical deception categories: (1) The Decoy — Deborah and Barak (Judges 4:12): lured Sisera's 900 iron chariots into marshy plain below Mount Tabor where they bogged down; 10,000 Israelites then swooped from the mountain. (2) The False Gift — the Trojan Horse. (3) The Holdup.

- N146 [MUST KNOW] The Trojan Horse as a template for market deception (5 techniques): (1) Make the deceptive vessel look attractive (Trojans' greed enticed them); (2) The double agent Sinon — told a million lies about why the horse was left behind, predicted dire consequences if rejected; (3) Reverse psychology — designed the horse so Trojans HAD to destroy part of their defensive wall to accept it (making the Greeks' real intent seem implausible); (4) Fleet concealed at Tenedos, warriors inside stayed silent; (5) The prophet Laocoon who hurled a spear at the horse to expose it as hollow was discredited by the Greek gods sending serpents to eat his sons.

- N147 [MUST KNOW] Playing dead as market strategy: The opossum is actually wide awake and alert while playing dead — anyone trying to carry it by the tail discovers its second line of defense: needlelike teeth. Market application: ambushes cluster around announcement days (employment numbers, PPI). September 9, 1993: bonds dropped 1.5% the day before the PPI release — then rallied 1.5% when the PPI showed the largest-ever recorded decline. Quantitative test: after 1,296 days when bonds showed small one-day changes, average change 5 days later = 1.29%. After 197 days with large changes (1 point+), average change 5 days later = only 1.05%. Volatility was 22% greater after small changes. Louis L'Amour: "When you see excessive volatility, be careful. When you DON'T see excessive volatility, be doubly careful." Bond ambush of May 1995: eight consecutive days within the same handle → bonds rallied six points for one of the most dramatic gains ever.

- N148 [MUST KNOW] Concealing coloration as quantifiable market phenomenon: Test using Swiss franc similarities. Each day, compare current move to the most similar past move. Hypothesis: markets move inversely to the most similar prior move (concealing coloration). Result: correlation between the most similar prior move and the next day's actual move = -0.10. Statistical significance: less than 2 in 100 by chance. Swiss franc is particularly deceptive: of 100 similarity relations tested (most similar x days out of last y days), only ONE showed a positive correlation with subsequent moves. This explains why nearest-neighbor, neural network, and clustering trading techniques consistently underperform.

- N149 [SHOULD KNOW] Disruptive behavior in markets: Formica subintegra ants use chemical warfare to spray "panic" signals into victim nests, throwing them into a rout and carrying off slaves. Market equivalents: October 19-20, 1987 crash — Dow dropped 508 points, then opened at 1856 and dropped to 1723; futures trading halted. The disruptive move scared all bulls out, leaving only the strong hands who then participated in a 250% rise over nine years. April 1993 platinum: dropped 7% in one day, clearing out weak bulls before a sustained rise. Grain markets most prone to disruptive behavior: limit up/down for days at a time after weather events.

- N150 [SHOULD KNOW] Trapping — the false gift: British pound broke its five-year high and went above $2.00 in September 1992 — the buy signal trend followers had waited for. Trap was set. Shorts rushed to cover, new buyers surged in. The pound then declined 60 cents to $1.40 in just four months, costing $40,000 per contract. Japanese yen: opened down more than 50 points on 20 occasions in 1995 → rose 5 points in first hour (luring buyers) → average decline 30 points by end of day. Dealers call Niederhoffer twice daily offering "attractive prices" — invariably as soon as he grabs the bait, a torrent of selling occurs in related markets.

- N151 [SHOULD KNOW] Tempo changes and fencing: Former assistant Jon Normile (U.S. epee champion two years) explained the September 1992 British pound episode as a "tempo fake" — the market delayed its actual move (slow to fast change), causing speculators to either misread or withdraw. December Canadian dollar: straight down trend September 7-29, 1992, but paused/stalled at .8175 for the week of September 13, giving mixed signals. When it resumed its downward course, caught off-balance speculators. Key caveat: Niederhoffer acknowledges this is a descriptive, retrospective framework — markets are rich enough to provide examples for all simple phenomena. The examples have no direct predictive value. Tautology risk from evolutionary framing.

## Chapter 12 — Returns and Randomness: Academic Style
[pages 367-384]
- N158 [SHOULD KNOW] The Chicago years and the random walk battle: Niederhoffer described the Clocker Lawton tout sheet at Suffolk Downs as the catalyst that led him to the University of Chicago — it explicitly mocked Jim Lorie's finding that stocks return 10%/year. He wrote to Lorie arguing that stocks had patterns from "humans' inability to weigh recent versus old events, differences in returns due to volatilities, emotions, liquidating value, and momentum in Fed policies." Lorie urged him to apply "single-minded determination" to uncover anomalies. Note from admissions committee: "Seems like our kind of nut. Reach to get him."

- N159 [MUST KNOW] Jim Lorie and the founding of CRISP: Lorie's Merrill Lynch connection — Louis Engel called in June 1961, asking for research data to support stock investing. Led to the creation of the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRISP), collecting monthly stock price, volume, capitalization, and dividends for every NYSE stock from 1926 to present. Merrill Lynch contributed $200,000. Larry Fisher (Dickensian brilliant-but-absent-minded professor) collected the data. Result of the research: "In all 2,211 year-to-year combinations between 1925 and 1991, only 72 had negative returns. For investments held at least 7 years, only 5 losses. For 10-year periods, 95%+ showed annual returns above 9.1%, 90% above 10.6%, and in half the periods 13.6%+." Jeremy Siegel summary: U.S. stocks have returned 6% above inflation per year for 200 years. Real fixed-income: 5% (1802-1870) but only 0.8% since 1920.

- N160 [MUST KNOW] The RH Hoe short squeeze disaster: Niederhoffer's first client was Bob Gwinn (who became chairman of Sunbeam, grew it from $25M to $1B+ in sales). Niederhoffer pitched a strategy of shorting overvalued stocks. Ideal short: RH Hoe, a Chicago printing equipment maker that hadn't earned money in 5 years, trading at $18 with $15/share in preferred dividend arrears. Gwinn gave him $25,000. Niederhoffer shorted. Stock rose to 20, 30, 40, 50 — he increased his short position. He covered between 90 and 95. Total loss: initial $25,000 + $50,000 more. Together with Gwinn: almost $100,000. One month after covering: RH Hoe declared bankruptcy. Wink Jaffee, when Niederhoffer mentioned "the biggest boom and bust I've ever seen," replied with a chuckle: "Oh yeah, we had some fun squeezing the shorts on that one. Really took some of those midwestern hayseeds to the cleaners." Lesson: always know who you're betting against before putting money on a "sure thing."

- N161 [MUST KNOW] Niederhoffer's attack on the random walk at Chicago's Ph.D. Finance Seminar: He announced he would show markets were "replete with regularities from the most microscopic tick-by-tick data right up to yearly moves within the decade." He criticized random walk proponents for being "too heavy-handed in their testing to uncover structure." When he said "You can't prove a negative," pandemonium broke out — booing and hissing. Professors demanded retraction. He was refused the chance to present his literature review. His statistical conclusions were challenged on "infinite variance" grounds. - N162 [MUST KNOW] Four nonrandom properties of market transactions: From research presented at Chicago (still valid in 1996): (1) General tendency for price reversal between trades. (2) Reversals concentrated at integers where slow participants place limit orders. (3) Fast-moving floor traders, knowing locations of limit orders, take positions at nearby prices, "pocketing the spread." (4) After two price changes in the same direction, probability of a third in the same direction is greater than after two changes in opposite directions. Odds ratios for reversals vs. continuations in transaction data normally run 5-to-1. These findings are now in investment and finance textbooks.
