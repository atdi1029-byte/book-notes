If You Only Remember 10 Things

  1. The Principle of Ever-Changing Cycles is the single most important law of speculation: any edge that becomes known to the market immediately loses its power. Published anomalies are self-defeating. The winning methods are always changing.
  2. Markets are ecological food chains. The public produces losses that feed dealers, who feed hedge funds, who feed decomposers. The role of the public is to be eaten. Understanding your position in this chain is survival itself.
  3. Deception is universal in nature and in markets. Charts, patterns, and technical signals are tools of deception as often as they are tools of insight. If moths, monkeys, and generals practice deception as survival strategy, speculators should expect it.
  4. Never use stop losses. Other traders always know where they are, and prices invariably move to trigger them before reversing in your original direction. Trade with low enough leverage that you can survive adverse moves.
  5. Buy after panics. Post-1940 data shows the Dow rises an average of 1% one month after a panic and 3% three months later. The worst performer among 18 major markets in one year averages +20.2% the next year.
  6. Count everything. Galton’s motto — “Wherever you can, count” — is the foundation of durable speculative edge. Test every received wisdom statistically before risking money.
  7. The vig kills. Bid-ask spreads, commissions, and bad execution compound to a staggering load. In a fair coin game where the broker takes 20 cents per toss, after 200 tosses the chance of ending a winner is 1 in 100,000.
  8. Switch-itis destroys more speculators than losses do. Constantly changing strategies — plunging after a loss, then going timid after a win — ensures you are always one move behind the rhythm.
  9. Tie yourself to the mast before temptation arrives. The Odysseus principle: pre-commit to exit rules with someone who will not be moved by your emotional pleading at the moment of decision.
  10. Artie’s deathbed warning captures the paradox of quantitative speculation: “Are you sure all those technical figures can take account of wars, elections, volcanoes?” The reply — “I’ve got it all under control” — is the hubris that precedes every ruin.

Prologue: The Old Trader and the Yen

The book opens not with autobiography but with fiction — a Hemingway-inflected short story about an old yen trader fighting the entire global financial system. The epigraph comes from The Old Man and the Sea, and the parallels are deliberate. Once rated number one in his field, the old trader has been broken by a single catastrophic position: short $300 million against the dollar at 93, watching in horror as the yen ripped to 88 in a matter of hours. Banks pulled his credit. Customers fled. His apprentice Lopez, an eighteen- year-old Mexican student working for free, is all that remains of his empire.

The trader is a composite of Niederhoffer himself — the sleepless vigils, the ulcers, the inability to leave the screen even to urinate, the desperate hope that some external force will bail him out. His only salvation is Bank Negara, Malaysia’s rogue central bank, which has already lost $10 billion going against the dollar and might stampede the market one more time at 7 PM New York time. The trader watches for the full moon, knowing that trends often change at the lunar cycle. He tracks intermarket signals — soybeans up, gold down — because he has quantified what happens to the yen under those conditions.

“A man should never give up hope. But it is better to have science.” — The Old Trader

The climax arrives when the Chairman of the Eminent Persons Council speaks in Japan calling for an equilibrium dollar at 80. The dollar drops from 94 to 92 instantly. The old trader seizes the moment, buying $375 million in seconds using three phones simultaneously, pressing speaker buttons and shouting to multiple brokers: “What’s your dollar bid and offer for 12.5 yards of yen?” He makes roughly $3 million.

Then hubris strikes. He adds a “fun” $4 million position. The sharks circle — the Bank of Japan buys dollars against him, and he loses $1 million on the add-on. He covers just in time. After he exits, the Bank of England, Bundesbank, and Federal Reserve all intervene to buy the dollar, sending it to 98. Had he not covered, the loss would have been $40 million — 100% of his capital.

“Why did I do something for fun? I never have fun trading. It is too serious.” — The Old Trader

George Soros appears as the counter- figure throughout. Where the old trader fights against governments, Soros is always aligned with them. Where the trader scrapes by with brokers who signal his presence to banks, Soros has direct lines and Reuters dealing machines. The contrast between the two men — one a Brooklyn striver, the other a Budapest survivor who learned from the Holocaust that the only safety lies in swimming with the current of power — establishes the book’s central tension.

This prologue is not merely literary exercise. It introduces every major theme of the 600 pages that follow: the speculator as lonely contrarian, the grinding vig that starts every trade behind, the intermarket connections that provide real edge, the catastrophic danger of adding risk after a win, the necessity of escape routes, and the fundamental question of whether quantified science can truly account for the forces that move markets.

The answer, as the rest of the book makes clear, is both yes and no. Science provides the best available foundation. But science practiced with hubris is merely a more sophisticated form of gambling.

Chapter 1: Brighton Beach Training

Brighton Beach, Brooklyn, is the Harvard of Niederhoffer’s speculative education. Born in 1943 at the southern edge of the borough, he grew up in a neighborhood of second fiddlers and underdogs. The Brooklyn Dodgers won zero World Series for 52 years, finally won in 1955, and moved to Los Angeles two years later. Ebbets Field was bulldozed and replaced with a housing project. Young Victor bet on the Dodgers in 1951 when they were up 13 games — and lost when Bobby Thomson’s homer decided the playoff. His first losing bet.

Brighton was once the horse racing capital of the world, with three tracks: Sheepshead Bay, Gravesend, and Brighton Beach. The great speculators — Diamond Jim Brady, the Vanderbilts, the Belmonts, Leonard Jerome — all frequented it. When gambling was declared illegal in 1910, the tracks were converted to housing developments. By the Roaring Twenties, thirty bathhouses lined the boardwalk. Immigration cycles created three-year store vacancy cycles: 100% occupancy to 90% vacancy. The neighborhood itself was a lesson in cyclicality.

Grandfather Martin Niederhoffer provides the first cautionary tale. He speculated in real estate and stocks during the 1920s with 5% equity on every position. He survived the first 200-point crash in November 1929, but the Dow then fell another 75% to 50 in May 1932 — burying him alive. He spent the rest of his life in a $25-per-month, 400-square-foot apartment, searching for a stake that never came. Martin’s lesson, burned into his grandson’s psyche: never carry leverage that a second crash can destroy.

Father Arthur “Artie” Niederhoffer is the book’s emotional spine. A police captain who moonlighted as a handball champion, musicologist, and quantitative analyst of everything he observed, Artie taught his son through games, through sports commentary at bedtime, and through relentless emphasis on economy of motion. The Danish from Sea Breeze Bakery and Breyer’s banana ice cream preceded nightly discussions of what separated champions from the merely good. The answer was always the same: use the least amount of input to achieve the greatest output. Marty Reisman in ping-pong, Ken Rosewall in tennis, Michael Jordan in basketball, Sugar Ray Leonard in boxing — all described with the same words: “effortlessly,” “never breaking a sweat.”

Niederhoffer’s squash training under Jack Barnaby, “the greatest coach in the history of racquet sports,” became a template for trading discipline. He practiced against himself four of every seven days, hitting one stroke over and over — forehand finesse, then backhand, then off the back wall. He played 3,500 consecutive days without a break and kept detailed diaries of every session. This deliberate-practice model transferred directly to research discipline in markets.

Three figures from sport and crime become speculative role models. René Lacoste, serving at Wimbledon while Queen Mary entered the court, never noticed the Queen — he was thinking only of his serve. Ted Williams, to escape birthday distractions during his .400 chase, simply changed the date of his birthday to October. Willie Sutton, the bank robber, dressed so perfectly as a police officer that he once answered citizen complaints while en route to rob a bank next door. The lesson: total concentration is a competitive advantage in any field.

Brooklyn Dodger pitcher Whitlow Wyatt gave Niederhoffer three rules he applies to every trading day. First: never let down for a moment — pitch to every batter as if facing him for the first time. Second: pitch to every hitter as though trying to prevent a long hit — be careful, watch for weakness. Third: no matter how low a batter’s average, you cannot afford to show him too much of your Sunday pitch or he will hit it. Applied to speculation: do not take calls, break for lunch, or allow visitors during trading hours. No distractions. Niederhoffer also adopted a margin rule from squash: “I always aim at least six inches above the tin, unlike my adversaries who love to shoot an inch above in a do-or-die effort.” Translated: “I never buy a market up on the day or sell one down on the day.” Never play with razor-thin margins.

Hank Shatkin, who owned a Chicago Board of Trade clearing firm employing over 100 floor brokers, believed competitive athletics was the best training for speculators. The exchange floors were studded with former professional athletes — at least eight members of the CBOT grain pits were former professional baseball or football players. The connection between athletic nerve and trading nerve runs throughout the book.

Artie’s most important lesson came in the form of sportsmanship: give the benefit of the doubt to the other side and you’ll get the best out of them. Niederhoffer told Soros this story about his own honesty. Soros immediately launched an audit of every trade Niederhoffer had ever done for him. The response, translated from Hungarian: “The more he talks about his honesty, the faster I count my silver.” Even protestations of virtue are data points in the ecology of deception.

Chapter 2: Panics and Hoodoos

The chapter opens with Scott Joplin’s “The Wall Street Rag” (1908), written with the notation “Panic in Wall Street, Brokers feeling Melancholy.” The piece captured the 1907 Panic, when call loan rates reached 150% per year, bank runs swept the country, and the Dow fell 45% from 96 to 53. Sixteen measures in, the music shifts to major key with the notation “Good times have come.” Joplin encoded the fundamental truth of panic recovery musically. Martin Niederhoffer had taken piano lessons from Joplin himself and gave him the emotional details of Wall Street pain and jubilation that shaped the piece.

Niederhoffer defines a panic as any day when the Dow closing price falls at least 10% from the highest prevailing close during the preceding 30 calendar days. Panics peaked at 38 in the 1930s, dropped to 4 in the 1980s, and had all but vanished by the 1990s. The critical finding: starting from 1940, the Dow was up 1% one month after panics and 3% three months later. After an S&P decline of 7.50 points, the average move one day later was +1.39 points, versus +0.12 after a random day. In March 1996, after the largest bond decline since 1987, Niederhoffer bought aggressively and doubled his position return in one day. Stocks rose 173 Dow points the next day — the largest one-day rally in Dow history at the time. The old men with canes captured the ancient wisdom: “These old veterans of the Street will be seen hobbling down on their canes to their brokers’ offices. They always buy good stocks to the extent of their bank balances, which have been permitted to accumulate for just such an emergency, quickly realize profits, and retire for another season to the quietude of their splendid homes.”

“I try to protect myself from sucker plays by waiting to buy until things look really grim. Blood in the streets isn’t enough. Nathan Rothschild said he liked to buy when the cannons are firing, and sell when the trumpets are blowing. That’s good enough for him but not for a weak hand like me.” — Niederhoffer

The Taiwan missiles of March 1996 provide a live example. When China lobbed missiles before Taiwan’s presidential elections, Hong Kong stocks fell 7.8% in one day and Taiwan dropped 5%. Niederhoffer bought Chinese mutual funds immediately. Within a week, Taiwan rose whenever missiles were launched. Taiwan was the best- performing market in the month after the missiles. Classic panic-buy setup, classic result.

But panic buying is not always correct. Niederhoffer acknowledges that buying after panics in bonds “almost brought me under.” The 1931–1932 period had 16 non-overlapping 10% panic declines as the Dow fell from 163 to 43. Artie’s warning: “They’re completely unpredictable — a war, a change in Fed policy, an earthquake.”

The Hoodoo concept is one of Niederhoffer’s most important practical ideas. A hoodoo is a former successful operator whose luck has permanently turned. Garet Garrett defined the type: “There is about him an air of departed prosperity. He was once a member of the NYSE, or Gould’s broker twenty years ago. All the past he understands, but for the present and future he is a source of fatal ideas and a borrower of money.” The Rothschilds would never do business with a hoodoo regardless of references, knowing that bad luck often conceals excess greed, rashness, or moral turpitude.

Ivan Boesky as hoodoo example: Jim Lorie arranged a breakfast meeting in 1986 at Boesky’s office. The walls were covered with photos of Boesky at commencement exercises, with political grandees, in pink Rolls Royces. The phone rang and Niederhoffer overheard “We’ll tender at 75...” He recognized the hoodoo feel and quietly exited. In his own firm, when a presentation arrives “available because of an incredible series of unlucky breaks,” a partner passes a note across the table: “H-O-O.”

Richard Whitney, president of the NYSE in the late 1920s, provides the ultimate cautionary tale. Morgan’s broker, Harvard Porcellian Club member, foxhunting estate in Far Hills — Whitney embezzled funds from the NYSE’s Gratuity Fund (set up for members’ widows and orphans) and the New York Yacht Club treasury, all to cover margin calls on worthless Distilled Liquors Corporation stock. When police arrived at his penthouse, he insisted on teaching them the difference between “magnums and jeroboams.” Inventoried: 47 suits, 12 walking sticks, 4 pink riding coats. Sentenced to Sing Sing for five years. Even pillars of the establishment can become hoodoos.

Gerald Loeb’s brokerage-house theory — sell stocks when brokerage profits peak, buy when they are in the red — sounds compelling. Niederhoffer tested it. Monthly data: the correlation between Merrill Lynch’s excess return in a month and the S&P return in subsequent months was positive 0.05 — Merrill doing well predicted the S&P doing better, not worse. The monthly contra-indicator theory is wrong. But annual data told the opposite story: Merrill Lynch’s excess return in one year versus S&P change the next showed a correlation of -0.3. In the five years when Merrill’s excess return exceeded 30 percentage points, the S&P declined in three of five subsequent years — an odds ratio of 11 to 1. Annual brokerage fortunes are a negative harbinger. Monthly is not.

The blue-chip permanence fallacy strikes at the heart of buy-and-hold orthodoxy. Of the twelve companies in the Dow Jones at the end of the 19th century, only General Electric survived into Niederhoffer’s day. Three out of four of grandfather Martin’s favorites went bankrupt. Worse, Martin steered Niederhoffer away from Disney, Walmart, Tam Brands, Capital Cities, Merck, and Noxell — which appreciated 4,000% to 10,000% over the next thirty years. Martin’s favorite saying, “You can never go broke taking a profit,” was also wrong. He took 10–15% profits consistently to avoid another crash repeat — good for profits two out of three times, but those profits were swallowed by losses from holding deteriorating old standbys. Niederhoffer’s first stock was Benguet Mining at 50 cents (the cheapest NYSE stock). He paid 6 cents commission plus 1/16 spread — down 25% before it moved. The stock went to $1, and he sold on grandfather’s advice. It then went to $30.

The round number phenomenon: after a commodity or stock breaks through a round number and pulls back, markets tend to be quite bullish. Limit orders cluster at multiples of 5 and 2.5 for large caps. When market orders overwhelm those clusters and break through, something significant is driving the move. Markets that breach round numbers have momentum.

The chapter also demolishes a favorite market myth. Niederhoffer tested Friday the 13th across 17 years: bonds actually went up an average of one-third of a point on Friday the 13th, a result that would occur less than 1 in 1,000 times by chance. Friday the 13th is a day to beware — for bond bears, not bears generally.

Chapter 3: Delphic Oracles and Science

The Delphic Oracle is the proto-model for all market forecasters. Out of 60 years of classified oracle responses, clear predictions constituted only 2%. The Oracle’s success rested on rarity of predictions combined with impossibility of falsification. Most responses were commands, warnings, or deliberately ambiguous statements. When Croesus asked whether to attack Persia, the Oracle replied: “Crossing the Halys, Croesus will destroy a great empire.” True — but it was his own empire that fell. When challenged, the Oracle blamed Croesus for failing to ask for clarification.

Niederhoffer translates this into a ten-point guide for running a modern oracle business. Set up shop far from clients — Jackson Hole, Santa Fe, New Haven. Install a guru pushing age 100 as head. Forecast rarely. When forced to predict, make it conditional on events unlikely to occur. Mystical ambiance precludes mundane comparisons. Evaluate your own forecasts before anyone else can. When certain, make a startling prediction only if the answer is already known; otherwise obscure the timing so it seems to precede the event.

The Federal Reserve is the modern Delphi. Marble-clad temples, fine food, evasive utterances, ascetic chairmen, infinite power. When a mole disclosed that the Fed kept transcripts, they agreed to release them only after a five-year delay. William Greider wrote that the Fed “performed the ancient priestly function: the creation of money.”

Nine characteristics define pseudoscientific market analysis: appeal to authority, absence of counting, untestable predictions, tautological framing, no allowance for sampling variation, paranoid tone, disregard of alternative explanations, self-evaluation of accuracy, and retrofitted systems. This checklist applies to roughly 90% of published market commentary.

The Superbowl Indicator demonstrates proper statistical thinking. After NFL team wins, the Dow averaged +259 points; after AFL wins, the average was -7.3. Odds against chance: 1 in 300. But with five major annual sporting events, ten competitors, and multiple possible predictors, the probability of finding such a correlation by chance rises to roughly 50%. The Bonferroni Adjustment — multiplying observed probability by the number of comparisons made — is essential. Without it, any dataset long enough will produce “impossible” results.

Random runs prove the point. Sixteen consecutive Friday yen declines in Q3 1995 had a probability of 1 in 65,536 for a coin-flip series. But over ten years with 2,500 different opportunities across five major markets in two directions, that “impossible” event becomes almost expected. Markets always show very long runs by chance alone.

Karl Popper provides the antidote: “A hypothesis is scientific only if it can be falsified.” Richard Feynman adds: “If you want to know the essence of scientific method, don’t listen to what a scientist may tell you. Watch what he does.” Martin Gardner’s Fads and Fallacies, required reading for all Harvard freshmen, showed Niederhoffer that after 50 years of research, not one demonstrable or repeatable paranormal effect had been discovered. The techniques of the bogus scientist are virtually identical to those of the average market guru.

A taxonomy of market gurus rounds out the chapter: the Mystic (Fibonacci), the Unappreciated (bitter), the Other World Person (reads parking lot data), the Mathematician (chaos theory), the Traditionalist (Gann), the Washingtonian (ex-government connections), the Correlation Expert, the Stand-Alone Artist, and the Insider. Photographs and autographs of deceased, failed, or incarcerated gurus hang in Niederhoffer’s trading room “to provide a certain deflationary ambiance.”

Chapter 4: Losses, Comebacks, Trends, and the Weather

Uncle Howie Eisenberg, “the greatest loser of all time,” inspires a fifteen-rule list of losing techniques, each with a market parallel. Tell your broker how much you like a trade. Cut gains early and refuse losses. When behind, chase with everything. Reveal your intentions by leaving stops at round numbers. Invite distractions. Take intercourse breaks during the trading day. Use experimental techniques for crucial trades. Celebrate before the event.

The Revenge Factor is the chapter’s core principle. Lacoste: “An unexpected defeat is generally followed by a series of successes; the best means of willing to win is to be beaten from time to time.” Capablanca: “Nothing is so healthy as a thrashing at the proper time.” Markets that have made a large down move often have greater mean- reversion potential than markets already rising. Data across 18 major markets from 1985 to 1995: the worst performer in one year averaged +20.2% the next year. In 42 years when a market declined 10% or more, the average subsequent-year change was +11.5%.

Three personal catastrophes demonstrate the cost of speculation. October 19, 1987: Niederhoffer was long bonds and his wife walked through the trading room mentioning the Dow was already down 300 points. He “sexulated” — acted on her implicit fear signal — and bought 100 S&P futures. Before he heard the word “Filled,” he was down $3 million. The SEC chairman then said the exchange might close. He bought another 50 S&Ps. The S&P ended down 80.75 points — $40,400 per contract. He took what would have been his best day ever and turned it into a nightmare.

The 1991 Gulf War loss: long bonds and S&Ps, short crude, each position up 50% on margin. Baker’s meeting with Aziz lasted eight hours. Partners said close positions. “Oh, no, they must be dotting the i’s.” At 2:30 PM Baker said “Regrettably...” — within two minutes, the Dow was down 80, bonds down 1.5, oil up $3. One word produced a $5 million swing in equity.

The gold/Libya loss of February 1983 teaches a different lesson. Niederhoffer was short gold, riding a bearish trend that had averaged $3/oz declines from Friday close to Monday open for three years. Gold jumped $7 on “technical buying” that Friday afternoon. That weekend at 4 AM Sunday, the US Navy shot down a Libyan jet. It looked like bad luck — but the Pentagon had been buzzing the area for weeks, and “the word was put out.” The gold market had anticipated the move. Information that seems fresh is often as smelly as a dead fish.

The chapter also presents a devastating critique of stop losses. Niederhoffer had a bond position at 108-14 with a stop at 107-30. A bullish employment number was released. But a large hedge fund had a typo: an order to sell 1,000 contracts “at even” was interpreted as 108 even instead of 109 even. Those contracts drove the price to 107.27, stopping him out. Price then moved immediately to 110. The stop turned a 150% profit into a 25% loss.

“Other traders always know. Invariably, the price moves to the level of the stop. The order is executed at a loss, and then the market turns around and moves wildly in your original direction.” — Niederhoffer

Frank Taussig’s “penumbra of fluctuation” provides the framework for thinking about trends versus reversals: prices tend to reverse within certain bands but give way to continuation when bands are broken. Contrarians outperformed trend followers in six of nine years from 1987 to 1996, averaging 26% versus 15% — but the practical conclusion is that prices “sometimes move like a wild beast, sometimes like a gentle lamb, and sometimes like a playful trout.” Context is everything.

Chapter 5: Winning and Self-Reliance

Niederhoffer’s undergraduate Harvard thesis (1963) tested “positive breakthroughs” — two consecutive increases in price plus two in volume — as predictors of future price rises. In the original 1948–1961 data, stocks showed a 73% chance of rising next month after positive breakthroughs versus 39% after negative ones. But when tested thirty years later on 1946–1991 data, the theory collapsed. Regression explained less than 1% of variability, with no t-statistics above 1. His own early theory, falsified by his own later testing. “How deflating.”

The chapter redeems itself with findings that did hold up. Volcanic eruptions and wheat: 14 major eruptions tested, average wheat price change eight months after a volcano was +11% — highly nonrandom. The mechanism: ash clouds block sunlight, damage crops. Indonesia’s Tambora volcano in 1815 created the “Year Without a Summer” in 1816, with English wheat prices doubling. Natural disasters are tradeable macro events.

“Unchanged” stocks as market predictor: on a typical NYSE trading day, about 25% of issues show no change. On the roughly ten days per year when unchanged stocks fall below 15%, the expected S&P change over the next twelve months is ten percentage points below normal. When unchanged stocks exceed 30%, the S&P goes up ten points above normal. This result would occur by chance fewer than 1 in 5,000 times. “Never sell a quiet market.”

The specialist system explains why prices cluster at round numbers. Exchange specialists hold exclusive licenses to make markets, and limit orders concentrate at multiples of 5 and 2.5 for larger stocks, 1/4 for smaller ones. Prices oscillate between these “magic numbers” until the concentration is broken. When market orders overwhelm the limits and punch through, strong underlying demand or supply is at work. Paul Cootner synthesized the framework: prices behave as a random walk with “reflecting barriers” — trending within bands, then reflecting back. Sidney Alexander’s filter rules confirmed it: small filters on the order of 5% showed significantly profitable results, with the smallest filters yielding the highest profits.

The gold pyramid trade is the chapter’s set piece. Niederhoffer turned $40,000 into $22 million by buying gold at $290 and adding to his holdings with profits every time it rose $10. Then he applied the Odysseus principle: knowing he would be tempted to hold too long, he gave his wife Susan standing instructions. “Do not listen to my entreaties if I wish to double further. If losses reach 50% of winnings, reduce positions by half. If I beg to be released, sell everything out.” When the Hunt brothers’ rumored liquidation hit and his wealth dropped roughly 50%, he called Susan begging her to “untie” him. She followed original orders and sold 75% of his positions. Had she not, his wealth would have gone to zero. Final result: he kept 5% of the high.

“The goddess of losses does everything possible to prevent her worshippers from allowing friends to protect them from her clutches.” — Niederhoffer

“Niederhoffering” becomes a verb. At Harvard, he took graduate-level courses as an undergraduate to get graded on a high curve — even with the worst grade in the graduate class (B+), it pulled his overall ranking to second of 150 economics majors. Professor Leontief later defined the technique: “Here you are, Niederhoffering the commodity markets by picking up the detritus of Soros’s trades.” In 24-hour marathon poker games at Harvard, he waited the entire length until he was dealt the perfect low hand. Opponents were so desperate to get even they would call any bet. The urge to get even is one of the most costly habits a speculator can have.

M.F.M. Osborne, a high-energy physicist at the U.S. Naval Research Laboratory, became Niederhoffer’s mentor. His paper “Brownian Motion in the Stock Market” (1959) remains the seminal paper in efficient markets. Osborne developed it by watching salmon in a river and noticing the similarity to stock price behavior. They collaborated for fifteen years. Niederhoffer considers him “the Pasteur of the field of efficient markets.”

Self-confidence runs through the chapter. Soros played tennis on October 19, 1987, while down at least $1 billion from the crash — played his best game ever, completely confident the market would create opportunities “with the same regularity and predictability as in the past.” Artie Niederhoffer played football for Brooklyn College, a team that won 1, tied 1, and lost 33 games with a cumulative score of 705–8 against them. Yet grandfather Martin wrote the coach an angry letter defending “All-American” Artie.

Chapter 6: The Nature of Games

The word “speculation” comes from speculare — men who stood at the back of Roman ships during sea voyages to find good fishing areas. Joseph in the Old Testament noticed a seven-year cycle of good and bad harvests in Egypt, convinced Pharaoh to tax excess in good years and warehouse for lean years, and became immensely wealthy. Cycle awareness is the ancient root of speculation.

The game of speculation defined: the object is to buy low, sell high. The board is the organized exchange. The equipment is a phone and money. The key strategy is to balance reward and risk; since most players lose, if you find yourself with the herd, change your plan. Trading from the middle — with the public, buying what everyone buys — is the surest ticket to disaster. Galton’s oxen study confirmed it: the compulsion to live in herds stems from inability to rely on oneself.

Adam Robinson, co-founder of the Princeton Review, applied contrarian thinking to standardized tests and markets. Early questions have obvious answers; later questions have answers the crowd always gets wrong. The truism “go with your first hunch” is a prescription for failure on harder questions. Applied to markets: go with the market at the beginning of a period, but against the market near the close.

The “fanny move” from squash becomes a market template. After six shots down the wall, fake a three-wall nick move by twisting the fanny two inches — the opponent rushes forward, and the actual shot goes straight. Six days in a row the market opens down and closes down. The seventh day it twists its fanny and goes up. Players buy, thinking the move is real. Instead it closes down. The next time it opens up, players do not buy — but this time the move is real.

An unexpected intelligence discovery at a 1995 dinner with an Indian squash player and derivatives expert led to one of Niederhoffer’s best trades. Office rental per square foot in Bombay was ten times the comparable cost in New York, yet the Indian stock market was valued at only half the P/E multiple of U.S. markets. “Something had to give. I bought the Indian stock market with abandon.” This is the gap-and-bridge principle: when two related valuations diverge dramatically, one must converge to the other.

Chain trading connects everything. A weak dollar leads to a strong mark, which pushes German bonds and stocks up, which pressures the pound and lira, which is bad for Mexico, which leads to selling the peso, Indian GDRs, and Telmex calls. Like DNA built from four simple units, good chains need few links. “If you add too many separate units to your position, it becomes unwieldy.”

The Hunt brothers silver escape provides a survival story. Niederhoffer was short silver twenty times his equity when the Hunts started bulling it from $10 toward $45. Silver went limit up two consecutive days. He found an escape as small as a “beaver lodge” — bought the spot month up $1, then switched out of shorts by selling spot and buying forward. Within a second, silver went from limit up to limit down on a planted rumor that the Hunts were selling. He covered at $11. Had he not, his remains would be “in Boot Hill.”

Soros on market positions: markets are like chess board positions — either closed (prices move in narrow bands, strategic play) or open (prices move rapidly, aggressive tactical play required). What makes Soros great is his ability to shift between the two, “fully leveraged during open positions like the British pound devaluation, patiently grinding out profit each month during closed positions.”

Chapter 7: Essential Board Games

Tom Wiswell — the undefeated World Checkers Champion for 25 years, retired at 71 — became Niederhoffer’s board-game mentor after Artie’s death. Every Tuesday for fifteen years, Wiswell came to the trading office in New York. Over those years, he drafted more than 10,000 proverbs connecting checkers to life. When Wiswell failed to appear one Tuesday, calling lost and confused on an elevator, Niederhoffer was in the middle of his worst trading loss — down 40% in one hour on yen. He did not take care of Tom as he should have. Wiswell was transferred to a VA home in Queens. “I’d been sitting on a goldmine.”

The proverbs divide into six categories, each with direct trading applications. On preparation: “The game is over before it starts because I did my homework.” “Before you ever push a piece, 90 percent of the work has already been done.” On the game itself: “You are in the greatest danger when your game appears the safest.” “Indecision is fatal. It is better to make a wrong decision than build up a habit of indecision.”

On deception: “The best trap is the trap your opponent does not see.” “When your opponent allows you to make a very inviting move, remember the story of the spider and the fly.” “The trouble with a loss is that it usually looks like a win or a draw.” “Smart gamblers never gamble, and when a master sacrifices a piece, you can usually prepare to resign.”

On postmortem analysis: “The postmortem often takes ten times as much time as the game. If only speculators would spend one-tenth the time analyzing a completed trade that they spend implementing it, they would have far fewer losses.” On character: “The player who knows how will usually draw; the player who knows why will usually win.” “If you have foresight, you will probably win; if you have insight, you will probably draw; if you have hindsight, you will probably lose.”

Perhaps the most important proverb for markets: “Moves that were once thought to draw are now known to lose; and moves once thought to win are now known to draw. Play is always in a state of flux.” This is the ever-changing cycles principle restated for the checkerboard.

T.H. Huxley provides the philosophical frame: “The chess board is the world, the pieces are the phenomena of the universe, the rules of the game are what we call the laws of Nature. The player on the other side is hidden from us. We know that his play is always fair, just, and patient. But we know, to our cost, that he never overlooks a mistake or makes the smallest allowance for ignorance.”

Soros on strategy: “If I can stay close to even when the door is closed, and come in for checkmate when the door is open, I’m delighted.” This patience — grinding during closed positions and striking during open ones — is what separates the permanent winners from the eventually ruined.

The chapter closes with a Wiswell observation that doubles as self-knowledge: “Great improvements in play are usually found by a player in solitude, and only rarely discovered in crossboard, tournament, or match play.” “The master knows exactly the right moment to do nothing.”

Chapter 8: Gambling the Vig

Artie’s reaction when young Victor reported winning a big paddle-tennis money match: “I don’t ever want to see you gamble. All gamblers die broke and most turn into degenerates along the way.” Every friend who had a gambler in the family confirmed it with a moment of silence, then: “Yes, my father was a gambler too, and he died broke.” The key conceptual distinction: gambling risk is created by the casino for entertainment; speculating risk is inherent in the real economy. But emotionally and economically, they share the same machinery.

The vig — the mandatory payment to the house — destroys all but the best-capitalized, lowest-frequency traders. NASDAQ bid-ask spread on a $10 stock is 2.5%; with 4x annual turnover, this erases the entire 10% secular trend in stocks. In a fair coin game where the broker takes 20 cents per toss, after 200 tosses the chance of ending a winner is approximately 1 in 100,000. Casino roulette at 5% house edge for 10,000 bets produces winning probabilities near zero. Options house edge typically runs 12–50%.

Marty Reisman, the former World Table Tennis Champion and master hustler, embodies the vig lesson. His warmup included shattering a cigarette at the far corner with his forehand (clocked at 112 mph) and playing with the edge of his racquet. But Reisman started with $4,000, ran penny stocks to $1.5 million in profit, then lost it all — his broker made over $1 million in commissions; the bid-ask spread added another double that. Reisman’s bookie father had warned him: “The house will grind through a sack of gold.” Forgotten and relearned.

Gambler’s ruin mechanics: a speculator with $2,000 putting $1,000 on a $50,000 face-value future fluctuating $400 per day faces daily fluctuations equal to 20% of bankroll. Three average daily moves against him wipe out margin and half his capital. Two forces grind the public: Mr. Vig (80% of ruin cases) and Mr. Leverage (20% of ruin cases). With less than 50% win probability, the losing player should bet maximum — the only way to win — which is why casinos have bet limits.

The poker loss to Oswald Jacoby is the chapter’s defining moment. After poker research at Chicago, Niederhoffer thought he was a master. Jacoby — from the pantheon of bridge and poker greats, also from Brooklyn — tapped him on every pot. When Niederhoffer folded, Jacoby showed worthless hands. When he called, Jacoby always held the lock. He lost 125% of his net worth ($5,000) in one game, had to borrow from his wife. He never played poker again, never bet on anything since. “Whenever I start to lose big in speculation, I think back to Jacoby, to my father bailing me out at Harvard, and to all the broken glass in Brighton.”

“Gambling in stocks, after following a legitimate business, is like quaffing brandy after sipping claret.” — William Fowler, 1870

On shorting: Niederhoffer considers shorting stocks a gamble with an initial house take of 3% plus 10% annual secular trend working against him. The number of short- only hedge funds is constantly diminishing; their reported returns of -20% per year drastically understate the true loss because funds that went belly up are removed from the sample. His fear of loss on shorts has prevented ruin but also prevented greatness. The squash parallel: Sharif Khan won the North American Open five years running because he was willing to take chances and make errors. Niederhoffer could go an entire match with no errors but could never consistently beat Khan. “I’ll never be a Soros. To go for broke takes the instinct of the professional gambler.”

Russ Shields, Niederhoffer’s Chicago poker nemesis, provides a coda on transferable skills. Raised in Kansas, responsible for 150 oil wells by age 15, Shields hustled golf with only a putter and used card counting in gin rummy. He returned twenty years after Chicago to pitch a computer-based highway map system for every road in America, navigable by satellite. Niederhoffer invested. By 1996, the system was working — GPS navigation for every car. The hustler’s eye for asymmetric opportunity applies equally to cards, commodities, and technology.

Chapter 9: Horse Racing and Market Cycles

The racetrack is Niederhoffer’s primary market education. He goes to the races when too stressed to trade. Favorites at short odds are like blue-chip growth stocks: they win more often but the payoff is lackluster when they do. Long-shot nags are like value stocks: they lose occasionally but losses are small since expectations are already low. The track’s 20% take — commissions, breakage, and track maintenance — is the exact analog of the exchange’s bid-ask spread plus commissions plus bad execution.

Bookie, Niederhoffer’s racing mentor, grew up near Sheepshead Bay Racetrack. He shoveled manure as a kid and eventually earned the trainer’s bet placements. Bookie clocked workouts in the morning, hired sentries for paddock body language, and never bet on a horse with an erection in the paddock (it makes the horse lag). His key technique: finding discrepancies between bookmaker odds and the pari-mutuel pool, then hitting favorable lines before odds adjusted — identical to timing orders around market opens. He spread bets among eight to ten bookmakers, keeping deposits small so none noticed. His favorite bet: a trainer’s horse sent out to win after poor recent performance, especially when the trainer had just reclaimed a horse at higher price than it was claimed away. Direct application: insider buying after disappointing earnings.

Bookie’s three secrets of success, revealed during a five-mile swim to Rockaway: integrity (customers must believe in your fairness), guts (courage to take big bets even when you cannot lay off), and initiative (give customers excitement). The meta-secret: “Live a good, balanced life. When I make this swim, I always take a partner. If I take bad risks in personal life, I might take too big a risk making the book.” The Dutch book — laying off bets on both sides at favorable odds for guaranteed profit regardless of outcome — is the essence of arbitrage.

Robert Bacon’s Secrets of Professional Turf Betting is mandatory reading for all new employees at Niederhoffer’s firm. “I consider it one of the best books I’ve ever read. I go back to it for a fresh supply of ideas when I get in a rut.” Its core principle: “Copper the public’s ideas at all times.”

Switch-itis is the killer of speculators and the chapter’s most important lesson. From Bacon’s “Keep Out of Those Switches!” chapter: the amateur bets to win, the horse runs third. Bets to show, the horse runs second. Bets on a long shot to show — it wins at $44.80 but pays only $2.40 to show. Niederhoffer’s own currency confession: sold yen (wrong), sold deutschemark (Fed intervened), increased size to 200 contracts to catch up, bought Swiss franc (cut profits too early), reduced to 25 contracts (missed the big pound move), reversed bonds (opened against him). “It’s not the losses that do speculators in — it’s the switches.”

The Principle of Ever-Changing Cycles is the single most important principle of speculation. From Bacon: any edge that becomes known gets driven down until it disappears. Beyer speed figures were profitable in the 1980s. When the Daily Racing Form began publishing them to the general public, a 1995 study showed that betting the highest Beyer figure produced a 38% net loss. What once was a valuable tool became a money loser. Then the cycle turns: bettors discard the number, odds improve, and the edge returns briefly — until it gets popular again. At any given moment, the winning method is the one that is currently unpopular.

Applied to markets: Bob Prechter, Elaine Garzarelli, and Mario Gabelli called the 1987 crash and became prophets. Their following drove their fills worse. Niederhoffer’s practice: “When I buy a mutual fund, I always look for the worst-performing advisor in the previous period, not the best.” For his 1996 crown jewel, he chose an Indian fund down 60% in 1995 while the average fund was up 25%.

Emmanuel Lasker provides the discipline standard: “When you see a good move, wait — look for a better one.” Professional handicappers spend 16 hours a day on research but bet only two or three races with the greatest profit potential. The professional’s one advantage over the market maker: the opportunity to be selective.

Chapter 10: Deception and Charts

Deception is universal, and charts are its tool. Niederhoffer fell “hook, line, and sinker” for technical analysis literature early in his career — the long-term charts, the repetitive cycles, the trendlines drawn with a ruler. It took five major lessons to learn that things are seldom what they seem. Markets use deception as a survival mechanism, just like every organism from viruses to humans.

Lesson 1 came from Artie’s nature walks at Brighton Beach: chameleons blending into leaves, praying mantises with false eyes, beetles with a hundred colors of disruptive coloration, moths looking like dead twigs. “Every great sports technique is learned from nature. Concealing, decoys, distraction, playing dead, Trojan horses.”

Lesson 4 — primate deception — provides the evolutionary logic. Young baboon Paul yelled a false danger signal to scare away female Mel who was digging for a bulb, then stole it when she fled. A chimp closed a metal food box and waited for the second chimp to leave, then reopened it — but the second chimp had been hiding behind a tree and came back to steal the food. Byrne and Whiten’s conclusion: “Machiavellian intelligence” — the ability to defeat one ruse with another — is likely the adaptive pressure that favored the enormous human brain. The only primate without a single documented case of trickery: the lemur family, the smallest-brained.

Lesson 5 changed everything. In March 1964, Niederhoffer visited John Magee, author of Technical Analysis of Stock Trends, in Springfield, Illinois. The office was a decaying building with windows boarded up to eliminate the influence of weather on objectivity, newspapers two weeks old to avoid “beclouding the timeless nature of conclusions.” Niederhoffer realized: if moths, monkeys, and men practice deception as a regular survival technique, speculators should pay more attention to deception than to Magee’s naive extrapolations.

B.H. Liddell Hart’s indirect approach provides the military framework: “The direct assault of new ideas provokes stubborn resistance. Conversion is achieved more easily by unsuspected infiltration.” Three historical deception templates emerge: the Decoy (Deborah and Barak luring Sisera’s 900 iron chariots into a marshy plain), the False Gift (the Trojan Horse), and the Holdup (the 1992 British pound collapse from $2.00 to $1.40 — trend followers bought the breakout above $2.00 and lost $40,000 per contract in four months).

Playing dead is quantifiable. After 1,296 days when bonds showed small one-day changes, the average change five days later was 1.29%. After 197 days with large changes (one point or more), the average was only 1.05% — but volatility was 22% greater after small changes. Markets that seem to be sleeping are actually coiled. Louis L’Amour: “When you see excessive volatility, be careful. When you don’t see excessive volatility, be doubly careful.”

Concealing coloration has been measured in the Swiss franc. Each day, compare the current move to the most similar past move. Correlation between the most similar prior move and the next day’s actual move: -0.10. Of 100 similarity relations tested, only one showed a positive correlation with subsequent moves. This explains why nearest-neighbor, neural network, and clustering trading techniques consistently underperform: the market actively conceals its patterns.

The 1987 crash is the supreme example of disruptive behavior: the Dow dropped 508 points on October 19, then opened at 1856 and dropped to 1723 the next morning. The disruptive move scared all bulls out, leaving only the strong hands who then participated in a 250% rise over nine years. And the 1992 British pound episode is a tempo fake — the market delayed its actual move, causing speculators to misread the signal.

Chapter 11: Sex

Somerset Maugham called sex “topic A,” yet no scholarly literature connects sex and speculation. Niederhoffer proposes to fill the gap, beginning with two personal anecdotes that demonstrate sex as competitive poison. At eleven, leading the Brighton handball finals 21–14 and 16–11, he saw a French girl in a bikini walk past with a crowd of cheering men. He kept looking. He lost. At the 1974 National Squash Championship, leading heavily, he started thinking about an attractive Daily News reporter who had arranged a post-match interview. He fell behind 5–0 in the fifth game before catching himself: “I was in no way going to give those stuck-up s.o.b.’s the satisfaction of seeing me lose.” He won 18–13. Rule: when sex is in the air, it is not the time to speculate.

Historical case studies confirm the pattern. John Law killed a rival for a woman’s affections, fled to France, and created the Mississippi Company bubble. Ladies frequented his drawing room, willing to “sexulate” in return for stock. Jesse Livermore went bankrupt four times, “married three times, keeping an endless supply of mistresses, drinking like a fish, yachting aboard his 202-foot Anita.” James Fisk used Erie Railroad money to buy Pike’s Grand Opera Palace, filled it with actresses as his private harem, and was shot dead by his girlfriend’s other boyfriend at 36. His abstemious partner Jay Gould, who thought of nothing but business, built a fortune that would make him a billionaire today.

Niederhoffer quantifies the relationship using the “sex-to-Shakespeare ratio” — books published on sex divided by books on Shakespeare, tracked from 1886 to 1995 using the World Library Retrieval System (30 million titles). Shakespeare books: 126 (1886) to 345 (1995). Sex books: 42 to 1,842. Correlation of the sex- to-Shakespeare ratio with annual Dow change: 0.5. Odds against this by random chance: 1,000 to 1. But Steve Stigler warns of “bogus correlation” — the Dow change predicts the ratio one year later just as well as the ratio predicts the Dow. Which way does causation run? Caution is necessary, but the data is suggestive.

Caroline Baum receives the chapter’s highest compliment: her Telerate bond market columns are “among the finest literature I have ever read.” Greenspan upon meeting her: “This is the one person in the world who can make the flattening of the yield curve sound pornographic.”

Grandfather Martin’s secret connection to Scott Joplin gets a darkly comic reveal. Martin took piano lessons from Joplin and personally carried his family’s castoff clothes to Joplin’s Harlem home, ostensibly to help the poor. The family felt warm satisfaction at the generosity. Seventy-five years later, a researcher confirmed: the clothes were for a brothel run by Joplin’s wife, in which Joplin resided.

The chapter’s practical wisdom distills to a single observation from Kenneth Fisher: of seven great speculators who ended as losers, three — Fisk, Heinze, and Livermore — lost because they were “oversexed.” J.P. Morgan’s solution: “He arranged that he and his wife would never be on the same continent at the same time.”

Chapter 12: Returns and Randomness: Academic Style

The chapter opens at the Chicago Ph.D. Finance Seminar, where Niederhoffer announced he would show markets were “replete with regularities from the most microscopic tick-by-tick data right up to yearly moves.” When he said “You can’t prove a negative,” pandemonium broke out — booing, hissing, professors demanding retraction. He was refused the chance to present his literature review. Ironic follow-up: all four graduate students at that meeting subsequently became partners at investment firms applying specialized knowledge to uncover inefficiencies.

Jim Lorie and the founding of CRISP anchor the chapter. Merrill Lynch’s Louis Engel called Lorie in June 1961 asking for research data to support stock investing. The result: the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRISP), collecting monthly stock data for every NYSE stock from 1926 forward. Key finding: in all 2,211 year-to-year combinations between 1925 and 1991, only 72 had negative returns. For investments held at least seven years, only five showed losses. Jeremy Siegel’s summary: U.S. stocks have returned 6% above inflation per year for 200 years.

Four nonrandom properties of market transactions survive rigorous testing: a general tendency for price reversal between trades; reversals concentrated at integers where slow participants place limit orders; fast-moving floor traders pocketing the spread at those integers; and after two price changes in the same direction, a greater probability of a third in the same direction than after two changes in opposite directions.

The RH Hoe short squeeze is a devastating practical lesson. Niederhoffer shorted this Chicago printing equipment maker — no earnings in five years, trading at $18. The stock rose to 20, 30, 40, 50. He increased his short. He covered between 90 and 95. Total loss: nearly $100,000. One month after he covered, RH Hoe declared bankruptcy. A broker chuckled: “Oh yeah, we had some fun squeezing the shorts on that one.” Always know who you are betting against.

Calendar effects stand up to statistical testing. Turn of month: first ten days of the month in S&P futures averaged +2.02 points versus +0.68 for the last ten days — almost a 3x difference. January Barometer: in 21 years when January declined, the next 11 months averaged +2.3% (rising only 50% of the time). In 40 years when January rose, the next 11 months averaged +8.7% (rising 80% of the time), with 99% confidence in nonrandom effect. Decennial patterns: “5” years averaged +27% (99.6% confidence). “7” years averaged -6.5%.

The Blue Monday finding is both proof of concept and proof of decay. In each of the 60 years before his Chicago days, chances of a Dow rise on Monday were lower than on Friday — a 20-percentage-point negative bias. The finding made Niederhoffer’s “immediate and extended family of customers a substantial nine figures.” But since the 1987 crash, Monday has been the strongest day and Friday the weakest — the exact opposite. Trading the Blue Monday effect he discovered 30 years ago would have cost over 2,000 Dow points in the post-crash period.

The published-anomalies paradox: “There’s always a web. But the problem is, the web is always changing.” When a significant effect is reported, the trade takes advantage “with great abandon, huge capital resources, and low costs.” When a delusion is widely believed, the trade front-runs the buying and selling. Niederhoffer’s own practice: when publishing his seasonality research, he “withheld the good stuff.”

Arthur Jensen’s Berkeley incident illuminates academic bias: Jensen published controversial educational research in the Harvard Educational Review, and professors demanded his firing. Niederhoffer discovered that none of the condemning professors had read the article. The university had only two copies, both checked out to the Genetics Department. He verified with the journal: not one outspoken critic had a subscription. The lesson applied to markets: “My views always seem to be completely against the weight of the best academic research. Invariably, a ticket to the poorhouse.” He tuned out all media except the National Enquirer.

Chapter 13: Connections to Monitor

Darwin’s cats-mice-bees-clover-cattle chain provides the template: every market is connected to every other through chains of substitution, liquidity, and sentiment. The most dramatic example: El Niño weakened Pacific winds, warmed the Humboldt Current, destroyed the Peruvian anchovy harvest, and forced farmers to substitute soybean meal for fish meal — soybeans rose from $3.40 to $11 per bushel in 1973.

Artie’s bedtime story about Leiningen and the Ants planted the seed. A prospector found a gold mother-lode but failed to ask the Indians about the army of ants that would descend. Artie applied it to markets in 1950: “In Brazil, they’re cutting down the rain forest. The ants are forced to wander. When there is terror in Brazil, there is terror in Texas. Everything is connected.”

The October 1987 crash was a chain reaction: Dow up 37% in ten months while bonds fell 20%, creating a 7.6% yield differential near the highest in history. Dollar declined 10%. On Sunday, October 18, Baker appeared on “Face the Nation” and threatened to let the dollar decline further. German Bundesbank President Pohl told his governors the next day: “Baker did it.” On crash day: dollar down 1%, copper and soybeans down 6%, Hong Kong down 11.1%. Within eight years, stocks tripled.

Niederhoffer’s market correlations table reveals hidden structure: Swiss franc, British pound, and deutschemark are 70–90% correlated with each other. Bonds versus gold: -0.27 correlation, confirming gold as inflation harbinger. Counterintuitively, rising interest rates (falling bond prices) tend to accompany a weak dollar, not a strong one.

Steve Stigler’s stork-birthrate correlation provides a permanent warning: decline in German stork population correlated with declining German birthrate. The same chart appeared with different labels in a major business magazine showing central bank purchases explaining U.S. interest rate declines. “Ninety-nine percent of the visual correlations of two time series are spurious.”

The 1994 dollar-yen success and 1995 failure tell the complete story. In 1994, whenever U.S. stocks took a big dive, central banks stepped in to arrest dollar declines. Whenever the dollar started declining against the yen around Tokyo lunchtime (10 PM New York), Niederhoffer bought — nine times out of ten, intervention followed. This made him the world’s most successful fund manager in 1994. Then in March 1995, the dollar collapsed from 93.50 to 88 in 36 hours. He bought expecting the usual intervention. Instead, the yen moved another 2.5% against him. With 10x leverage, that meant a 25% loss in assets within an hour. He was premature by only four hours — Japanese central banks reversed the move at 4 PM. He bought Japanese stocks expecting a rally on a trade deal — the market dropped 5% when the deal was announced. Over the next ten months, Japanese stocks rose 40% from his exit point.

Five reasons explain why intermarket analysis becomes obsolete: interrelations always change; former employees managing $1 billion collectively arbitrage away the obvious patterns; books like Intermarket Analysis have made these connections required reading; computerized black boxes tracking correlations are widely available; and top traders have formed breakfast clubs to discuss current interrelations. The connections list to monitor includes pizza orders at the White House (war alert), tanker levels, mall traffic, cigarette butt length, Big Gulp cup fill level, full moon, travel plans of Soros, active beaver lodges, and migrating bird counts.

The Japan field trip confirms the limits. Fruit costs in Japan: $75 cantaloupe, $50 peach. Conformity: out of 300 male office workers counted, 299 wore white button-down shirts. Suspicion: Japanese institutions receive U.S. economic data ahead of U.S. traders through government- industry connections. “The moves in Japanese markets on the day before important U.S. numbers are invariably on the mark.”

Chapter 14: Music and Counting

Leibniz: “Music is the pleasure the human soul experiences from counting without being aware it is counting.” This sentence bridges Niederhoffer’s musical education and his statistical trading approach. Artie was the “Music Cop” — he hummed, sang, danced, and played violin everywhere. Schubert’s Trout Quintet was his favorite, and Niederhoffer hears it in markets during gentle, favorable uptrends. Haydn’s “Surprise” Symphony — fortissimo chord “to make the women jump” — maps to sudden spikes after holiday- like meandering.

The LoBagola pattern captures one of Niederhoffer’s most original market observations. LoBagola’s 1930 book described elephants on a “romp” trampling everything in their path — and always returning via the same route. Markets do the same: T-Bill futures traced the path 5→16→6→17→7→11; silver went $5→$50→$5. Prices revisit old levels with eerie reliability. The key is patience — set the trap where you know they will return.

Elliott Wave theory is rejected as nonfalsifiable pseudoscience: two Elliott Wave theorists always disagree on what wave count applies. No Fibonacci ratio appears in mainstream scientific literature. Niederhoffer rejects it as a system that “describes everything and predicts nothing.”

Francis Galton is the model counter. His motto: “Wherever you can, count.” He carried a pin and index card at all times to discreetly tabulate anything noteworthy, structuring observations into three categories: more, equal to, or less. Seven- year-old Victor first saw counting in action at Garber Stadium, watching Artie make notations — OTWK (off-the-wall killer), KW (killer winner), A (ace) — calculating win probabilities after runs of winning and losing points.

Mozart’s two defects become Niederhoffer’s self-diagnosis: gambling addiction and writing music with “too many notes.” Niederhoffer’s parallels: “I make too many trades.” The two greatest speculative sins are excess frequency and the gambler’s compulsion. Soros’s advice: “Whatever you do, don’t trade for me until you’ve had your feet in the grave for a few weeks.”

Brother Roy built a computer voice synthesizer linked to the price feed. When prices hit extremes: “New low in crude.” “Oh, copper!” “Soros selling.” When losses hit a predetermined target: “Tapped out. Call it a day.” They eventually disconnected it — infuriating to have a kibitzer when losing. A separate music synthesizer assigned instruments to markets — piano for stocks, strings for interest rates, shakuhachi flute for the yen — but the output was too cacophonous to be useful.

Paul Buethe, a Columbia MBA from Mergers and Acquisitions, was recruited after Niederhoffer heard him playing technically flawless Gershwin while nursing a big loss. “A man who could play piano like that belonged in trading where life and death hang in the balance every minute.” After big losses, they play Chopin’s Funeral March as a duet.

The chapter’s climax maps Beethoven’s Fifth to the October 1987 crash. The symphony structure — three quick notes plus one long (da-da-da-DUM), wild abandon, silence, then triumph in C major — mirrors October 19–20. Down 500 points. Short upticks of hope. Then violent selling resumed, trading halted everywhere except the Chicago CBOT Major Market Index. Out of silence: sustained buying, Dow up 200 points by close. “The seeds were planted for the greatest bull market in history, encompassing a tripling in the next nine years.”

Chapter 15: The Ecology of Markets

Niederhoffer proposes each futures and foreign exchange market as an ecological food chain. Energy (losses) flows from the public — inflexible, undercapitalized, hopeful — to primary consumers (banks and dealers: Bankers Trust, Goldman Sachs, Citibank) who extract bid-ask spread profits year after year. These flow to secondary consumers (large hedge funds: Quantum, Tiger, Omega) who feed on dealer uncertainty and government information flow. At the bottom, decomposers (floor traders, telephone brokers, bankruptcy specialists) recycle orders and extract slippage. The whole system requires a constant stream of new public participants to sustain itself.

“The role of the public in this field is to be eaten.” — Niederhoffer

The managed futures industry is a particular target. It grew from $2 billion to $25 billion between 1981 and 1996. First-year funds report average returns above 50% (Rentzler discovered), thanks to survivorship bias: funds that report at year-end are winners, and losers withdraw. Attorney John Allen: “Who, in his right mind, would invest in managed futures, if he knew he had to make 34% in one year just to equal the yield of a T-bill?”

Foreign exchange dealer mechanics expose the inner workings. Banks earn from the bid-ask spread on $1.2 trillion per day in volume. The key advantage: no central clearing mechanism, so customers must exit at the same bank they entered. Banks know customer deposit amounts and therefore know when customers will be forced to sell. Niederhoffer calls this the Roach Motel: “Easy to check in, very tough to check out.” Banks track customer positions via computer programs and know whether each customer is a trend follower, pyramider, or stop-loss operator. In yen markets, dealing banks talk daily to track who is long and who is short.

Government bond auctions inject energy into the system. Bonds fall roughly one-third of a point in the three days preceding each quarterly auction and rise one-third of a point afterward — data from 27 auctions since 1987. The two-thirds-point round trip on $60 billion of quarterly debt translates to roughly $400 million per year of energy flowing through the system.

Hedge funds are the top predators: roughly $70 billion as of mid-1996. Quantum Fund returned 35% per year for 35 years, turning $1,000 in 1962 into $1.5 million by 1995. Their edge: insider access to government information flow. “Business, governments, and dealers would benefit from some market event. They then join hands and circle to the left.” Game-preserve holdings, revolving-door government officials, spouses at central banks, tennis with central bankers.

The March 1995 decomposer circle demonstrates the food chain in action. After losing 50% of assets under management in one hour: friends called to report rumors, brokers called to increase margin and demanded immediate payment at the worst momentary price, banks demanded repayment of all outstanding loans, leading industry weeklies ran stories even after Niederhoffer persuaded them he was viable, and clearing banks pulled credit. Like Capablanca after his famous loss, he came back +60% in the next twelve months.

Niederhoffer never uses stops: “Having a fixed rule to exit provides my adversaries too great an advantage. It would be the equivalent of automatically defaulting in a game if an opponent hits a series of good shots.” The alternative: trade with low enough percentage of capital at risk so disaster cannot knock at the door. Floor traders profit by “running the stops” — intentionally moving prices to trigger preset exit levels.

The Principle of Competitive Exclusion applies to markets: no two species with identical styles can occupy the same niche. Chicago gold versus New York gold could not both survive. CBOT stock futures and IMM stock futures could not coexist. Coexistence requires niche differentiation — Treasury bonds (30-year) and Eurodollar futures (3-month) trade seven blocks apart, move tick for tick, but occupy separate niches. Homeostasis returns prices to equilibrium. Contrarians and value investors are the main homeostatic forces; trend followers and options hedgers create positive feedback. If trend followers lose consistently, “they will not prosper or reproduce.”

Finale

The Finale is the book’s emotional crescendo. Doc Bo Keeley — veterinarian, hobo, national paddleball champion — lives in the closet of Niederhoffer’s basement six months a year when not riding the rails. His economic indicators from Skid Row constitute genuine intelligence: the hobo-to- tramp ratio (hobos read the Wall Street Journal before using it as insulation; tramps just insulate), freight car counts at fixed points, the smiles-to-growls ratio at Skid Row movies, dog dental work at vet clinics as a leading indicator, the beef-to-potato ratio at Salvation Army, and cigarette butt length. February 1996: Doc Bo reported double-normal freight car rates in Jacksonville, Denver, and Salt Lake City — employment obviously booming. Niederhoffer shorted bonds. February employment came in at +800,000 (one of the largest ever), and bonds dropped three points.

Bargaining as speculation training: Niederhoffer’s reticence once cost him $15 million — in a 1965 partnership split, he said “equal” when he could have gotten 90%. Now he bargains for everything: “That’s a little over my budget; is there any adjustment possible for cash or quantity?” He averages 50% off retail at owner-operated stores. “The extra 1/100 of 1 percent I save in face value on each transaction is good for at least 50 percent of my total profits over the course of an average year.”

Candlestick analysis receives a systematic indictment: no quantitative studies of results have ever been conducted; many patterns require three or more events each at 1/4 probability, yielding only 1/64 frequency — insufficient for scientific analysis; and patterns are subject to varied interpretation. When colleague Jon Normile walked in to announce bonds fell because of a “Black Doji Star,” the office responded with ritualistic chanting.

The July 16, 1996 panic provides a real- time example of everything the book teaches. The S&P dropped 11% from July 1 in ten days; NASDAQ fell 19% from May 31. In a 45-minute vortex, S&P futures dove from 625 to 610.25. Mutual funds sold index futures for liquidity when no buyers of individual stocks appeared. Niederhoffer lost 15% of assets in one hour without collegial support. He closed positions at break-even in desperation after the Dow rallied 200 points, then watched it rally another 50 after he sold.

Value stocks receive surprising skepticism. Niederhoffer tested 30 lowest price-to-book stocks from Value Line each year against the S&P: in 6 of 10 years, value stocks underperformed. $100 in value stocks became $250 versus $280 for the S&P. “Thank God I only buy in for 5 percent of the deals when it comes to value stocks.”

Artie’s last checkers game is one of the great scenes in financial literature. After 35 years and thousands of games, Victor had never won. On December 28, 1980, in their final game, Artie said “You have a good move” — Victor found the solution to block him. “Great, you finally beat me. I’m blocked on the board the same way the cells are blocked in my body. You’re the master now.” Victor realized later that Artie had seen this final position coming many moves before and purposely played toward it — giving his son his first and final victory.

Artie’s deathbed conversation at 4 AM in Memorial Hospital: “If you are here at this hour, I must really be on the way out. I know how you hate hospitals.” Then his final warning: “Are you sure all those technical figures of yours can take account of fundamentals like wars, elections, volcanoes? I’ve had to deliver lots of bodies of destitute bums to the morgue from the Bowery, and many of them had more statistics on them from the Morning Telegraph than you compiled.” Victor’s reply: “Dad, don’t worry. I’ve got it all under control.”

“Go out and get me some ices.” — Artie’s final words, eight hours before he died

After Artie died, Niederhoffer gave up his wife, home, wealth, daily nurturing of his children, business partners, trophies, and racquets. He cried for Artie every day for five years. Eventually he rebuilt — Tom Wiswell for board games, courted future Mrs. Niederhoffer, played piano with Robert Schrade (who embodied Artie’s character), developed a surrogate familial relationship with Soros through tennis, chess, and speculation.

The Code of Brighton is Niederhoffer’s political philosophy: referees (governments) should be nearly invisible. No referee in Brighton ever took more than a quarter of a player’s points — if they did, players would stop trying. When the income referee takes more than half your points, “does he really expect me and others to try very hard?” But political mispricing creates opportunity: Mexican treasury bills after the 1995 bailout yielded 50–100% nominal. Politicians needing to prevent further devaluation made the trade safe. Niederhoffer bought “with abandon” as politicians patted themselves on the back.

Steve Stigler sent Niederhoffer a Texas study on “Heritability of Angling Vulnerability in Largemouth Bass.” Bass caught three or more times (vulnerable stock) versus those never caught (wary): the offspring of vulnerable parents were caught twice as often. The market parallel: vulnerable public participants breed more vulnerable participants through imitation and inherited risk appetite; wary survivors breed better- adapted traders. Evolution selects for market savvy, and the public is a renewable resource via naive new entrants.

The book closes with “A Long Two Minutes” — a set piece condensing two minutes of market chaos into seven pages. 8:30 AM, Bundesbank press conference, dollar cratering overnight, Hong Kong down 4%. Headline: “BUNDESBANK LEAVES INTEREST RATE UNCHANGED.” Dollar up 2%, bonds up 3%, buy more. Then “CORRECTION” flashes: rates actually raised 50 basis points. Dollar down 2% then another 2%. Margin calls. An accidental fill on the wrong account. Neighbor lawsuit. Computer drones: “Victor, you’re tapped out.” Victor doubles up. Susan walks in: “Can you make sure the kids finish their piano practice? You look like you’ve been through a deluge. Everything OK?” Victor: “Oh, no, business as usual.” The timestamp: “New York, 8:32 A.M.”

Trading & Investing Lessons

The Revenge Factor as Mean Reversion Strategy

Niederhoffer’s data across 18 markets showing +20.2% average returns after worst-performing years validates the contrarian approach. The practical application: after a market crash, buying beaten-down assets is statistically favorable. This maps directly to buying quality stocks after 20%+ drawdowns and holding through the mean-reversion period.

Switch-Itis as the Core Trading Pathology

The racetrack lesson translates perfectly: most trading losses come not from being wrong on direction but from constantly changing size, instrument, and timeframe in reaction to recent results. A disciplined trader who maintains consistent position sizing and strategy through inevitable drawdowns will outperform a brilliant but impulsive trader who switches after every loss.

The Vig as Position Sizing Constraint

Niederhoffer’s gambler’s ruin calculations prove that transaction costs compound to destroy all but the most efficient traders. The practical rule: minimize trading frequency, negotiate tighter spreads, and ensure that expected profit per trade exceeds total friction by a meaningful multiple.

Ever-Changing Cycles and Strategy Decay

Every profitable strategy decays once discovered. The Beyer speed figure story is the template: profitable for a season, then a money loser once published. This applies directly to backtested trading systems — any edge visible in historical data is already being arbitraged away by the time it is implemented.

The Odysseus Rule for Risk Management

Pre-committing to exit rules before entering a trade — and giving those rules to someone who will enforce them regardless of your emotional state — is the single most effective risk management technique in the book. The gold pyramid trade proved it: Susan’s enforcement of the pre-committed exit saved Niederhoffer from total ruin.

Hoodoo Recognition as Due Diligence

The hoodoo concept is a screening tool for counterparty risk. When a pitch comes wrapped in photos with politicians, outsized lifestyle signaling, and explanations of “bad luck,” the odds favor walking away. The Rothschilds’ refusal to deal with hoodoos regardless of references is a timeless risk filter.

Key Takeaways

Speculation Is Applied Science

The only durable edge comes from rigorous quantitative testing. Count everything. Falsify hypotheses. Apply the Bonferroni adjustment. Ninety percent of market commentary is pseudoscience dressed in the robes of the Delphic Oracle.

Markets Are Ecological Systems

Energy flows from public to dealers to hedge funds to decomposers. Understanding your position in the food chain — and the vig each layer extracts — determines whether you survive long enough to exploit any edge.

Deception Is the Default

Markets actively conceal their patterns. Swiss franc data shows negative correlation between similar past moves and future moves. Charts do not reveal truth — they reveal what the market wants you to see.

Mean Reversion Is Real But Dangerous

Post-panic buying works statistically, but buying into panics in bonds nearly destroyed Niederhoffer. The revenge factor provides an edge only with adequate capitalization and genuine patience.

The Vig Kills Silently

Bid-ask spreads, commissions, and bad execution compound to a staggering tax. The public loses not because it is wrong about direction but because the vig grinds away any right decisions before they compound.

Hubris Precedes Ruin

“I’ve got it all under control” is the most dangerous sentence in speculation. Niederhoffer catalogs his own hubris — buying more S&Ps during the 1987 crash, adding a “fun” position after a yen win — with unflinching honesty. The quantified speculator is still human.

Contrarian Ideas

Stop Losses Are Counterproductive

Contrary to standard risk management advice, Niederhoffer argues stops feed information to adversaries and invariably trigger at the worst moment. The alternative — appropriate leverage — is harder but more effective.

Shorting Stocks Is Almost Always a Losing Game

Even Soros lost more money selling short than in any other activity. The costs compound: interest, dividends, risk premium, and a 10% secular trend working against you.

Published Anomalies Are Self-Defeating

Every calendar effect, seasonal pattern, and momentum signal decays once published. Niederhoffer’s own Blue Monday effect reversed completely after the 1987 crash. The only profitable anomaly is the one nobody knows about.

Value Investing Underperforms

In Niederhoffer’s testing, 30 lowest price-to-book stocks underperformed the S&P in 6 of 10 years. The conventional wisdom about cheap stocks is at best unreliable.

Athletic Background Matters More Than Finance Degrees

Exchange floors are studded with former athletes. Competitive sports teach concentration, loss management, and economy of motion — all more relevant to trading success than academic credentials.

The National Enquirer Is Better Than the Financial Press

Niederhoffer reads no financial news, owns no television, and does not follow the market commentary that drives most traders’ decisions. His only newspaper: the National Enquirer. The less conventional information you consume, the less likely you are to be part of the herd.

Proposals & Frameworks

The Pseudoscience Checklist (9 Items)

Appeal to authority, absence of counting, untestable predictions, tautological framing, no allowance for sampling variation, paranoid tone, disregard of alternatives, self-evaluation of accuracy, retrofitted systems. Apply to any market commentary before risking capital.

The Market Ecology Model

Public (prey) → primary consumers (banks/dealers) → secondary consumers (hedge funds) → decomposers (floor traders, brokers). Energy flows from public losses through the system. Constant new public entrants sustain the ecosystem.

Wiswell’s Six Categories of Game Wisdom

Rules of the game, preparation (before), play (during), deception, postmortem (after), and character of a winner. Each category maps directly to a trading discipline.

Five Templates of Market Deception

The Decoy (luring into unfavorable terrain), the False Gift (Trojan Horse breakouts), the Holdup (ERM as trap), Playing Dead (low volatility before explosive moves), and Tempo Changes (delayed moves that cause misreading).

The Odysseus Risk Management Protocol

Pre-commit to exit rules. Give enforcement authority to someone who will not be moved by emotional appeals. Specify in advance: “If I beg you to release me, tighten my bonds.” Must be established before temptation, not during.

What Didn’t Fit

Jesse Livermore’s Complete Arc

Livermore, the “Boy Wonder,” traded with Niederhoffer’s grandfather Martin at bucket shops on New Street. His core philosophy — test Wall Street maxims, never accept received wisdom — influenced Niederhoffer’s entire approach. But Livermore went bankrupt at least three times before 1929. He put his last chips in during the early 1930s and was wiped out. He spent ten years trying to scare up a stake, tried to sell a book of insights, and failed. In 1940, at the Sherry-Netherland Hotel, he penned an eight-page suicide letter and shot himself in the hatcheck room. Even geniuses with timeless wisdom can be destroyed. Track record with full context is required.

The Damon Runyon Soda Bottle Partnership

Young Vic, Stevie, and René collected soda bottles for the two-cent deposit on national holidays, earning $15–$20 a day ($300 in today’s money). Partners planted stolen sodas in Vic’s box; police arrived and arrested him. Artie’s “Judas steer” story followed — a steer who always led others up the slaughterhouse ramp but somehow slid back to the bottom before meeting his doom. Markets play the same trick: move a few ticks in one direction to induce positions, then reverse. Beware tax-shelter investments where your “friend” backs out after you have committed funds.

The Harvard Endowment as Contrarian Signal

As of June 30, 1995, Harvard’s endowment had $35.3 billion in derivative face value on $7.7 billion net equity — leverage far exceeding Soros. They were long Sears, short J.C. Penney, and $1 billion short in stocks. Counterparties bought securities Harvard needed to borrow to pressure delivery. Even $7 billion endowments can be front-run.

The Water Main Break

On April 13, 1992, Niederhoffer held a short bond position and planned to exit at the close. The Chicago Board of Trade flooded — trading was canceled for the first time ever. When the exchange reopened three days later, he was down 100% on his equity. “Sometimes you win and sometimes you lose, and sometimes you get rained out.” Force majeure events can wipe positions regardless of correctness.

Trading While Wife Gave Birth

Niederhoffer brought a portable screen into the delivery room during Susan’s 40-hour labor, stealing glances and trading on a cellular phone during contractions. The doctor complimented him on “keeping the family posted.” Susan yelled: “Get out of here with your screen. Go trade pork bellies.” The only time in eighteen years she had done so.

Doc Bo’s Nixon Chain

The full macroeconomic domino sequence: 1960, Fed Chairman Martin kept tight monetary policy, Nixon lost to Kennedy. Nixon in 1972 used Arthur Burns to keep rates down, closed the gold window, imposed wage-price controls — worldwide liquidity explosion. Commodity demand plus low reserves led to the 1973 Israeli war and OPEC oil shock, then stagflation, then the S&P declining 52% in real terms from December 1972 to September 1974. Volcker tightened, inflation broke, and the Reagan bull market followed. Each political decision propagated through global markets for decades.

About the Author

Victor Niederhoffer (born 1943, Brooklyn) is a speculator, statistician, and former U.S. National Squash Champion. He earned his Ph.D. in statistics from the University of Chicago under Jim Lorie, taught at Berkeley, and went on to manage one of the most successful commodity funds in the world through the mid-1990s. His first client, Tim Horne, saw a $100,000 investment grow to approximately $6 million over 14 years — a compounded return of about 30% annually. As of mid-1996, most rating services ranked him number one for periods up to three years.

Niederhoffer served as George Soros’s primary broker and advisor for commodities and fixed-income trades through most of the 1980s, developing a personal relationship built on tennis, chess, and mutual competitive obsession. He was also a world-class squash player, winning the U.S. Championship and barely losing a game during ten years of reign.

In 1997, shortly after this book was published, Niederhoffer suffered catastrophic losses during the Asian financial crisis that wiped out his fund. He rebuilt, blew up again in 2007, and rebuilt once more. His career is the living embodiment of the book’s central tension: the quantified speculator who understands the dangers of hubris yet repeatedly succumbs to it.

Why This Book Matters

The Education of a Speculator is one of the most intellectually ambitious books ever written about financial markets. Where most trading memoirs offer either war stories or systems, Niederhoffer delivers a genuine philosophy of speculation rooted in science, ecology, game theory, music, literature, and competitive athletics. No other book in the genre connects Darwin to the CBOT, Beethoven to the 1987 crash, or primate deception to chart patterns with such rigor and such personal honesty.

The book matters most for what it demonstrates through its author’s own trajectory. Niederhoffer catalogs his losses with more care than his gains. He shows how the same qualities that produce exceptional returns — contrarianism, conviction, high leverage — also produce catastrophic ruin. The Principle of Ever-Changing Cycles applies to the speculator himself: the methods that made him number one in 1994 destroyed him in 1997. The book is, in retrospect, an eerily complete manual for both the making and unmaking of a speculative fortune.

Most importantly, it is a love letter to Artie — the police captain father who taught counting, economy of motion, sportsmanship, and the humility to recognize that some forces are too powerful to fight with statistics alone. Every reader who has ever said “I’ve got it all under control” will recognize themselves in Niederhoffer’s reply to his dying father — and will understand, perhaps for the first time, why that sentence is the most dangerous in speculation.
