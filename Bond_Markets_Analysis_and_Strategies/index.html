Glossary

Bond Basics

Basis point (bp): One hundredth of a percentage point. A yield rising from 4.00% to 4.25% has risen 25 bp.

Par value: The amount repaid at maturity, usually $1,000 per bond. Also called face value or principal.

Coupon: The interest a bond pays, stated as a rate on par. A 5% coupon pays $50 a year on $1,000.

Maturity: The date the debt ends and principal is repaid. Under 5 years is short-term, over 12 long-term.

Zero-coupon bond: A bond that pays no interest and is sold at a discount. Buy at $610 today, receive $1,000 in ten years.

Floating-rate note: A bond whose coupon resets to a reference rate plus a margin, such as SOFR + 1%, so its price stays near par.

Inverse floater: A bond whose coupon falls when rates rise, built by splitting a fixed-rate bond. Its duration can exceed the bond's maturity.

Callable bond: A bond the issuer may redeem early, usually when rates fall. Investors demand a higher yield for this.

Putable bond: A bond the investor may sell back to the issuer early, typically at par. It yields less than a plain bond.

Accrued interest: Interest earned since the last coupon date, paid by a buyer to the seller. 60 days into a $30 half-year coupon period is $10.

Clean / dirty price: The quoted price without accrued interest, and the actual price paid including it.

Yield and Risk Measures

Yield to maturity (YTM): The single discount rate that equates a bond's cash flows to its price. It assumes coupons are reinvested at that same yield.

Current yield: Annual coupon divided by price. $50 on a $900 bond is 5.56%; it ignores the gain to par.

Yield to worst: The lowest of the yields to every call date and to maturity, the conservative quote for callable bonds.

Total return: The return over a chosen horizon given assumptions for reinvestment and the sale price, the book's preferred measure.

Reinvestment risk: The risk that coupons are reinvested at lower rates. On a 15-year 7% bond it supplies half the promised return.

Duration (modified): The approximate percentage price change for a 100 bp move in yield. A duration of 7 means about a 7% move.

Macaulay duration: The present-value-weighted average time to a bond's cash flows; equal to maturity for a zero.

Effective duration: Duration computed by repricing with a model, letting cash flows change; required for callable bonds and MBS.

DV01 (PVBP): The dollar price change for a one basis point yield move. A $1 million 10-year 5% bond has a DV01 of about $779.

Convexity: The curvature of the price-yield relationship. Positive convexity means gains on falling yields exceed losses on rising yields.

Negative convexity: Price gains capped as yields fall, as with callable bonds and mortgage securities.

Key rate duration: Sensitivity to a yield change at one point on the curve, such as 10 years; key rate durations sum to total duration.

Spread duration: Sensitivity of price to a change in credit spread alone, holding Treasury yields fixed.

Credit spread: The extra yield over a Treasury of similar maturity. A corporate at 5.5% when Treasuries yield 4% has a 150 bp spread.

Duration times spread (DTS): Spread duration times spread, a measure of credit risk. Duration 5 at 100 bp gives a DTS of 500.

Rates and the Curve

Yield curve: Yields of bonds of similar quality plotted against maturity; usually upward-sloping.

Spot rate: The yield on a zero-coupon bond for one specific maturity, used to discount a single payment on that date.

Bootstrapping: Deriving spot rates one maturity at a time from coupon bond prices, starting from the short end.

Forward rate: A rate for a future period implied by today's spot rates. With 4% for 1 year and 5% for 2 years, the 1-year rate one year forward is 6.01%.

Term premium: The extra yield investors demand for longer maturities, which is why forward rates overshoot future rates.

Real rate / breakeven inflation: The yield after inflation, read from TIPS; the gap between nominal and TIPS yields is expected inflation.

SOFR: Secured Overnight Financing Rate, the overnight Treasury repo rate that replaced LIBOR as the main floating reference rate.

Repo (repurchase agreement): A collateralized loan: sell a bond and agree to buy it back slightly higher. $10 million overnight at 2% costs about $556.

On-the-run: The newest Treasury issue at each maturity, most liquid and yielding slightly less than older off-the-run issues.

Mortgages and Structured Products

Loan-to-value (LTV): Loan amount divided by property value. $240,000 on a $300,000 home is an 80% LTV.

Pass-through: A security passing mortgage payments, including prepayments, directly to investors.

Prepayment: Principal repaid ahead of schedule, mostly from refinancing when rates fall or from home sales.

CPR / SMM: Annual and monthly prepayment rates. A 6% CPR equals an SMM of about 0.51% a month.

PSA benchmark: A convention: CPR rises 0.2 points a month to 6% at month 30. "200 PSA" is twice that speed.

Contraction / extension risk: Principal returning too fast when rates fall, or too slowly when rates rise.

CMO: Collateralized mortgage obligation, which divides pass-through cash flows into tranches with different prepayment exposure.

Tranche: One slice of a structured deal with its own claim on cash flows and losses.

PAC bond: A tranche with a fixed principal schedule protected by support bonds within a prepayment band such as 90–300 PSA.

IO / PO strip: Securities receiving only the interest or only the principal of a mortgage pool. IOs rise in value when rates rise.

Subordination: Junior tranches that absorb losses first, protecting senior tranches.

Excess spread: Collateral interest left after paying bond coupons and fees, the first cushion against losses.

Special purpose vehicle (SPV): A separate entity that buys assets in a true sale and issues bonds, insulated from the originator's bankruptcy.

Debt service coverage (DSC): A property's net operating income divided by its mortgage payments. $1.25 million over $1 million is 1.25.

Credit and Valuation

Option-adjusted spread (OAS): The spread over the model's rate paths after removing the value of embedded options. Static spread minus OAS is the option cost.

Static (Z) spread: The constant spread over every Treasury spot rate that prices a bond's cash flows.

Binomial tree: A lattice of possible future short rates used to value bonds with embedded options.

Monte Carlo simulation: Valuation by averaging a security's value over hundreds of random rate paths; used for MBS.

Conversion ratio / price: Shares received for a convertible bond, and par divided by that number. 50 shares per $1,000 is $20 a share.

Covenant: A promise in the bond contract, such as a limit on additional debt or on payments to shareholders.

Interest coverage: EBIT divided by interest expense. $500 million over $100 million is 5 times.

Fallen angel: A bond downgraded from investment grade to high yield, often sold below fair value by forced sellers.

Default intensity: In reduced-form models, the conditional probability of default per unit of time, inferred from spreads.

Recovery rate: The share of par recovered after default; about 70% for secured loans, 40% for high-yield bonds.

Portfolios and Derivatives

Tracking error: The standard deviation of portfolio return minus benchmark return. 1 point means returns within ±1 point two years in three.

Immunization: Matching portfolio duration to a liability's date so that price and reinvestment effects offset.

Cash flow matching: Buying bonds whose payments meet each liability on its date; safer and 3–7% costlier than immunization.

Futures contract: An exchange-traded agreement to buy or sell later at a fixed price, marked to market daily.

Cheapest-to-deliver (CTD): The deliverable bond with the highest implied repo rate, which sets the Treasury future's price.

Interest-rate swap: An exchange of fixed for floating payments on a notional amount; receiving fixed adds duration.

Cap / floor: Strips of options paying when a floating rate rises above, or falls below, a set level.

Swaption: An option to enter a swap. A payer swaption gains when rates rise.

Credit default swap (CDS): Insurance against default. Protection on $10 million at 1% costs $100,000 a year.

CDS-bond basis: CDS spread minus bond spread; a negative basis means protection is cheap relative to the bond.

If You Only Remember 10 Things

  1. A bond's price is the present value of its payments, so price and yield always move in opposite directions.
  2. Duration is price sensitivity, not time: a duration of 7 means about a 7% price move for a 1-point change in yields.
  3. Convexity makes gains bigger than losses for plain bonds; callable bonds and mortgage securities have the opposite, negative convexity.
  4. Yield to maturity hides two assumptions, holding to maturity and reinvesting at the same yield; total return analysis makes them explicit.
  5. Value bonds with spot rates, not one yield, and treat forward rates as rates you can lock in, not forecasts.
  6. Every mortgage contains a borrower's prepayment option, and every mortgage bond investor has sold it.
  7. Structuring (CMOs, tranches, CLOs) moves risk between investors; it never makes the underlying risk disappear.
  8. For bonds with options, use OAS and effective duration, and remember both depend on a model and a volatility assumption.
  9. In credit, avoiding losers matters more than picking winners, and analysis starts with the business model and the covenants.
  10. Futures, swaps, options and CDS let managers change duration and credit exposure cheaply, but every hedge leaves basis risk.

Chapter 1: Introduction to Bonds

Primer

Two habits of the bond world run through the whole book. Bond people quote changes in yields in basis points: one basis point (bp) is one hundredth of a percentage point, so a yield moving from 4.00% to 4.25% has risen 25 bp, and "100 bp" is one full percentage point. And they talk about yields far more than prices. The yield is the return you lock in if you buy the bond at today's price and it pays as promised. Price and yield are two ways of saying the same thing: name one and the other follows, which is why "yields rose" and "bonds fell" are the same headline.

A bond is a debt instrument: the issuer borrows money and promises to repay the principal plus interest on dates written into the contract. That simple promise supports the largest securities market in the world, about $114 trillion outstanding, far more than the world's stock markets. The United States accounts for roughly $46 trillion of it, around 40% of the total. Fabozzi sorts the global market into six sectors: sovereign (national government) debt, semi-government debt from agencies and local authorities, corporate bonds, asset-backed and mortgage-backed securities, covered bonds, and collateralized debt obligations. Each sector has its own issuers, investors and risks, and the book spends its middle chapters on them one at a time.

Every bond has a maturity, the date the debt ends and the principal comes back. The market calls bonds under five years short-term, five to twelve years intermediate, and over twelve years long-term; a few perpetual bonds never mature at all and simply pay interest forever. Maturity matters for three reasons: it tells you how long you will receive interest, it tells you when the principal returns, and it drives how violently the price moves when interest rates change. The par value (also face value, principal or redemption value), usually $1,000 per bond, is the amount repaid. It can come back all at once at maturity, a bullet, or in pieces over the bond's life when the bond amortizes, as mortgages do.

The coupon is the interest, stated as a rate on par: a 5% coupon on a $1,000 bond pays $50 a year, in the United States usually as two $25 payments six months apart. Coupons come in several shapes. A fixed coupon never changes. A floating-rate coupon resets periodically to a reference rate plus a fixed margin. A zero-coupon bond pays nothing until maturity and is bought at a deep discount, so the gap between price and par is the interest. A step-up coupon rises on a schedule, a deferred coupon pays nothing for the first few years, and a payment-in-kind (PIK) bond lets the issuer pay interest with more bonds instead of cash, a feature mostly seen in weaker credits.

Many bonds carry embedded options, rights written into the contract that let one side change the cash flows. A call lets the issuer redeem the bond early, usually at par or a little above; a put lets the investor sell it back to the issuer; a conversion right lets the investor swap the bond for the issuer's shares; a currency option lets the holder choose which currency is paid. The rule of thumb is that an option belongs to whoever benefits from it. A call helps the issuer, so a callable bond must offer a higher yield to compensate the investor; a put or a conversion right helps the investor, so those bonds yield less than a plain bond from the same issuer.

Plain English

A call works like refinancing a mortgage, with you as the bank. A company sells you a 10-year bond paying 6%, callable after three years. Three years later rates have fallen and the company could borrow at 4%. It calls your bond, hands back your $1,000, and borrows again more cheaply. You get your money back exactly when the best you can do with it is 4%. If rates had risen instead, the company would happily keep paying you 6% while new bonds paid more.

So the issuer keeps the good outcome and you keep the bad one. That one-sided deal is a real cost to you, and it shows up as a higher yield on callable bonds. A put is the mirror image: if rates rise you can hand the bond back at par and reinvest at the new, higher rate, so you accept a lower yield for that protection.

Bonds need a common language. Each issue in the United States carries a CUSIP, a nine-character identifier: the first six characters name the issuer, the next two the issue, and the last is a check character. Foreign securities use a twelve-character CINS. The system is run by S&P on behalf of the American Bankers Association. Traders cite a bond by issuer, coupon and maturity, so "PG 2.45s 11/3/2026" is the Procter & Gamble bond with a 2.45% coupon maturing on November 3, 2026, the "s" standing in for the percent sign. The shorthand matters because a single large company may have dozens of bonds outstanding at once, each with its own coupon, maturity and features.

Fabozzi lists nine risks a bondholder carries, and the first four are about interest rates. Interest-rate risk is the biggest for high-quality bonds: when market yields rise, the price of a bond already issued must fall until its yield matches the new market rate. Call and prepayment risk is the chance of getting your principal back early, just when rates have fallen. Yield curve risk arises because rates at different maturities rarely move by the same amount, so two portfolios with the same overall sensitivity can fare very differently. Reinvestment risk is the chance that coupons must be reinvested at lower rates than expected. It works against interest-rate risk, since falling rates raise prices but lower reinvestment income, and that offset is the basis of the immunization strategies in Part V.

Credit risk breaks into three pieces. Default risk is the chance the issuer fails to pay. Credit spread risk is the chance that the extra yield investors demand over Treasuries widens, cutting the price even though the issuer pays every coupon on time. Downgrade risk is the chance that a rating agency lowers its opinion of the issuer, which usually widens the spread on the spot. Spread risk is easy to underrate: in a risk-off panic, spreads on investment-grade bonds can widen 100 to 200 basis points with no change at all in the issuer's real chance of default. Counterparty risk, the chance that the other side of a derivative fails, is a form of credit risk too, small for exchange-traded contracts backed by a clearinghouse and large for private, over-the-counter deals.

Plain English

Spread risk with numbers. Say you own a corporate bond yielding 5.5% when the Treasury of the same maturity yields 4%: a spread of 150 basis points. The company is doing fine. Then a recession scare hits and investors demand 300 basis points over Treasuries from every company like it. Treasury yields don't move, but your bond's yield has to rise to 7% for anyone to buy it.

If the bond's price moves about 7% for each percentage point of yield (a "duration" of 7, Chapter 4), that 1.5-point jump costs you about 10.5% of its value. Nobody defaulted and no coupon was missed, yet you are down a tenth of your money until the fear passes. That is why a high-quality bond can still have a bad year.

The remaining risks are just as real. Liquidity risk is the chance you cannot sell at fair value when you need to; most bonds trade rarely, some never after their first week. Exchange-rate risk hits anyone holding a bond paid in a foreign currency, since a fall in that currency cuts the value of every coupon and the principal. Volatility risk affects bonds with embedded options, whose value depends on how much markets expect rates to swing. Inflation, or purchasing-power risk, erodes what fixed payments can buy. And Fabozzi adds "risk risk": not knowing what the risk of a security actually is. "We didn't know this could happen" is the refrain after every financial calamity, from inverse floaters in 1994 to subprime mortgage bonds in 2008.

Green bonds raise money for climate and environmental projects, with more than $500 billion issued so far, against an International Energy Agency estimate that over $50 trillion of investment is needed by 2035 to hold warming to 2°C. Chapter 2: Bond Pricing

Bond pricing rests on one idea, the time value of money: a dollar today is worth more than a dollar later, because today's dollar can be invested and earn interest in the meantime. To compare a payment in the future with money today, you discount it: the present value of a future amount is PV = FV / (1 + r)n, where r is the interest rate per period and n the number of periods. At 5% a year, $1,000 due in ten years is worth $1,000 / 1.0510, about $614 today. A stream of equal payments, an annuity, has its own shortcut, PV = C × [1 − (1 + r)−n] / r, and a coupon bond is simply an annuity of coupons plus one lump sum of principal at the end.

The price of a bond is therefore the present value of every payment it promises, each discounted at the yield the market demands. U.S. bonds pay coupons twice a year, so the convention is to halve the annual coupon and the annual yield and double the number of periods. A 10-year bond with a 5% coupon pays $25 every six months for twenty periods and $1,000 at the end. If the market wants 5%, discounting everything at 2.5% per half-year gives a price of exactly $1,000. If the market wants 6%, discounting at 3% per period gives $925.61; if it wants 4%, discounting at 2% gives $1,081.76. Nothing about the bond changed in those three cases, only the return investors require from it.

That is why price and yield always move in opposite directions. A bond's coupon is fixed when it is issued, but the market's required yield changes every day. If new bonds of the same quality start paying 6%, nobody will pay $1,000 for an old bond paying 5%; its price has to fall until a buyer earns 6% from the combination of its smaller coupon and the gain as the price climbs back to par by maturity. If market yields fall to 4%, the old 5% bond becomes more valuable than a new one, and buyers bid it above par until it too yields only 4%. The price adjusts so that the bond, whatever its coupon, offers the same return as everything else in the market of its quality and maturity.

Look at the right-hand column. A one-point drop in yields gains 8.2%, but a one-point rise loses only 7.4%. Two points down gains 17.2%; two points up loses 14.2%. The gains are always a little bigger than the losses. That lopsidedness is the convexity the next paragraph describes, and it is a quiet gift to anyone who owns a plain bond.

Plot price against yield and you do not get a straight line but a curve that bows toward the origin, a shape called convex. Fabozzi draws four properties from it. All option-free bonds move opposite to yields, but by different percentages. For very small yield changes, the percentage moves up and down are almost the same. For large changes they are not. And for a change of the same size, the price rise when yields fall is larger than the price drop when yields rise. That last property is a built-in advantage for the bond owner, and it is the reason convexity, measured in Chapter 4, is something investors pay for and something callable bonds and mortgage securities take away.

Comparing the coupon with the market yield tells you where a bond trades. When they are equal, the bond sells at par; when the coupon is above the yield, at a premium; when it is below, at a discount. None of these is a bargain or a rip-off in itself. A premium bond pays more income now and gives back part of its price by maturity; a discount bond pays less income and makes it up through the climb to par. That climb is the pull to par: as maturity approaches, fewer coupons remain to be valued, and any bond's price drifts toward its face value whatever happens to rates. A premium bond bought at $1,081.76 will be worth $1,000 on the day it matures, and the investor who paid the premium knew that going in.

Three features decide how far a price moves for a given change in yield. For the same maturity and starting yield, a lower coupon means a bigger move, and a zero-coupon bond moves most of all, because all of its value sits in the distant final payment. For the same coupon and starting yield, a longer maturity means a bigger move. And for the same coupon and maturity, a higher starting yield means a smaller move. Fabozzi's example: a 25-year bond with a 9% coupon loses 10.30% of its value when yields rise 100 basis points from 7%, but only 6.75% when they rise the same amount from 14%. That third rule is why bond investors worried so much in the low-rate years after 2008: with yields near historic lows, prices were more sensitive than at any time in living memory.

Plain English

Why do long bonds swing more? Compare a 2-year and a 30-year bond, both paying 6% and both priced at $1,000 when the market wants 6%. Now the market wants 8%. The 2-year bond falls to about $964: you are stuck with a below-market coupon for only two more years. The 30-year bond falls to about $774: you are stuck with it for thirty years, and the market charges you for every one of them.

If the market wants 4% instead, the 2-year bond rises to about $1,038 and the 30-year to about $1,348. A two-point move in rates is a small event for a short bond and a big one for a long bond. The same logic explains the coupon rule: a low-coupon bond returns less of your money early, so more of its value is tied up far in the future, where changes in the discount rate bite hardest.

Not every bond has a fixed coupon. A floating-rate note pays a reference rate plus a quoted margin, with the coupon reset every quarter or half-year. Because the coupon keeps catching up with the market, a floater's price stays close to par as long as its margin matches the spread investors currently demand for that issuer; if the issuer's credit worsens and the required spread widens, the price falls below par even though the coupon floats. Many floaters also carry a cap, a maximum coupon, or a floor, a minimum, and those limits behave like options that come into play only when rates move far enough. For an investor, a floater swaps interest-rate risk for credit-spread risk: the rate moves are absorbed by the coupon, the credit worries are not.

The inverse floater turns that logic upside down. Its coupon is a fixed number minus a multiple of the reference rate, K − L × reference rate, so the coupon falls when rates rise. Dealers create inverse floaters by splitting an ordinary fixed-rate bond, the collateral, into two pieces: a floater whose coupon rises with rates and an inverse floater that gets whatever interest is left. Because the floater's price barely moves, the inverse floater absorbs the whole price change of the collateral, concentrated in a smaller piece. Its duration, Fabozzi shows, equals (1 + L) times the collateral's duration, where L is the leverage factor. Inverse floaters were behind some of the worst losses of the 1994 bond rout, including the Orange County bankruptcy.

Finally, the price you see quoted is not quite the price you pay. Coupons are paid every six months, but bonds change hands every day, and the seller is owed the interest that has built up since the last coupon date, the accrued interest. The quoted price is the clean price; the amount that actually changes hands is the dirty price (or full price), clean plus accrued. How the days are counted depends on the market: corporate and municipal bonds use 30/360, treating every month as 30 days; Treasuries use actual/actual; money-market instruments use actual/360. Quoting clean prices keeps the price chart from jumping every six months when a coupon is paid.

Plain English

Accrued interest is like settling the rent when you sell a house mid-month. A $1,000 corporate bond pays a 6% coupon, $30 every six months. You sell it 60 days after the last coupon, using the 30/360 count, so 60 of the period's 180 days have passed. The buyer will receive the full $30 at the next coupon date, but you held the bond for a third of the period. So the buyer pays you the quoted (clean) price plus $10 of accrued interest, and keeps the whole $30 when it arrives. Each side ends up with interest for exactly the days it owned the bond.

Chapter 3: Measuring Yield

A bond earns money in three ways, and every yield measure in this chapter is a different attempt to capture them. There are the coupon payments themselves. There is the capital gain or loss as the price moves toward par by maturity or toward wherever it is sold. And there is interest on interest, what the coupons earn after they are reinvested. The simpler measures leave out one or two of these, and the most popular measure includes all three only by making assumptions most investors never notice. Fabozzi's message is blunt: the conventional yield figures tell you little about what you will actually earn, and the honest alternative, total return, makes you state your assumptions out loud.

The simplest measure, current yield, is the annual coupon divided by the price. A bond paying $50 a year that costs $900 has a current yield of 5.56%. It is easy to compute and easy to understand, and it is incomplete. It ignores the $100 gain the buyer will collect when the bond matures at $1,000, and it ignores what the coupons earn after they are reinvested. For a premium bond the error runs the other way: current yield ignores the loss as the price drifts down to par. Current yield survives because income-oriented investors care about cash in hand, but as a measure of the return on a bond it misses the two other sources of return.

The standard measure is yield to maturity (YTM), the single discount rate that makes the present value of all remaining payments equal to today's price, the bond's internal rate of return. It includes all three sources of return, but only under two assumptions: that the investor holds the bond until maturity, and that every coupon is reinvested at the yield to maturity itself. Neither is usually true. Most bonds are sold before maturity at whatever price the market then offers, and coupons are reinvested at whatever rates exist when they arrive. So YTM is the return you earn only if rates stay put for the whole life of the bond, and Fabozzi calls it a promised yield rather than a realized one.

The reinvestment assumption matters more than intuition suggests. Take Fabozzi's 15-year bond with a 7% coupon, bought at $769.40 for a yield to maturity of 10%. To earn that 10%, the investor must end with $3,325.30, a total dollar return of $2,555.90. The coupons supply $1,050 of it and the climb to par supplies $230.60. The remaining $1,275.36, nearly half the total, must come from interest on interest, earned only if every coupon is reinvested at 10% for the rest of the bond's life. If rates fall and coupons earn 5%, the realized return falls well short of the advertised 10%. For long, high-coupon bonds, reinvestment can supply as much as 80% of the total dollar return.

So half of the "10% yield" printed on the screen is really a forecast that rates will stay at 10% for fifteen years. If they fall, the bond still pays every coupon on time, and you still earn less than 10%.

Two patterns follow. The longer the maturity, the more of the return depends on reinvestment, because coupons have longer to compound. The higher the coupon, the more of the return depends on reinvestment, because there is more cash to reinvest. At the other extreme sits the zero-coupon bond, which pays nothing until maturity and so has nothing to reinvest. It is the only bond whose yield to maturity is exactly the return the investor will earn if it is held to maturity and does not default. That property makes zeros the building blocks of the yield curve in Chapter 6 and the natural tool for an investor who must meet a known payment on a known date, a theme that returns in liability-driven investing in Chapter 27.

Bonds with call features need their own yield measures. Yield to call repeats the YTM calculation but uses the call date and call price in place of the maturity date and par. A bond with several possible call dates has a yield to first call and a yield to first par call; a bond with a sinking fund, which retires part of the issue each year, has a yield to sinker. The most conservative of all is yield to worst, the lowest of the yields computed to every call date, sinking-fund date and maturity. For a premium callable bond the worst case is usually an early call, since that cuts off the high coupon soonest, and yield to worst is the figure dealers quote. All of these measures inherit YTM's flaws, adding the assumption that the bond is actually called on the date chosen.

Fabozzi's answer is total return, or horizon analysis, which forces the investor to state a horizon, a reinvestment rate and a yield at the horizon. The method has five steps: add up the coupons plus the interest earned by reinvesting them at the assumed rate; project the bond's sale price at the horizon from the assumed yield; add the two to get total future dollars; compute the semiannual return as total future dollars divided by the purchase price, raised to the power one over the number of periods, minus one; and double it to annualize. In his example, a 20-year 8% bond bought at $828.40 and held three years, with coupons reinvested at 6% and a 7% yield at the end, earns a total return of 17.16% a year, far above its 10% yield to maturity, because the expected fall in yields lifts the sale price.

Plain English

The 17.16% example, step by step. You pay $828.40 for a 20-year bond that pays $40 every six months. You plan to sell in three years (six coupons) and you guess that coupons can be reinvested at 6% and that the market will want 7% from the bond when you sell.

Six coupons of $40 reinvested at 3% per half-year grow to $258.74. In three years the bond has 17 years left; priced at 7%, it sells for $1,098.51. Total future dollars: $258.74 + $1,098.51 = $1,357.25. Your money grew by a factor of $1,357.25 ÷ $828.40 = 1.638 over six half-years, which is 8.58% per half-year, or 17.16% a year. Change any guess, say a 9% yield at the end, and you can rerun the same five steps for that scenario. That is the point: instead of one number with hidden assumptions, you get a range of outcomes tied to views you can argue about.

Critics object that total return needs too many assumptions. Fabozzi's reply is that yield to maturity makes assumptions too, a reinvestment rate equal to the yield and a holding period equal to the bond's life; it simply hides them. Total return puts every assumption on the table, which allows scenario analysis: the same bond can be run under rising, falling and unchanged rates, steeper and flatter curves, and different reinvestment rates, and a manager can see which bonds hold up across the range rather than which one shows the highest number under a single unspoken forecast. Portfolio managers use exactly this approach to compare a bond they own with one they might buy, and the swap analysis of Part V is built on it.

The chapter ends with yield measures for special cases. A floater's discount margin is the spread over the reference rate that makes its projected cash flows worth today's price, computed by assuming the reference rate never changes, which also ignores any cap or floor. Mortgage securities use a cash flow yield, a monthly internal rate of return converted to a bond-equivalent basis, which adds a third shaky assumption: that prepayments arrive as projected. A portfolio yield is not the average of the bonds' yields but the single rate that values all the portfolio's cash flows at its market value. Treasury bills are quoted on a discount basis over 360 days and must be converted to compare with bonds. And yield changes can be measured in basis points or, for comparing very different rate levels, as relative (percentage) changes.

Chapter 4: Duration and Convexity

If one number could describe a bond's interest-rate risk, it would be duration, the approximate percentage change in price for a 100 basis point change in yield. A bond with a duration of 7 should lose about 7% of its value if yields rise one percentage point and gain about 7% if they fall one. The name causes endless confusion because duration is measured in years and began life as a measure of time, but Fabozzi insists that it be read as a measure of price sensitivity, and he gives a vivid reason.

“Duration is a price sensitivity measure, not a time measure. A CMO tranche can have duration of 40 while underlying mortgages are 30-year—making no sense as a time measure.” — Frank J. Fabozzi

The family has several members. Macaulay duration, devised by Frederick Macaulay in 1938, is the weighted average time until the bond's cash flows arrive, each payment weighted by its share of the bond's present value; for a zero-coupon bond it equals the maturity. Modified duration divides Macaulay duration by one plus the yield per period, and that small adjustment turns it into the measure that matters: the approximate percentage price change for a 100 basis point move. For coupon bonds, modified duration is always less than maturity, because coupons return part of the investor's money before the end. The bond market quotes modified duration almost everywhere, and when people say "duration" without a qualifier, this is usually what they mean.

Two dollar versions translate percentages into money. Dollar duration is modified duration times price times 0.01, the dollar change in price for a 100 basis point move. The price value of a basis point (PVBP), also called DV01, is the dollar change for a single basis point, dollar duration divided by 100. Traders think in DV01 because it adds up across positions: a desk can sum the DV01s of every bond it holds and know how much money it makes or loses for each basis point the market moves, and hedgers size their futures and swaps (Part VI) to cancel that number. A position with a DV01 of $5,000 gains or loses about $5,000 for every one-basis-point move in rates, $500,000 for a full percentage point.

Plain English

Duration as a rule of thumb. The 10-year bond with a 5% coupon from Chapter 2, priced at par, has a modified duration of about 7.79. That means: for each percentage point that yields rise, the price falls about 7.79%. On a $1 million holding, a one-basis-point rise costs about $779, and that $779 is the position's DV01.

The handy part is that you can now compare very different bonds on one scale. A 2-year bond might have a duration of 1.9 and a 30-year bond a duration of 15. Put $1 million in each and the 30-year position carries roughly eight times the rate risk, even though both cost the same. If you want to cut a portfolio's rate risk in half, you halve its duration; you don't need to know anything else about the bonds.

Duration follows the same rules as price volatility in Chapter 2. It is longer for longer maturities, longer for lower coupons, and longer when yields are low. A 25-year 9% bond has a modified duration of 11.21 when it yields 7% but only 7.21 when it yields 14%. Duration also moves in the bondholder's favor as yields change: when yields rise, duration shrinks, so each further rise does less damage; when yields fall, duration grows, so each further fall does more good. That self-correcting drift is the curvature of the price-yield line seen from another angle. And duration shortens with the passage of time for an option-free bond, since fewer distant cash flows remain.

Modified duration has a hidden assumption: that the bond's cash flows do not change when yields change. For a callable bond, a putable bond or a mortgage security, they do. Falling rates make a call likelier and prepayments faster, so the cash flows shrink just as yields fall. For these bonds Fabozzi uses effective duration, computed by repricing the bond with a valuation model at a yield a little higher and a little lower, letting the cash flows change in each case: (P− − P+) / (2 × P0 × Δy). The difference can be dramatic: a callable bond with a modified duration of 5 may have an effective duration of 3, and a leveraged mortgage security with a modified duration of 7 can have an effective duration of 50. Commercial software uses this formula for every bond because it works whether or not the bond has options.

The inverse floater shows why duration is not time. Split $100 million of collateral with a duration of 8 into an $80 million floater and a $20 million inverse floater with a leverage factor of 4. If rates rise 100 basis points, the collateral loses about 8%, or $8 million. The floater's coupon resets upward, so its price barely moves. The whole $8 million loss lands on the $20 million inverse floater, a 40% decline, so its duration is 40, exactly (1 + 4) × 8. That is longer than the 30-year maturity of the collateral behind it, an impossibility if duration measured time, and a perfectly sensible number if it measures how much the price moves. Investors who bought inverse floaters in 1993 as "short-maturity" bonds learned the difference in 1994.

Duration draws a straight line through a curve, so it gets small moves right and large moves wrong. Convexity adds the curvature: percentage price change ≈ −duration × Δy + ½ × convexity × (Δy)². Fabozzi's 25-year 6% bond has a modified duration of 10.62 and a convexity of 182.92. For a 200 basis point rise in yields, duration alone predicts a loss of 21.24%, a large overestimate. Adding the convexity term gives −17.58%, close to the actual −18.03%. Different vendors scale convexity differently, some building the one-half into the number, but the price estimate comes out the same once each formula is used as intended.

Adding convexity bends the guess to follow the real curve and lands within about a tenth of a point. For everyday moves, duration is enough; for big moves, you need both.

Positive convexity, which every option-free bond has, means price gains on falling yields exceed price losses on rising yields of the same size. Of two bonds with the same duration, the one with more convexity does better whichever way rates move, so in a volatile market investors pay up for it. Negative convexity is the reverse, and it belongs to callable bonds and mortgage securities. When yields fall below a callable bond's coupon, the likelihood of a call rises and the price stalls near the call price, so the upside is capped while the downside is not. Mortgage securities suffer the same way: falling rates speed up refinancing, returning principal at par just when the investor would most like to keep the bond. Whether convexity is worth its price depends on volatility; in calm markets, selling it by buying callable bonds can pay.

“The value of convexity depends on expected rate volatility. If investors expect small changes in rates, the convexity of a bond is of no value. If rates are expected to change by a large amount, convexity is very valuable.” — Frank J. Fabozzi

Real portfolios need more than one number. Portfolio duration is the market-value-weighted average of the holdings' durations: a portfolio 10% in a bond with duration 4, 40% at 7, 30% at 6 and 20% at 2 has a duration of 5.4, and each bond's contribution to duration, weight times duration, shows where the risk sits. But the average assumes every yield moves by the same amount. Spread duration measures sensitivity to the credit spread alone, which matters for floaters with almost no rate duration but plenty of spread risk. Key rate durations, Thomas Ho's measure at eleven points from three months to thirty years, show exposure to each part of the curve; they sum to the total. SEDUR and LEDUR, from Klaffky, Ma and Nozari, measure exposure to the short and long ends' slope.

Chapter 5: Interest Rate Theory

Every bond price is a discounted value, so the question of what sets the discount rate sits underneath the whole book. Fabozzi surveys three classical answers to why interest rates exist and what fixes their level, then turns to the modern questions that dominate today's markets: the split between real and nominal rates, the idea of a natural rate of interest, whether any asset is truly safe, how negative rates work, and what the long history of U.S. rates says about what counts as normal. None of the theories gives a formula for tomorrow's yields, but each explains a force a bond investor has to reckon with, and together they explain why rates move when central banks, savers, borrowers and inflation shift.

The oldest answer is time preference. John Rae, Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk and Irving Fisher argued that people value present goods more than future goods, so a lender must be paid to wait. Böhm-Bawerk, writing in 1889, gave three reasons. People expect to be richer later, so an extra dollar of future income means less to them. People systematically undervalue the future. And roundabout production, using time to build tools before producing, yields more in the end: a villager who spends weeks laying a pipe from a spring ends up with more water than one who walks to the spring every day. That extra productivity is what lets borrowers pay interest out of the gains from waiting.

Irving Fisher drew the strands together in his 1930 Theory of Interest, which he dedicated to Rae and Böhm-Bawerk. His "impatience and opportunity" theory treats the interest rate as an index of a community's preference for a dollar of present income over a dollar of future income. Impatience, the desire to consume now, pushes rates up; opportunity, the chance to invest productively, pushes them up too, because borrowers can afford to pay more when their projects earn more. A society of patient savers with few good projects has low rates; an impatient society full of promising ventures has high ones. The theory explains the long-run level of real rates better than their day-to-day swings, which is where the next two theories come in.

The loanable funds theory, built by Dennis Robertson and Bertil Ohlin on Knut Wicksell's work, treats the interest rate as the price that clears the market for borrowed money. Demand for funds comes from businesses investing, households borrowing and governments running deficits; all but the government borrow less when rates are high. Supply comes from savings, from banks and other intermediaries, from government surpluses and from changes in the amount of cash people hold. When demand for loans rises faster than savings, as in an investment boom or a surge in government borrowing, rates rise; when savings pile up with nowhere to go, rates fall. The theory is the natural way to think about the effect of large budget deficits on bond yields.

John Maynard Keynes offered a different lens in 1936. In his liquidity preference theory, the interest rate is the price of giving up liquidity, set by the supply of money and the public's desire to hold it rather than bonds. People hold money for three motives: transactions (day-to-day spending), precaution (a cushion for emergencies) and speculation (waiting to buy bonds more cheaply if rates rise). An increase in the money supply then works through three effects. The liquidity effect pushes rates down at once. The income effect pushes them back up as spending and incomes rise. The price-expectations effect pushes them up further if people come to expect more inflation.

Plain English

The three effects explain a puzzle that comes up again and again: a central bank prints money to lower rates, and a year later rates are higher than when it started.

Week one: the central bank buys bonds and floods banks with cash. Everyone has more money than they want to hold, so they buy bonds, and yields fall. That is the liquidity effect, and it is quick. Months later the cheap money has done its job: businesses borrow and build, people spend, incomes rise, and everyone needs more money for everyday spending, so they sell bonds to get it, and yields drift back up. That is the income effect. If, on top of that, people start to expect higher inflation, lenders demand more to make up for it and yields rise further. That is the price-expectations effect. Whether easy money leaves rates lower or higher in the end depends on which of the slow effects wins.

Fisher also gave bond investors the most useful formula in the chapter, Fisher's law: the nominal interest rate roughly equals the real rate plus expected inflation. Since 1997 the U.S. Treasury has issued TIPS, Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, whose principal rises with the consumer price index, so their yield is a market-quoted real rate. The gap between an ordinary Treasury yield and a TIPS yield of the same maturity is the market's breakeven inflation rate: if the 10-year Treasury yields 1.90% and the 10-year TIPS 0.80%, investors expect inflation of about 1.10% a year. Wicksell's natural rate is the real short rate consistent with full employment and stable inflation; estimates have trended down for decades in the United States, Canada, the euro area and the United Kingdom.

Plain English

Breakeven inflation is a bet you can read off two prices. Using the numbers above, $10,000 in the regular 10-year Treasury earns 1.90% a year, fixed. The same $10,000 in TIPS earns 0.80% a year plus whatever inflation turns out to be, because its principal is marked up with the consumer price index.

If inflation averages exactly 1.10%, the two come out even. If it averages 3%, the TIPS holder earns about 3.8% a year and the regular Treasury holder still earns 1.90%, which after 3% inflation is a loss in real terms. If inflation averages 0.5%, the regular Treasury wins. So 1.10% is the market's price for inflation, and every investor choosing between the two is quietly saying whether they think inflation will come in above or below it.

The idea of a risk-free rate rests on the idea of a safe asset, and Fabozzi treats it as an open controversy. Safe assets do four jobs: they satisfy regulatory requirements for banks and insurers, serve as pricing benchmarks, act as collateral in lending, and anchor the theory used to price every other asset and derivative. Gary Gorton defines a safe asset as one that can be valued at face value without expensive and prolonged analysis. Yet the long history of sovereign defaults and inflations makes the notion shaky, as a former Treasury official put it.

“The idea of risk-free sovereign bonds is best thought of as an oxymoron or as an anomaly of recent history. It is not a useful, necessary or an enduring feature of the financial landscape.” — Peter Fisher, former U.S. Treasury undersecretary

Safe assets also shape the level of rates. A study by Ferreira and Shousha covering 1970 to 2018 found that the net supply of sovereign safe assets from eleven advanced economies explains about 20% of the variation in natural interest rates. Since the 1990s, central banks in emerging markets have piled up Treasuries and other sovereign bonds as reserves, soaking up supply; the study estimates this pushed natural rates down by about 50 basis points. Gorton and Ricardo Caballero describe a wider global shortage of safe assets, as the world's appetite for them grew faster than the supply of reliable government debt. When the scarce asset is in heavy demand, its yield falls, and with it the floor under every other rate in the economy.

When deflation threatens and rates are already at zero, some central banks have gone below it. Negative rates mean depositors pay to keep money in the bank. The case for them: lower borrowing costs, a weaker currency that helps exporters, and a penalty that pushes banks to lend reserves rather than park them. The case against: savers are taxed to help debtors, investors are pushed into speculative assets, banks lend to weaker borrowers for too little reward, banks' interest margins are squeezed, and pension funds and life insurers with long promises to keep are badly hurt. The European Central Bank went negative in 2014, followed by Denmark, Sweden, Switzerland and the Bank of Japan in January 2016; by September 2019 about a quarter of developed-market investment-grade bonds yielded less than zero, and critics argue the policy failed to deliver the hoped-for growth.

Plain English

What does a negative yield actually mean for a bond buyer? A one-year government bond that pays back $100 and has no coupon, priced to yield −0.5%, costs about $100.50 today. You hand over $100.50 and get $100 back a year later: you pay the government 50 cents to hold your money.

Why would anyone do that? Because the alternatives can be worse. A bank may charge more than that on a large deposit, cash in a vault costs money to store and insure, and an investor who expects deflation, or expects yields to fall further, can still profit from the bond rising in price. Big institutions that must hold safe assets by rule simply have no choice.

History puts all of this in perspective. From 1870 to 1978, long-term U.S. yields mostly stayed between 2% and 7%. Then came the Volcker shock: to break double-digit inflation, Fed chairman Paul Volcker pushed the federal funds rate above 20%, and 30-year Treasuries yielded more than 14%. Two recessions followed, six months in 1980 and sixteen months in 1981–82, but inflation fell from 13.3% in 1979 to 3.9% in 1982, and long-term corporate bonds, which lost 4.18% in 1979, began a four-decade rally. After 2008 rates sank to near zero, and when that was not enough the Fed bought long-term bonds through quantitative easing, swelling its balance sheet to $4.5 trillion; by July 2020 the 10-year Treasury yielded 0.62%. Over long periods, Fabozzi notes, rates tend to revert toward their mean.

Chapter 6: Term Structure of Interest Rates

The yield curve plots the yields of bonds with the same credit quality against their maturities, and for U.S. investors the Treasury curve is the benchmark against which everything else is priced. It usually slopes upward, with longer maturities yielding more, but it can be flat, humped or inverted, with short rates above long ones. Fabozzi uses the chapter to make a deeper point: the yield curve that appears in newspapers, built from coupon-paying bonds, is the wrong tool for valuing bonds. The right tool is the curve of spot rates, and from it come the forward rates that tell an investor what can be locked in today for borrowing or lending in the future.

The problem with the ordinary curve is that a coupon bond is really a bundle of separate payments, each due on a different date. A 10-year Treasury pays twenty coupons and a final principal, and each of those twenty-one payments should be discounted at the rate for its own date, not at one average yield for the whole bond. The rate for a single payment on a single date is the yield of a zero-coupon bond maturing then, the spot rate. Two Treasuries with the same maturity but different coupons will therefore have slightly different yields to maturity, even though the market is pricing every individual payment at the same spot rates. The spot rate curve is the clean version: one rate for each date, free of coupon effects.

Spot rates are found by bootstrapping, working out from the short end one maturity at a time. Start with Treasury bills, which are already zero-coupon, to get the 6-month and 1-year spot rates: say 5.25% and 5.50%. Then take a 1.5-year Treasury priced at par with a 5.75% coupon, so it pays $2.875 at six months, $2.875 at one year and $102.875 at eighteen months. The first two payments are discounted at the known spot rates; the only unknown is the rate for the third payment, and solving 100 = 2.875/1.02625 + 2.875/1.0275² + 102.875/(1 + z/2)³ gives a 1.5-year spot rate of 5.76%. Repeat with each longer bond and the curve builds itself out to thirty years.

Arbitrage keeps the spot curve honest. The Treasury's STRIPS program lets dealers separate a coupon bond into its individual payments and sell each as a zero, or put the zeros back together into the original bond. If a Treasury were priced below the sum of its payments valued at spot rates, dealers would buy it, strip it and sell the pieces at a profit; if it were priced above, they would buy the pieces and rebuild the bond. That trade, done constantly by government bond dealers, forces coupon bond prices into line with the spot curve. It also explains why valuing a bond with a single yield can produce a price that the market will not pay, a point that becomes central in the valuation models of Chapters 19 and 20.

Plain English

Think of a coupon bond as a fruit basket. A 10-year Treasury is a basket of twenty-one separate IOUs: one due in six months, one in a year, and so on, with the big one at the end. Each IOU has its own fair price, set by the going rate for money borrowed for exactly that long. The basket is worth the sum of its pieces.

If a store sold the basket for less than the fruit inside, people would buy baskets, take them apart and sell the fruit. If it charged more, people would buy the fruit and assemble their own baskets. Treasury dealers do exactly that with STRIPS, so the price of every Treasury is held to the sum of its payments valued at spot rates. Bootstrapping just works backward from the basket prices to find what each piece of fruit must cost.

Spot rates imply forward rates, the rates for borrowing or lending over a future period that can be locked in today. If the 1-year spot rate is 4% and the 2-year spot rate is 5%, an investor can either buy a 2-year zero or buy a 1-year zero and roll the proceeds into another one-year bond next year. The two plans give the same result only if next year's 1-year rate is about 6.01%, and that break-even rate is the 1-year rate one year forward. The essential point, Fabozzi stresses, is that forward rates are hedgeable rates, not forecasts. They are what can be locked in with today's bonds, and the evidence is that they are poor predictors of the rates that actually arrive.

Plain English

The 6.01% with real money. You have $100 to invest for two years. Plan A: buy a 2-year zero at 5% a year. In two years you have $100 × 1.05 × 1.05 = $110.25. Plan B: buy a 1-year zero at 4%, giving $104 in a year, then reinvest for another year at whatever the rate is then.

To end with the same $110.25, the second year must turn $104 into $110.25, a return of 6.01%. So today's prices contain a 6.01% rate for next year, and you can lock it in by choosing Plan A over Plan B. That's all a forward rate is. It is not a prediction that rates will be 6.01% next year; it is the rate at which you'd be indifferent between the two plans. Most of the time the rate that actually shows up next year is lower, because longer bonds also pay a premium for tying up money longer.

Why does the curve usually slope upward? The pure expectations theory says forward rates equal the market's expected future spot rates, so an upward slope means the market expects rates to rise; the evidence rejects it, because forward rates consistently overshoot the rates that follow. The liquidity theory adds a term premium: investors demand extra yield for tying up money longer and bearing more price risk, so forward rates equal expected rates plus that premium, which explains why the curve usually slopes up even when no rise is expected. The preferred habitat theory says investors prefer particular maturities and must be paid to leave them, so supply and demand at each maturity shape the curve. Market segmentation, its extreme version, says each maturity is a separate market; it is now largely rejected.

Yields on other bonds are quoted as a spread over the Treasury of similar maturity, the benchmark spread, and Fabozzi lists seven things that drive it. The type of issuer matters, and so does its perceived creditworthiness. Embedded options move it: a call widens the spread, a put narrows it. Taxability matters, which is why tax-exempt municipal bonds yield less than Treasuries. Expected liquidity matters, and so does financeability, how cheaply the bond can be borrowed against in the repo market. Term to maturity is the seventh. Even among Treasuries, the newest issue at each maturity, the on-the-run bond, yields a little less than older off-the-run issues because it is more liquid and cheaper to finance.

The Treasury curve is not the only benchmark. The swap curve, built from the fixed rates on interest-rate swaps (Chapter 31), covers more maturities and reflects bank credit, and many investors prefer it; the swap spread is the swap rate minus the Treasury yield of the same maturity. For decades the floating side of those swaps was LIBOR, a forward-looking unsecured rate set from bank estimates, used in more than $200 trillion of contracts. After the LIBOR rate-rigging scandal regulators moved the market to SOFR, the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, an overnight, backward-looking rate based on actual Treasury repo trades. Because SOFR carries no bank credit risk, it runs below LIBOR, and contracts switching over need a spread adjustment to keep their value.

The curve's shape shifts constantly with monetary policy, inflation expectations, supply and demand, and flights to safety. A normal curve slopes upward; an inverted curve, with short rates above long rates, has preceded most U.S. recessions; a flat curve is often a stage between the two; a humped curve, peaking at middle maturities, is rare. Frank Jones's study of Treasury returns from 1979 to 1990 found that parallel shifts and twists (changes in slope) together explained 91.6% of returns, butterfly shifts (changes in curvature) 3.4%, and the remaining 5% went unexplained; the moves also come bundled, a downward shift usually arriving with a steeper and more humped curve. The non-parallel part sounds small, but for a large portfolio it can mean large gains or losses, which is why yield curve risk is often the biggest unmanaged risk in a bond portfolio and why key rate durations exist.

Chapter 7: Treasury & Agency Securities

The U.S. Treasury is the largest single issuer of debt in the world, and its securities are the benchmark for every other dollar bond. Treasury bills mature in a year or less and pay no coupon; they are sold at a discount and repaid at par. Treasury notes mature in two to ten years and Treasury bonds in more than ten, both paying semiannual coupons. TIPS protect against inflation by adjusting their principal, floating-rate notes reset with the 13-week bill rate, and STRIPS are zero-coupon securities created by separating the payments of notes and bonds. Because Treasuries are backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. government, their yields are the base rate to which every other bond adds a spread.

Treasuries are sold at regular auctions. Bidders submit either a competitive bid, stating the yield they will accept, or a noncompetitive bid, agreeing to take whatever yield the auction produces, up to $5 million per bidder. Since 1998 every auction has been a single-price, or Dutch, auction: the Treasury fills noncompetitive bids first, then accepts competitive bids from the lowest yield upward until the issue is sold, and every winner pays the same price, set by the highest accepted yield. About two dozen primary dealers must bid meaningfully in every auction and make markets afterward. Trading begins before the auction even happens, in the when-issued market, which helps the Treasury discover the price it will get.

Plain English

A single-price auction with small numbers. The Treasury is selling $100 of bills. Small investors put in $10 of noncompetitive bids, so $90 is left for the competitive bidders. Their bids, from the lowest yield up: $30 at 4.00%, $40 at 4.02%, $30 at 4.03%, and $20 at 4.05%. Filling from the lowest yield, the first three bids add up to $100, more than the $90 needed, so the cut-off is 4.03% and the last bidder at that yield gets only part of what it asked for.

Everyone who wins, including the bidder who would have accepted 4.00% and the small investors, gets 4.03%. The bidder at 4.05% gets nothing. Paying everyone the same yield encourages bidders to bid what they really think the bills are worth, since bidding low no longer means paying more than everyone else.

Treasury bills are quoted in an old-fashioned way. Their price is stated as a discount yield: the discount from face value as a percentage of face value, scaled to a 360-day year. A 90-day bill selling for $9,900 per $10,000 has a $100 discount, and ($100 / $10,000) × (360 / 90) = 4.00%. That figure understates the true return twice over: the investor earns $100 on the $9,900 actually paid, not on $10,000, and a year has 365 days, not 360. Restated as a bond-equivalent yield, ($100 / $9,900) × (365 / 90), the bill yields about 4.10%, the number to compare with notes and bonds. The difference looks small but matters to anyone choosing between a bill and a short note.

The STRIPS program, launched in February 1985, lets any Treasury note or bond be split into its separate payments. A $500 million 10-year note with a 5% coupon becomes twenty coupon strips of $12.5 million each, one for every semiannual payment date, plus a $500 million principal strip due at maturity. Coupon strips due on the same date are interchangeable and share an identifier, whatever bond they came from; principal strips keep a separate identity. Some foreign buyers prefer principal strips because their home countries tax the gain as a capital gain rather than interest. Strips can also be put back together, which is the arbitrage described in Chapter 6: if a coupon bond is cheap relative to its pieces, dealers strip it; if it is rich, they reconstitute it.

TIPS work by adjusting their principal. At each payment the original principal is multiplied by an index ratio, the current consumer price index (CPI-U) divided by its level when the bond was issued. The coupon rate never changes, but it is applied to the adjusted principal, so the dollar coupons grow with inflation. At maturity the investor receives the larger of the adjusted principal or the original par, so a stretch of deflation cannot cut the principal below where it started. Because a TIPS yield is a real yield, the gap between it and a regular Treasury of the same maturity measures the inflation the market expects, the breakeven rate of Chapter 5.

Plain English

A TIPS bond's money grows with prices. Buy $1,000 of TIPS with a 1% coupon. Over the next year prices rise 3%, so the principal becomes $1,030. Your 1% coupon is now paid on $1,030: $10.30 a year instead of $10. If prices rise another 3% the following year, the principal moves to about $1,061 and the coupon rises again.

At maturity you get back the inflated principal, so what you lent buys roughly the same amount of goods as when you lent it. A regular Treasury pays back exactly $1,000 however much prices have risen. That's why TIPS pay a much lower stated yield: the inflation protection is part of what you're buying.

Primer

The next paragraph is about repo, the way dealers and funds borrow against bonds. The idea is a pawnshop for securities. You hand over a bond, receive cash for it, and agree to buy the bond back later at a slightly higher price; the difference is the interest. The lender holds the bond in the meantime, so if you don't come back, it simply keeps it. Because the loan is backed by safe collateral, it is cheap, which is why almost every large bond position in the world is financed this way.

Behind the Treasury market sits the repo market, where portfolio managers and dealers finance their holdings. A repurchase agreement is a collateralized loan dressed as a sale: the borrower sells Treasuries and agrees to buy them back at a slightly higher price, the difference being the interest at the repo rate. Borrowing $10 million overnight at 2% costs about $556. The lender protects itself with a haircut, lending perhaps 98% of the collateral's value, and the haircut grows with the collateral's risk and maturity. In a tri-party repo, a custodian bank handles the collateral and daily revaluation. When a particular bond is in heavy demand, perhaps to cover short sales, it goes special, and its owner can borrow against it at a rate well below the general repo rate.

The repo market is the plumbing of the financial system. More than $4 trillion changes hands in it every day, and a blockage spreads quickly: in September 2019 overnight repo rates spiked to 10% when cash grew scarce, and the Federal Reserve had to step in. The Fed also uses repo as a policy tool, its reverse repo facility setting a floor under short-term rates. Repo rates normally sit below the federal funds rate because repo is secured and fed funds are not. The special rate on popular issues is part of why on-the-run Treasuries trade at slightly lower yields than older ones: owning the newest bond carries a financing advantage as well as better liquidity.

The other half of the chapter covers federal agencies and government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs). The eleven Federal Home Loan Banks issue about $1 trillion of consolidated obligations, on which each bank is liable for the others' debts. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the mortgage giants, have been in government conservatorship since September 2008, supported by agreements with the Treasury but without an explicit guarantee; the market prices their debt as if the government stands behind it, a too-big-to-fail assumption that investors relied on in 2008. The Farm Credit System lends to agriculture; the Tennessee Valley Authority, the largest public power system, funds itself from operations and debt; and Farmer Mac provides a secondary market for farm loans. Agency debt trades at a spread over Treasuries that blew out during the 2008 crisis.

Chapter 8: Corporate Debt Instruments

When a company fails, its creditors are paid in an order set by law and by their contracts, and that order shapes every corporate bond's risk and yield. Secured debt, backed by specific collateral such as property, equipment or receivables, comes first on the pledged assets. Senior unsecured bonds, called debentures, have no collateral but rank ahead of other unsecured claims. Senior subordinated and subordinated bonds agree in their contracts to stand behind the senior debt. Preferred stock comes next, and common shareholders, who own whatever is left, come last. The further down the ladder a bond sits, the less it recovers in a default and the more yield it must offer.

U.S. bankruptcy comes in two forms. Under Chapter 7, the company is liquidated and its assets are paid out in strict order of priority, the absolute priority rule. Under Chapter 11, it reorganizes and keeps operating. Within days of filing it usually obtains debtor-in-possession (DIP) financing, new loans that rank ahead even of existing secured creditors because they keep the business alive. The company can reject leases and other contracts and sell assets free of liens with court approval. A reorganization plan needs the approval of each class of creditors, two-thirds by amount and half by number, and the court can impose it on a dissenting class through a cram-down. In practice, the final split depends on bargaining power, and absolute priority is often bent.

Most corporate bonds can be retired early. A traditional call follows a schedule of declining premiums, for example callable first at 103 and stepping down to par over five years. A make-whole call instead pays the present value of the remaining payments discounted at a Treasury yield plus a small spread, so the investor loses little: a 10-year 6% bond called after six years, when the 4-year Treasury yields 3% and the make-whole spread is 50 basis points, is redeemed at $109.26. A sinking fund requires part of the issue to be retired each year, by lottery at par or by open-market purchases. And call protection is not the same as refunding protection: a nonrefundable bond cannot be retired with money raised from cheaper new debt but can still be called with cash from asset sales or new equity.

Plain English

The make-whole call is designed so the issuer has no reason to call just because rates fell. In the example, your bond still has four years of 6% coupons left. The formula values those remaining payments as if they were a Treasury plus half a point: discounted at 3.5%, they are worth $109.26 per $100. So the company must pay you $109.26 to take the bond away, roughly what you'd need to buy a similar stream of safe payments today.

Compare an old-style call at 101: if rates fall, the company could take your bond for $101 while it's worth $109 to you, keeping the $8 difference. Under a make-whole, that gift disappears, so companies use the call mainly for reasons like a merger or a change in plans, not to refinance cheaply at your expense.

Corporate bonds trade very differently from stocks, and the gap has widened. Since 2007, tougher capital rules under Basel III and the Volcker Rule have cut dealers' bond inventories by about 80%, while corporate issuance grew about 50% as companies locked in low rates. Data from TRACE, the reporting system for bond trades, showed that of 37,000 eligible issues in 2012, 38% never traded at all, 23% traded only a few times, and just 1% traded every day. The average stock trade was about $7,000, the average investment-grade bond trade about $500,000. Electronic platforms such as MarketAxess and Tradeweb have filled part of the gap with all-to-all trading, in which buy-side firms now supply about half the liquidity.

Companies borrow in other shapes too. Medium-term notes are sold continuously off a shelf registration in whatever amounts and maturities investors ask for, often through reverse inquiry, and despite the name range from nine months to a hundred years (Walt Disney sold a 100-year note in 1993). Structured notes combine a note with a derivative to give the investor exposure to rates, stocks, commodities, credit or currencies. Commercial paper is short-term unsecured borrowing, usually under 30 days and never over 270, sold at a discount. Its weak spot is rollover risk: issuers repay old paper by selling new paper, and when the $1.8 trillion market froze after Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008, they lost their funding overnight. Backup bank lines exist for exactly that day.

Collateralized loan obligations (CLOs) pool leveraged loans, floating-rate loans to companies with junk ratings, and fund them with layers of bonds. In Fabozzi's example, the AAA-rated Class A tranche is 80% of the deal and paid first; Class B (A-rated, 8%), Class C (BBB, 3%) and Class D (BB, 2%) follow; the 7% equity tranche at the bottom absorbs the first losses and keeps whatever cash is left, aiming for 12–15% returns. Coverage tests on overcollateralization and interest coverage protect the senior tranches: if the loans deteriorate and a test fails, cash that would have gone to the junior tranches is diverted to pay down the senior ones. Leveraged loans recover about 70% in default, high-yield bonds about 40%.

Plain English

A CLO is a layer cake built from loans, and losses eat it from the bottom up. Picture $100 of loans funding the five layers above. If $5 of the loans are lost, the $7 equity layer absorbs all of it and every bond above it is untouched. If $10 is lost, the $7 of equity is wiped out, the $2 of Class D above it is gone too, and Class C loses $1 of its $3. The AAA layer would only lose money if more than $20 of the $100, a fifth of the whole pool, were lost.

That is why the same pool of risky loans can produce a AAA bond: the top layer is protected by everything beneath it. The coverage tests add a second line of defense, cutting off cash to the lower layers as soon as the loans start to sour, before the losses actually reach the top.

How often do corporate bonds default? From 1981 to 2012 the average annual default rate was about 1.5% for all corporates, 4.26% for high-yield and just 0.11% for investment grade. Defaults cluster in recessions, reaching about 11% of high-yield issuers in 1990–91 and 13% in 2009, and they peak about three years after a bond is issued, the aging effect. What matters for returns is the default loss rate, the default rate times one minus the recovery rate, and recessions hurt twice: more companies default just when recoveries are lowest, 42% in 2008 against 70% in 2013. A portfolio with a 4% default rate and 40% recovery loses about 2.4% a year, which a high-yield spread must more than cover.

High-yield bonds come in two kinds: original-issue junk, rated below investment grade from the start, and fallen angels, downgraded after an LBO, a recapitalization or a business decline. The 1980s buyout boom added deferred-coupon structures, step-ups and PIK bonds, to give over-borrowed companies breathing room. Ratings themselves move, and rating transition matrices show how: of Aa-rated bonds, about 91.4% are still Aa a year later, while downgrades outnumber upgrades. Event risk can move a rating overnight. In the 1988 RJR Nabisco buyout, a $25 billion deal, Moody's cut the company from A1 to B3 and its spreads jumped from 100 to 350 basis points, teaching the whole investment-grade market that almost any company could be the next target.

Chapter 9: Municipal Securities

Primer

Municipal bonds are all about taxes, so one term matters here. Your marginal tax rate is the rate you pay on your last, or next, dollar of income: if you're in the 35% bracket, an extra $100 of taxable interest leaves you $65. It is the rate that decides whether tax-free interest is worth more to you than taxable interest, because the question is always what the next dollar of interest keeps after tax. A general obligation means a bond backed by a government's power to tax; a revenue bond is backed only by the money a specific project brings in.

States, cities, counties and their agencies borrow in the municipal bond market, and the security behind each bond matters as much as the issuer's name. An unlimited-tax general obligation bond is backed by the issuer's full faith and credit and its power to raise income, sales and property taxes without limit, the strongest pledge. A limited-tax GO is capped by law. Revenue bonds are repaid only from a project's income, a toll road, airport, hospital or water system, under a trust indenture. Double-barreled bonds combine both sources. Moral obligation bonds carry a state's promise, but not its legal duty, to refill a reserve fund, which a legislature must approve each year. Dedicated tax bonds are paid from a specific tax.

Revenue bonds follow a strict flow of funds. Every dollar the project earns goes into a revenue fund and is paid out in order: first to an operation and maintenance fund, because a bridge that isn't maintained earns nothing; then to the sinking fund that pays bondholders; then to a debt service reserve fund held against future shortfalls; then to renewal and replacement and to reserves for extraordinary repairs; and finally to a surplus fund. Bondholders therefore have a net revenue pledge, paid after operating costs. Two covenants protect them: a rate covenant requires the issuer to charge enough to cover costs and debt service, and an additional bonds test limits new borrowing against the same revenues. Each sector, from airports to student loans, has its own risks.

Revenue bonds are only as good as the enterprise behind them, so their analysis looks like a business analysis. Airport bonds depend on passenger traffic and on the contracts with airlines that use the airport. Toll road and gas-tax bonds depend on traffic counts and on the existence of free alternatives. Hospital bonds depend on occupancy, on the mix of patients, and on reimbursement rules set by Medicare, Medicaid and private insurers. Water and sewer bonds are among the safest, since households pay for water before almost anything else. Higher-education bonds turn on enrollment and endowments, public power bonds on electricity demand and fuel costs, and industrial development bonds on the private company that actually uses the facility. In each case the question is whether the project will earn enough, after operating costs, to cover the debt.

The defining feature of most municipal bonds is that their interest is exempt from federal income tax, so they yield less than taxable bonds of similar quality. The comparison is made with the tax-equivalent yield: the tax-exempt yield divided by one minus the investor's marginal tax rate. A 3.4% tax-free yield is worth 5.23% taxable to an investor in the 35% bracket and 6.18% to one in the 45% bracket, so the exemption is worth most to the highest earners. Bonds issued in high-tax states such as New York and California are often triple tax-exempt for residents, free of federal, state and local tax, which pushes their yields lower still. Because of the exemption, municipal spreads are quoted against a generic AAA general obligation curve rather than Treasuries.

Plain English

Should you buy a 3.4% tax-free muni or a taxable corporate bond? It depends on your bracket, because what matters is what you keep.

A 45% taxpayer who earns 6.18% on a taxable bond pays 2.78 points in tax and keeps 3.40%, the same as the muni. So for that investor, a muni at 3.4% beats any taxable bond of similar quality yielding less than 6.18%. For someone in a low bracket, the muni's lower yield is mostly a loss, which is why munis belong in the accounts of high earners and almost never in tax-sheltered retirement accounts, where the tax break is wasted.

Municipal credit can be strengthened from outside. Before 2007, about half of new municipal bonds carried bond insurance from monoline insurers, which promised to pay if the issuer did not. When those insurers were downgraded for guaranteeing subprime mortgage securities, insured munis lost most of their extra value and the practice shrank sharply. Banks also back munis through letters of credit, the strongest being a direct-pay letter that the trustee draws on without first asking the issuer. Pre-refunded bonds are the safest munis of all: the issuer has placed Treasuries in escrow whose cash flows match every remaining payment, so the bonds effectively carry Treasury credit. Issuers refund to escape restrictive covenants, reshape their maturities or lock in lower rates.

Municipal defaults are rarer than corporate defaults but far more common than the headline numbers suggest. Moody's counted only 113 defaults among rated issuers over nearly 50 years, a cumulative rate of about 0.1%, but that excludes the many unrated bonds. Broader studies found 2,521 defaults from 1970 to 2011 and 2,563 among general-purpose local governments in 2009–2015 alone. Causes include recessions, revenue projects whose feasibility studies proved too rosy, fraud and mismanagement, and, occasionally, a simple unwillingness to pay. The largest bankruptcies are Detroit (2013, $18.5 billion), Jefferson County, Alabama (2011, $3.2 billion), Orange County (1994, $1.7 billion), San Bernardino (2012) and Stockton (2012).

Municipal bankruptcy happens under Chapter 9 of the Bankruptcy Code, which has no liquidation option, since a court cannot sell off a sovereign state's city without violating the Tenth Amendment; the court's role is limited to deciding eligibility, approving a plan and overseeing it. A growing cause of distress is pension obligations: nearly half of Detroit's $18.2 billion of long-term debt was unfunded retirement benefits. Investors also tend to overreact to municipal trouble, treating one issuer's failure as a threat to all, a pattern a fund manager captured well.

“When General Motors and American Airlines filed for bankruptcy, nobody invested in corporate bonds was worrying what that meant for their Procter & Gamble and Walmart bonds. But whenever there is an isolated problem with a municipal bond issuer, there’s an overreaction that this means the entire market is in trouble.” — James Evans, Eaton Vance

Two special corners round out the chapter. In 1998 the four largest tobacco companies agreed to pay 46 states $206 billion over 25 years, and many states sold tobacco settlement bonds backed by those payments, starting with New York City's $709 million deal in November 1999; their credit rests on cigarette sales, not on the state. Municipal variable-rate debt comes as variable-rate demand obligations, which reset daily or weekly and can be put back at par on seven days' notice, as floating-rate notes, and as auction-rate securities. Dealers also create municipal floater and inverse-floater pairs by placing a fixed-rate bond in a trust; with a 50/50 split, the inverse floater has twice the duration of the bond underneath. Build America Bonds, from 2009, were taxable munis with a federal subsidy to issuers.

Chapter 10: International Bonds

Primer

This chapter assumes you're comfortable with exchange rates and currency forwards. An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another: if one euro costs $1.10 and later $1.00, the euro has fallen about 9% against the dollar. A forward contract fixes today the rate at which you will swap currencies on a future date. A U.S. investor who will receive €1,000 in a year can sell those euros forward now and know exactly how many dollars will arrive, whatever happens to the euro in the meantime. That is what "hedging the currency" means below.

Global debt securities total about $119.7 trillion, of which $94.5 trillion is domestic and $25.2 trillion international. The dollar dominates, at $50.6 trillion, followed by the euro at $20.4 trillion and the yen at $12.8 trillion. Fabozzi divides the international market into three kinds of bond. A foreign bond is issued in another country's domestic market, in that country's currency, by a foreign borrower, and the markets have nicknames: Yankee bonds in the United States, Samurai in Japan, Bulldog in Britain, Kangaroo in Australia, Maple in Canada, Matador in Spain. A Eurobond is sold outside the jurisdiction of any single country, through an international syndicate, to investors in several countries at once. A global bond trades in several domestic and Euro markets simultaneously.

Fabozzi also sorts countries by how developed their economies and capital markets are. Developed markets, such as the United States, Japan, Germany and Britain, have high incomes, slower growth and deep, liquid markets with strong regulators. Emerging markets, led by China and India, are growing faster but cannot fund their governments at home, because their local capital markets are too small or their savers unwilling to lend. Frontier markets, 29 countries from Argentina and Kenya to Vietnam, are earlier still, and their lack of transparency makes even pricing their bonds uncertain. The emerging-market category is defined less by credit quality than by one shared weakness, which the book puts plainly.

“Emerging market issuers rely on international investors for capital. Emerging markets cannot finance their fiscal deficits domestically because domestic capital markets are poorly developed and local investors are unable or unwilling to lend to the government. Although emerging market issuers differ greatly in terms of credit risk, dependence on foreign capital is the most basic characteristic of the asset class.” — Frank J. Fabozzi

Eurobonds have their own conventions. Because their investors are spread across legal systems, they find it hard to agree on protective clauses, so Eurobond covenants are weaker than those on U.S. corporate bonds. They pay coupons once a year rather than twice, a habit from the days when mailing coupons across borders was expensive. That means their yields must be converted before they can be compared with U.S. bonds: the bond-equivalent yield of a Eurodollar bond is 2 × [(1 + annual yield)1/2 − 1], always a little less than the annual yield. A Eurobond yielding 6% annually is equivalent to 5.91% on a U.S. semiannual basis, since a U.S. bond paying 5.91% in two halves compounds to the same 6% over a year.

The typical investment-grade Eurobond is governed by English law, unsecured and senior, and its few protections are standardized. A negative pledge stops the issuer from pledging assets to other lenders unless the Eurobond holders get the same security. A cross-default clause makes the bonds due at once if the issuer defaults on other borrowings above a small threshold, so holders are not left behind while other creditors move first. Most documents also restrict the sale of material assets or subsidiaries, though what counts as material varies. Alongside Eurobonds, non-U.S. companies raise dollars through Rule 144A private placements, which skip SEC registration and can be traded only among large qualified institutions, and through Euro medium-term notes, the offshore version of the MTN programs in Chapter 8.

Covered bonds are the backbone of European bank funding. They began with the German Pfandbriefe of 1769 and give the investor dual recourse: a claim on the issuing bank plus a claim on a segregated cover pool of high-quality mortgages or public-sector loans. If the bank fails, the pool is ring-fenced from its bankruptcy and keeps paying the bondholders, and the bank must keep topping up the pool with good assets while it is alive. Fabozzi notes that no covered bond has defaulted in more than 250 years, which is why they trade at tight spreads. They differ from the mortgage-backed securities of Part III in that the loans stay on the bank's balance sheet and the investor still has a claim on the bank itself.

For a U.S. investor, a bond paid in another currency has three sources of return: the coupon income, the price change in local currency, and the gain or loss on the currency. Historically the currency piece has been the biggest source of volatility in international bond portfolios, often larger than the effect of interest rates. A German government bond that returns 4% in euros in a year when the euro falls 10% against the dollar leaves its American owner down about 6.4%. Bond investors buy foreign bonds for their rates and credit, but without a decision about currency they are really running a currency portfolio with some bonds attached.

The hedging choice is therefore one of the most important an international bond investor makes. Covered interest rate parity ties the forward exchange rate to the gap between two countries' interest rates: the currency with the higher rate trades at a forward discount of about the same size. So hedging with forwards locks in the exchange rate but also gives up the yield advantage of a higher-yielding market; there is no free extra yield. Buying currency put options protects against a fall while keeping the upside, at the cost of a premium. Leaving the position unhedged makes sense only with a strong currency view or a very long horizon. Empirically, hedged international bond portfolios have shown lower volatility and better risk-adjusted returns than unhedged ones.

Plain English

Why can't you just buy a higher-yielding foreign bond, hedge the currency, and pocket the extra yield? Say U.S. one-year rates are 2% and Australian rates are 5%. If you could buy Australian bonds at 5% and lock in today's exchange rate for converting back, everyone would do it, borrowing dollars at 2% to earn a risk-free 3%.

The forward rate is what stops it. Banks set the one-year forward price of the Australian dollar about 3% below today's price, because that's what makes the trade come out even. You earn 5% in Australian dollars and lose about 3% converting back at the locked-in rate, leaving roughly the same 2% you'd have earned at home. So a hedged foreign bond is mainly a bet on that country's bond prices, not its higher yield.

Countries are credits too, and sovereign analysis looks at three pillars. Serviceability asks whether a country can pay its bills this year: its foreign exchange reserves, external debt payments and current account. Solvency asks whether its debt path is sustainable over the medium term: the budget balance before interest, the trend in debt to GDP. Structural fundamentals ask whether it can grow out of its debts: its institutions, governance, rule of law, political stability and economic diversity. Moody's rates sovereigns on economic strength, institutional strength, government financial strength and susceptibility to event risk. The crucial distinction is between external debt in a foreign currency and domestic debt in the country's own currency, which can be inflated away; most sovereign defaults are on external debt, because a government cannot print dollars.

Part III: Mortgage & Structured Products

Part III is about securitization, turning piles of loans into bonds. It starts with the home mortgage itself, moves to the agency pass-throughs that pool mortgages, the CMOs that slice those pools into pieces with different risks, the nonagency deals that carry credit risk as well, commercial mortgage bonds, and asset-backed securities built from car loans, credit cards and stranger things, before ending with the funds through which most people actually own bonds. The thread running through all of it is prepayment: a borrower's right to pay early, and what that right does to the bondholder.

Chapter 11: Residential Mortgages

Primer

The next seven chapters rest on one idea, securitization. A lender makes thousands of similar loans, sells them to a separate legal entity, and that entity issues bonds paid from the borrowers' monthly payments. The lender gets its money back to lend again; investors get bonds backed by a large, diversified pile of loans instead of by one company's promise. Everything that follows is about how those monthly payments are passed to the bondholders and who takes the hit when borrowers pay late, pay early, or don't pay at all.

A residential mortgage is a loan secured by a home, and the lender faces four risks: credit risk, that the borrower defaults; liquidity risk, since an individual loan is hard to sell; price risk, since a fixed-rate loan loses value when rates rise; and prepayment risk, since the borrower can pay off early, usually when rates have fallen. The single best indicator of credit risk is the loan-to-value (LTV) ratio, the loan amount divided by the home's value. A borrower who owes $240,000 on a $300,000 house has an LTV of 80% and $60,000 of equity to protect; one who owes $285,000 has only 5% and little reason to keep paying if prices fall. Credit scores and documentation complete the picture.

The standard American loan is the fixed-rate, level-payment mortgage: the same monthly payment for 15 or 30 years, each payment covering that month's interest plus some principal. Early payments are mostly interest; later ones mostly principal. On a $300,000, 30-year loan at 6%, the payment is $1,798.65, and in the first month $1,500 of it is interest and only $298.65 reduces the balance; after ten years the borrower still owes about $251,000. Because the payment never changes, the lender bears all the interest-rate risk, funding a 30-year asset with shorter-term money. The 30-year fixed loan with a free option to prepay is largely an American creation, made possible by the government-sponsored enterprises; most other countries rely on shorter or variable-rate loans.

Plain English

Why is the prepayment option so valuable to the borrower? Take that $300,000 loan at 6%: $1,798.65 a month. If rates fall to 4%, the borrower refinances into a new 30-year loan at 4% and pays $1,432.25, saving about $366 a month, roughly $4,400 a year, for the cost of some paperwork.

The lender, or the bondholder who bought the loan, gets the $300,000 back at the worst moment: just when new loans pay only 4%. If rates had risen instead, the borrower would have kept the cheap 6% loan for as long as possible. So every mortgage contains a call option for the borrower, and every mortgage bond investor has sold one. Most of Part III is about pricing and managing that option.

Other designs shift the risk around. An adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) resets its rate periodically to a reference rate plus a margin, moving rate risk to the borrower, usually with limits on each reset (say 2 points) and over the life of the loan (say 6 points). A hybrid ARM is fixed for three, five, seven or ten years and then adjusts every year; the 5/1 is the most popular. An interest-only loan defers principal payments for an initial period. A negative amortization loan lets the payment fall below the interest due, adding the shortfall to the balance, and the payment-option ARMs that let borrowers choose their payment helped fuel the 2008 crisis. A balloon loan amortizes slowly and comes due in a lump after five or seven years.

Loans are also sorted by who will buy them. Conforming loans meet the size, documentation and LTV standards of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac and can be pooled into agency securities. Nonconforming loans miss those standards, most often jumbo loans above the size limit, and must be held by banks or sold into the private-label market of Chapter 14. The other key split is credit quality: prime loans go to borrowers with strong credit and full documentation, subprime loans to borrowers with damaged credit and a higher chance of default. Mispricing subprime risk, and packaging those loans into securities rated AAA, was at the heart of the 2008 crisis, which is why so much of the chapter's material on credit comes from that period.

Underwriting a mortgage comes down to a few numbers. The borrower's credit score, usually a FICO score, summarizes their history of paying debts. The payment-to-income ratio compares the monthly housing payment, and separately all monthly debt payments, with gross income; lenders traditionally wanted the first under about 28% and the second under about 36%. The LTV sets the borrower's equity, and loans above 80% usually require private mortgage insurance, which pays the lender part of any loss. Documentation, full income and asset verification versus "stated income," completes the file. In the years before 2008, these standards loosened at the same time through low-documentation loans, high LTVs from inflated appraisals and teaser-rate ARMs, and the combination, not any single factor, is what made the loans so fragile.

The crisis also revealed strategic default. Between 2008 and 2012, as home prices fell as much as 60% in places like Nevada, many borrowers owed far more than their homes were worth. Some of them could have kept paying but chose to stop, reasoning that walking away cost less than paying off a loan on an underwater house. Studies estimated that about a quarter of foreclosed homeowners fell in this group. Strategic default showed that negative equity, not just the inability to pay, drives defaults, and it changed how analysts model mortgage credit: a loan with a high current LTV is risky even if the borrower has a steady job, because the borrower's incentive to keep paying has vanished.

Behind every mortgage is a servicer, which collects the payments, manages escrow accounts for taxes and insurance, chases delinquent borrowers and starts foreclosures. Servicing rights are a separate asset that can be bought and sold, and the servicing fee, typically 25 to 50 basis points a year on the outstanding balance, comes out of the borrowers' interest before anything reaches investors. The servicer matters more than its fee suggests. When Conseco Finance went bankrupt in 2002, the court raised the servicing fee on its securitized loans from 50 to 115 basis points to keep the servicing running, and the extra cost came straight out of the cushion protecting the junior bondholders, whose bonds were downgraded.

For investors, the four risks of a single mortgage translate directly into the risks of mortgage bonds. Credit risk is handled by agency guarantees in Chapter 12 and by subordination in Chapter 14. Liquidity risk is solved by pooling, since thousands of standardized loans can support a security that trades every day. Price risk is the ordinary interest-rate risk of Part I. Prepayment risk is the one that makes mortgage bonds different from anything else: the timing of the cash flows depends on millions of household decisions, and those decisions depend on interest rates. The rest of Part III is largely a set of inventions for dividing, pricing and managing that prepayment risk.

Chapter 12: Agency MBS Pass-Throughs

A mortgage pass-through security pools thousands of mortgages and passes their monthly payments, interest, scheduled principal and prepayments, through to investors, minus servicing and guarantee fees. Three agencies guarantee them. Ginnie Mae securities are backed by loans insured by the FHA, VA or USDA and carry the full faith and credit of the U.S. government. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guarantee pools of conventional conforming loans with their own guarantee, which the market treats as nearly as good. Because of the explicit government backing, Ginnie Mae pools have historically traded a few ticks (fractions of a point) above Fannie Mae pools with the same coupon. Since the guarantee removes credit risk, what remains for the investor is interest-rate and prepayment risk.

Each pool is described by a handful of numbers. The weighted average coupon (WAC) is the average rate the borrowers pay, the weighted average maturity (WAM) the average time left on the loans, and the weighted average loan age (WALA) how long they have been outstanding. Average loan size, credit scores, LTVs and geography round out the description. The pass-through coupon paid to investors is below the WAC by the servicing and guarantee fees. These characteristics matter because they predict prepayments: a pool of large loans to borrowers with high credit scores refinances faster when rates fall than a pool of small loans, since the dollar savings are bigger and the borrowers find it easier to qualify.

Prepayments come from three sources, each modeled separately. Housing turnover, people moving because of jobs, divorce, death or a growing family, runs at a fairly steady 5–7% of a seasoned pool each year and hardly depends on rates. Cash-out refinancing, borrowing against home equity, depends mainly on home prices and household needs. Rate-and-term refinancing, replacing a loan with a cheaper one, is highly sensitive to rates and is the dominant source of prepayment swings and of the negative convexity of mortgage bonds. Partial prepayments, extra principal sent with the monthly payment, add a little more, and defaults on guaranteed loans also show up as prepayments, because the agency pays the investor in full.

Prepayment speeds are quoted in a few standard ways. The single monthly mortality rate (SMM) is the fraction of the remaining balance prepaid in a month; the conditional prepayment rate (CPR) is its annual equivalent, CPR = 1 − (1 − SMM)12. The PSA benchmark assumes CPR rises by 0.2 percentage points a month from zero to 6% at month 30 and stays at 6% after that. It is a convention for communicating, not a forecast: "200 PSA" means twice the benchmark speed, "50 PSA" half. The spread of outcomes is enormous. For a typical 30-year pass-through, the average life is 15.11 years at 50 PSA, 5.92 years at 300 PSA and just 2.78 years at 700 PSA.

The speed numbers, translated. A CPR of 6% means about 6% of the pool's remaining loans pay off early over a year. Per month that works out to an SMM of about 0.51%: on a pool with $290 million left, roughly $1.5 million of extra principal arrives that month on top of the scheduled payments.

"Average life" is roughly how long, on average, each dollar of principal stays out. Depending on what borrowers do, the same security behaves like a 15-year bond or a 3-year bond. You don't know which when you buy it, and the answer depends on rates, which is exactly the problem the next paragraphs describe.

Refinancing follows an S-curve. When the rate borrowers pay is at or below current mortgage rates, almost nobody refinances. As current rates drop below the pool's rate, the incentive grows and prepayments climb steeply. Past a certain point, roughly when the pool's rate is 30% above the current rate, the curve flattens: everyone who can and wants to refinance already has. Over time, pools also show burnout. After a long stretch of low rates, the borrowers still in the pool are those who could not refinance, because of poor credit, too little equity or simple inertia, so each further fall in rates produces fewer prepayments than the last. Prepayment models therefore look at a pool's history, not just today's rates.

All of this gives pass-throughs negative convexity. When rates fall, prepayments speed up and investors get principal back at par just when they can only reinvest at lower rates, so the price rises much less than a regular bond's: contraction risk. When rates rise, prepayments slow, the average life stretches, and the investor is stuck with a below-market coupon for longer, so the price falls more: extension risk. That combination made pass-throughs a poor fit for many institutions. Thrifts funded with short deposits feared extension; insurers selling guaranteed investment contracts needed predictable cash flows; pension funds wanted to lock in long rates and feared contraction. Their needs led to the CMO structures of the next chapter.

“Faster-than-expected prepayments enhance the return of a pass-through purchased at a discount. But for a pass-through purchased at a premium, faster prepayments reduce returns—the investor realizes a capital loss plus must reinvest at lower coupon rates.” — Frank J. Fabozzi

Agency pass-throughs trade mainly in the TBA (to-be-announced) market, a forward market in which buyer and seller agree on only six things: the agency, the maturity, the coupon, the face amount, the price and the settlement date. The specific pools are named just two days before settlement. Treating pools as interchangeable makes the TBA market the second most liquid bond market in the world after Treasuries; it kept trading through the 2008 crisis even as Fannie and Freddie were seized, earning it the nickname "the eighth wonder of the world." Since June 2019, Fannie and Freddie pools have been issued as a single Uniform MBS (UMBS), interchangeable in TBA trades. A dollar roll, selling a TBA for one month and buying it back for a later month, works as cheap financing when the roll trades special.

Not every pool trades as a generic TBA. Pools with characteristics that slow refinancing, such as small loan balances, loans in states with high refinancing costs, low credit scores or high LTVs, are sold as specified pools, and buyers pay a premium over the TBA price, called a pay-up, to own them. The logic follows from the S-curve: a borrower with a $90,000 loan saves far fewer dollars by refinancing than one with $500,000, so the small-balance pool is less likely to prepay when rates fall and has less negative convexity. The TBA market, meanwhile, tends to deliver the worst pools allowed, since sellers keep the better ones to sell at a pay-up. Pricing a pass-through therefore means pricing the specific borrowers in it, not just its coupon.

Chapter 13: Collateralized Mortgage Obligations

A collateralized mortgage obligation (CMO) takes the cash flows from pass-throughs and redistributes them among several classes of bonds, called tranches, with different claims on principal. First issued in 1983, CMOs exist because different investors want different slices of prepayment risk: a bank wants short, predictable cash flows, an insurer wants long ones. Fabozzi stresses the limit of the idea. A CMO cannot eliminate prepayment risk; the total risk is fixed by the mortgages underneath, and the structure only moves it from one tranche to another. Every tranche that gets more protection is matched by another that takes more risk. Most CMOs are issued as REMICs, a tax structure that avoids taxation at the level of the deal.

The simplest structure is sequential-pay. In Fabozzi's example, $400 million of 7.5% pass-throughs back four tranches, A, B, C and D. Every tranche receives interest on its balance each month, but all the principal, scheduled and prepaid, goes to tranche A until it is paid off, then to B, then to C, then to D. At 165 PSA, tranche A is retired in month 81, B in month 100, C in month 178 and D in month 357. Their average lives are 3.48, 7.49, 11.19 and 20.27 years, against 8.76 years for the collateral as a whole. One pool has become a short bond, two medium bonds and a long bond, each suited to a different buyer.

A Z-bond, or accrual tranche, adds a twist. It receives no cash interest while the earlier tranches are outstanding; its interest is instead added to its own balance, and the cash that would have paid it is used to retire the earlier tranches faster. In the example, adding a Z-bond retires tranche A by month 64 instead of 81, shortening A and reducing its exposure to extension. The Z-bond itself is a long, zero-like security that appeals to investors who want to avoid reinvestment risk, since there are no coupons to reinvest until it starts paying. Its price swings, however, are large, because like any zero its value sits in distant cash flows.

Tranches can also be split by coupon. A fixed-rate tranche can be divided into a floater and an inverse floater: in Fabozzi's example, a $96.5 million tranche becomes a $72.375 million floater paying LIBOR plus 50 basis points and a $24.125 million inverse floater paying 28.50% minus three times LIBOR. With a leverage factor of 3, every 100 basis point move in LIBOR moves the inverse floater's coupon 300 basis points the other way, while the two coupons together always average the original 7.5%. Synthetic coupon tranches go further, splitting interest and principal in unequal shares, so that one tranche pays 9% on its balance and another 3%; investors use them to bet on prepayment speeds.

The most important innovation is the planned amortization class (PAC), introduced in March 1987. A PAC bond follows a fixed principal schedule that is met as long as prepayments stay within a band, say between 90 and 300 PSA. The schedule is kept by support bonds, which absorb the variation: when prepayments run fast, the excess goes to the support bonds, and when they run slow, the support bonds wait so the PAC still gets its scheduled principal. PACs suit investors who need predictable cash flows, and the support bonds carry concentrated prepayment risk and higher yields. The protection is only as strong as the support bonds behind it.

Someone has to own the support bonds, and their position is the mirror image of the PAC's. Every bit of extra stability the PAC enjoys comes from the support bonds absorbing the swings, so support bonds have the most violent average lives in the deal: a support tranche might have an average life of 2 years if prepayments are fast and 20 years if they are slow. They pay correspondingly higher yields, and they are bought by investors who think they can forecast prepayments better than the market, or who are paid enough to take the risk. Fabozzi's broader point holds again: the PAC's predictability is not created by the structure but transferred from the support bonds, and the deal's total prepayment risk is exactly what it was in the pool.

“The key to the prepayment protection offered by a PAC bond is the amount of support bonds outstanding. If the support bonds are paid off quickly because of faster-than-expected prepayments, there is no longer any protection for the PAC bonds.” — Frank J. Fabozzi
Plain English

Think of the support bonds as a shock absorber. The mortgage pool pays out principal at an unpredictable pace; the PAC bond wants a smooth, fixed schedule, say $2 million a month. When borrowers prepay $5 million in a month, the PAC takes its $2 million and the support bonds take the other $3 million, getting paid off early. When borrowers prepay only $1 million, the support bonds get nothing and the PAC still gets its $2 million from the scheduled payments.

The catch is that a shock absorber can wear out. A long refinancing wave keeps sending extra principal to the support bonds until they are fully paid off. After that there is nothing left to absorb the next fast month, and the PAC starts taking prepayments like any ordinary tranche. That is the warning in the quote: a PAC's safety is measured by how much support is still outstanding, not by the band printed when it was sold.

The protection band itself drifts. The effective collar, the range of speeds over which the schedule can still be met, changes every month with actual prepayment experience. If prepayments have been slow, more support bonds remain and the band widens; after a fast stretch, it narrows. In a structure with several PACs paid in sequence, the shorter PACs enjoy wider effective collars, for example 90 to 450 PSA instead of the original 90 to 300, because the longer PACs behind them act as extra support. Once the support bonds are gone, the deal effectively turns into a sequential-pay structure, which is why analysts call a PAC with little support left a "busted" PAC.

At the extreme, a pass-through can be split into its interest and its principal. A principal-only (PO) strip receives only principal and is bought at a deep discount; faster prepayments return that principal sooner at par, so when rates fall a PO gains twice, from faster cash flows and from a lower discount rate, and when rates rise it loses twice. An interest-only (IO) strip receives only interest and has no principal to return; it is worth something only as long as the mortgages stay outstanding. When rates fall and borrowers refinance, the interest stream disappears and the IO's value collapses; when rates rise, it lasts longer. IOs therefore have negative duration, rising in value with rates, which makes them useful as hedges, and the IO and PO together are always worth the pass-through.

Plain English

Split a $100 million pool of 6% mortgages into two pieces. The PO holder will eventually get the whole $100 million of principal, but not for years, so it might cost $60 million today. The faster borrowers pay, the sooner that $100 million arrives and the better the PO does.

The IO holder gets the 6% interest on whatever balance is still outstanding each month, and nothing else. If rates fall and half the pool refinances in a year, half the interest stream is gone forever. If rates rise and nobody refinances, the interest keeps flowing for years. So the IO goes up when rates rise, the opposite of almost every other bond. A bank worried about falling rates can own IOs to offset its losses elsewhere, which is why they are used as hedges.

Chapter 14: Nonagency RMBS

Nonagency, or private-label, residential mortgage-backed securities are pools of loans that Fannie, Freddie and Ginnie will not guarantee: jumbo loans, loans with limited documentation, subprime loans. Without a government or agency guarantee, investors bear the credit risk, so these deals must build in their own credit enhancement. That makes them different in kind from the agency CMOs of Chapter 13. Agency CMOs slice only prepayment risk among tranches; nonagency deals also slice credit risk, deciding which bondholders absorb losses when borrowers default and in what order. This credit tranching, applied to subprime mortgages in the mid-2000s, was the machinery through which the housing collapse reached investors around the world.

The dominant form of protection is the senior-subordinated structure. The deal issues senior bonds and a stack of subordinate bonds beneath them, and losses are absorbed from the bottom up: the lowest subordinate tranche takes the first dollar of loss, the next tranche the next, and the senior bonds lose nothing until everything beneath them is gone. The subordinate tranches earn higher yields for standing in front. How much subordination a senior bond needs to earn a AAA rating depends on the expected losses on the loans, which is why the rating agencies' loss assumptions, and their errors before 2008, mattered so much. A deal of subprime loans needs far thicker protection than a deal of prime jumbo loans.

Subordination would shrink quickly if the junior bonds were paid down as fast as the seniors, so deals use a shifting interest mechanism. For the first five years, 100% of prepayments go to the senior bonds, building up the subordinate share of the deal. Over the next four years the senior share of prepayments steps down, for example to 70%, 60%, 40% and 20%, and after year nine prepayments are shared pro rata. Step-down triggers, a delinquency test and a cumulative loss test, can halt the schedule if the loans are going bad, keeping the protection in place when it is most needed. Deals issued after the crisis use tighter triggers and longer lockouts.

Plain English

Why send all the early prepayments to the senior bonds? Picture a $100 million deal: $90 million senior, $10 million subordinate, so the seniors have 10% of the deal standing in front of them. Suppose borrowers prepay $20 million in the first few years.

If that $20 million were split pro rata, the seniors would drop to $72 million and the subordinates to $8 million, still 10% of the deal. Sent entirely to the seniors, it leaves $70 million senior and $10 million subordinate, so the cushion is now about 12.5% of the deal. The protection grows during the early years, when defaults are most likely to show up, and the triggers make sure it isn't released if the loans start going bad.

Protection also comes from the deal's cash flows and from outside. Excess spread is the gap between the interest the borrowers pay and the interest the bonds pay plus fees, and it is the first line of defense, absorbing losses month by month. Overcollateralization means the loans' balance exceeds the bonds', and a cash reserve fund adds a buffer at the start. Third-party protection, bond insurance or letters of credit, was common before 2007 and has almost vanished since the monoline insurers were downgraded. Rating agencies look at three groups of risk: the assets (borrower quality, the originator's record, concentrations, historical loss curves), the structure (the waterfall, triggers and reserves) and the third parties (the servicer, the trustee, and the legal true-sale opinion).

Nonagency cash flows must account for defaults, which agency deals never see. In an agency pool a seriously delinquent loan is simply bought out of the pool at full balance, so the investor sees it as a prepayment. In a nonagency pool the servicer forecloses, sells the house and passes on the net proceeds as recovered principal, months or years after the default, and the shortfall is written off against the lowest subordinate tranche. Servicers must therefore report voluntary prepayments, ordinary payoffs, separately from involuntary prepayments caused by defaults, because the two are distributed differently: voluntary principal follows the shifting-interest schedule, while losses are allocated from the bottom. The crisis taught that defaults depend above all on house prices, since rising prices let a struggling borrower sell instead of default.

Defaults are measured much as prepayments are. The monthly default rate is the share of the pool balance newly in default; its annual version is the conditional default rate (CDR), CDR = 1 − (1 − MDR)12. The industry's SDA benchmark assumes CDR rises from 0.02% to 0.60% over 30 months, stays there to month 60, then falls to 0.03% by month 120, reflecting the fact that mortgage defaults peak a few years after origination. As with PSA, the benchmark is a common language for scenarios, and analysts run deals at multiples of it to see which tranches survive. Loss depends on default and on loss severity, the share of the balance not recovered after foreclosure, which can exceed 50% in a falling market.

The market that emerged after 2008 is more conservative. RMBS 2.0 deals pool prime jumbo loans with full documentation, and non-QM deals pool loans to creditworthy borrowers who don't fit the standard rules, such as the self-employed with irregular income; both use simpler structures than the legacy subprime deals. The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 requires sponsors to keep 5% of the credit risk of their securitizations, "skin in the game," and forbids them from hedging it away, so that originators share the losses on bad loans. Loans meeting the definition of a qualified residential mortgage are exempt. The rule targets the originate-to-distribute model in which lenders had no reason to care whether loans would be repaid.

The lesson of the chapter is that structure cannot create quality. A senior-subordinated deal can carve a pool of risky loans into bonds with very different risks, and the senior bonds can be genuinely safe if the subordination is thick enough for the losses that actually occur. But the whole edifice rests on the loss estimate. When subprime loss assumptions proved far too low, losses cut through the subordinate tranches and into bonds that had been rated AAA, and the correlation between loans, all tied to the same national housing market, meant that the diversification investors had counted on did not exist. Credit tranching redistributes risk; it does not make it disappear.

Chapter 15: Commercial MBS (CMBS)

Primer

Commercial real estate is valued by the money it earns. Net operating income (NOI) is a property's rent and other income minus its operating costs (maintenance, taxes, insurance, management), before paying the mortgage. An office building that collects $2 million a year in rent and spends $750,000 to run itself has an NOI of $1.25 million. That number is what pays the loan, so lenders and bond analysts start every commercial mortgage analysis from it.

Commercial mortgage-backed securities pool loans on income-producing property: apartment buildings, offices, shopping centers, warehouses, hotels and health-care facilities. Commercial mortgages differ from home loans in two basic ways. They are usually nonrecourse: the lender can take the property if the loan defaults but cannot pursue the borrower's other assets. And the borrower is judged mainly on the property's cash flow rather than on personal income. Two ratios dominate. The debt service coverage (DSC) ratio, net operating income divided by debt service, shows how comfortably the property pays the loan; lenders typically want at least 1.25. The LTV, usually 75% or less, shows the cushion if the property must be sold.

Property types carry very different risks. Multifamily apartments have the steadiest cash flows and lowest default rates, since people always need housing, and Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac run their own multifamily programs. Office buildings enjoy long leases of five to ten years but suffer when big tenants leave or recessions push vacancies up. Retail centers live or die by their anchor tenants, and co-tenancy clauses let smaller tenants cut their rent if an anchor departs. Industrial warehouses are cheap to build and easy to re-lease, and e-commerce has boosted demand for them. Hotels are the most volatile, effectively re-leasing every room every night, and are judged by revenue per available room. Health-care properties add the risk of changes in Medicare and Medicaid reimbursement.

Plain English

The two ratios, on one building. An office building earns $1.25 million a year after its operating costs (its NOI). The mortgage payments are $1 million a year. DSC = $1.25 million ÷ $1 million = 1.25: the building earns 25% more than it needs to pay the loan, so rents could drop by a fifth before the payments are in danger.

The building is worth $16 million and the loan is $12 million, an LTV of 75%. If the borrower walks away, the lender owns a building it can sell for up to a quarter less than its appraised value and still get its money back. Because commercial loans are usually nonrecourse, those two cushions, cash flow and property value, are all the lender has.

Unlike homeowners, commercial borrowers can't simply refinance at will. Loans carry strong call protection, in four forms. A lockout forbids prepayment for a set number of years. Defeasance lets the borrower replace the property as collateral with a portfolio of Treasuries producing the same cash flows, so the bondholders keep their payments and lose all credit risk. Prepayment penalty points charge a declining percentage of the balance, for example 5% in the first year falling to 1% in the fifth. Yield maintenance charges a make-whole premium that compensates the lender for lost interest. As a result, prepayment risk, which dominates residential MBS, is a minor issue in CMBS, and credit risk is the main event.

The main credit risk at the end of a commercial loan is balloon risk. Commercial mortgages typically run five to ten years but amortize as if they lasted 25 or 30, so a large balance remains at maturity and must be refinanced. If credit markets are tight or the property's value has fallen when the balloon comes due, the borrower may be unable to refinance even if it never missed a payment. The loan then extends or defaults. Balloon risk was the great worry of 2008–2012, when CMBS delinquencies rose above 9%, and it is why investors watch the "maturity wall" of loans coming due in any given year as closely as they watch rents.

CMBS deals rely on three kinds of servicer. The master servicer handles routine administration, collecting payments, managing escrows, reporting to the trustee, and advancing missed payments to bondholders as long as it expects to recover them. The primary servicer deals with borrowers day to day. The special servicer takes over loans in default or close to it and decides whether to foreclose, modify the loan or extend it. That role creates a conflict of interest: special servicers often own the B-piece, the first-loss tranche, and their decisions about the timing of foreclosures and the terms of workouts can favor that tranche over the senior bondholders. Their choices shape recoveries throughout the deal.

A typical conduit CMBS deal is tranched both for credit and for time. The senior AAA bonds are split into several classes, A-1 through A-4 or more, that receive principal in sequence, giving buyers a choice of short or long average lives; mezzanine and subordinate classes sit below them, with the unrated B-piece at the bottom. Losses are written off from the bottom up, while scheduled principal and any prepayments flow from the top down. Many deals also carry an interest-only class that collects the excess of the loans' interest over the bonds' coupons. When a loan is seriously delinquent, an appraisal reduction cuts the amount the master servicer advances, shifting the shortfall onto the junior tranches before the property is even sold.

Deals come in two broad shapes. A single-borrower deal is backed by one large loan on one property or a portfolio of properties owned by one sponsor, for example a trophy office tower or a hotel chain; its risk is concentrated and its analysis is close to a corporate credit analysis of the property. A conduit, or multi-borrower, deal pools loans to many unrelated borrowers across property types and regions, and its analysis looks more like a residential deal's, with diversification, subordination and a waterfall doing the work. Within a conduit, concentration still matters: a deal in which the ten largest loans make up half the balance behaves more like a handful of single-property bets than a diversified pool.

For bond investors, CMBS sit between corporate bonds and residential MBS. Like corporates, their value turns on credit: the cash flows of real businesses, the tenants who pay rent, and the market for the buildings themselves. Like residential MBS, they are tranched, and the senior bonds rely on subordination below them. Because prepayment is restricted, CMBS have more stable average lives and better convexity than residential mortgage bonds, which makes them attractive to insurers and pension funds that need predictable cash flows. The trade-off is exposure to the commercial property cycle, in which office vacancies, retail closures or a collapse in hotel travel can hit many loans in a deal at the same time.

Chapter 16: Asset-Backed Securities

Asset-backed securities (ABS) apply securitization to almost anything that produces predictable cash: car loans, credit card balances, student loans, equipment leases, utility charges, even music royalties. The legal foundation is the true sale: the originator sells the assets to a special purpose vehicle (SPV), a separate company that does nothing but hold them and issue the bonds, so that if the originator later goes bankrupt its creditors cannot reach the assets. The originator can raise money more cheaply than on its own credit, because the SPV's bonds can be rated higher than the company itself, and investors get a direct claim on the cash flows whatever happens to the originator. The structure is the same idea as Chapter 14, applied more widely.

ABS use the same forms of credit enhancement as private-label mortgage deals. Internally, there is senior-subordinated tranching with losses absorbed from the bottom up, overcollateralization where the assets exceed the bonds, excess spread where the assets earn more than the bonds pay, and a cash reserve fund deposited at the start to cover losses before excess spread builds up. Externally, bond insurance and letters of credit were once common but largely disappeared after the monoline insurers failed in 2007 and 2008. Dodd-Frank's 5% risk retention applies to ABS sponsors as well, and the retained slice cannot be hedged or sold. Many deals also have an optional cleanup call that lets the sponsor retire the bonds once the pool is small.

Why securitize at all instead of issuing corporate bonds? Fabozzi gives four reasons: lower funding costs, more diverse sources of funding, earlier recognition of earnings for financial reporting, and, for banks and other regulated firms, relief from capital requirements. The first is the big one. His example is Exceptional Dental Equipment, a company rated double-B that has made $300 million of installment loans to dentists. Borrowing on its own name, it pays the yield on double-B bonds. Selling the loans to a bankruptcy-remote trust lets investors judge the loans themselves, and with credit enhancement most of the bonds can be rated far higher than the company, so the blended cost of the deal comes in below its corporate borrowing rate.

The legal plumbing has grown more elaborate. Since 1997, accounting rules have made two-step securitizations standard: the originator sells the assets to an intermediate SPV, the depositor, which sells them on to the issuing trust. In the Harley-Davidson Motorcycle Trust 2007-3, for example, the seller was Harley-Davidson Credit Corp., the depositor Harley-Davidson Customer Funding Corp., and the issuer the trust itself, with the seller continuing to service the loans. Credit card deals use a master trust: the bank pledges a large pool of accounts and issues many separate series from it, each with its own terms and enhancement, all paid from the same pool. The structures exist to make the true sale hold up in court.

Auto loan ABS have the most predictable prepayments of any sector, because car loans are paid off early mainly when cars are sold, traded in, repossessed or wrecked, not when rates fall; nobody refinances a car loan to save half a point. Prepayments are measured by the absolute prepayment speed (ABS), monthly prepayments as a share of the original pool, which can be converted to SMM. Typical issuers are captive finance companies such as Ford Motor Credit and Caterpillar Financial Services. A deal is usually split into several sequential senior classes (A-1 to A-4), followed by subordinate classes, with overcollateralization, reserves and excess spread for support. Because of their simplicity and liquidity, auto ABS serve as the benchmark for pricing the rest of the ABS market.

Credit card ABS work differently because card balances revolve: customers pay down and borrow again constantly. Deals therefore have a revolving period, lasting from 18 months to ten years, during which principal collections buy new receivables instead of repaying bonds, keeping the pool's size constant. After it ends, principal is paid out to bondholders. If the portfolio deteriorates, a pay-out event (early amortization) ends the revolving period immediately and starts repaying the bonds. Analysts watch the gross portfolio yield, the charge-off rate, the net portfolio yield that pays bondholders' interest, delinquencies at 30, 60 and 90 days, and the monthly payment rate; a very low payment rate warns of slow repayment and possible early amortization.

Plain English

A credit card deal is a bucket that stays full. Say the bank puts $1 billion of card balances into the deal. Every month cardholders pay back, say, 15% of what they owe, $150 million, and keep spending on their cards. During the revolving period, the deal uses that $150 million to buy $150 million of new charges, so the bucket stays at $1 billion and bondholders receive only interest.

The key safety number is net portfolio yield: the interest and fees cardholders pay, minus the balances written off as uncollectable. If cardholders pay 18% and 5% of balances are charged off, the net yield is about 13%, enough to pay bondholders 3% or 4% with room to spare. If charge-offs climb until that cushion is gone, the pay-out event kicks in: the deal stops buying new charges and starts handing all collections to bondholders to get them out quickly.

Some ABS are created by law. When states deregulated electric utilities, the utilities were left with plants and contracts they could no longer recover through regulated rates, so-called stranded costs. Legislatures allowed them to add a competitive transition charge to every customer's bill and declared the right to collect it a property right that could be sold to an SPV and securitized. These rate reduction bonds come with a true-up mechanism that adjusts the charge periodically so collections match what the bonds need, giving them very stable cash flows and near-AAA credit. At the exotic end, Fabozzi notes bonds backed by music royalties, the best known from David Bowie in 1997, with Ashford & Simpson and James Brown following.

The common thread in ABS analysis is to understand the asset first. Auto loans default when borrowers lose jobs and cars lose value; card balances turn bad gradually and in large numbers; utility charges are collected from nearly every household by law; royalties depend on whether people keep listening. Each asset class has its own historical loss curve, the pattern of losses over the life of a pool, and a structure is only as safe as its enhancement is large relative to that curve under stress. The structure then decides who bears the losses and when, and the servicer decides how well the assets are collected; Conseco's servicing failure showed that even good assets can disappoint if the company collecting them falters.

Chapter 17: Collective Investment Vehicles

Primer

A fund's net asset value (NAV) per share is simply what its holdings are worth, minus what it owes, divided by the number of shares. A bond fund holding $1 billion of bonds with 50 million shares has a NAV of $20 a share. For an ordinary mutual fund, that is also the price you buy and sell at; for funds that trade on an exchange, the market price can drift away from NAV, and much of this chapter is about why and by how much.

Most individuals, and many institutions, own bonds through collective investment vehicles: mutual funds, closed-end funds, exchange-traded funds and hedge funds. They offer what a single investor can't easily build. Diversification across hundreds or thousands of bonds is impossible with a small account when bonds trade in lots of $100,000 or more. Liquidity lets fund shares be sold every day, or every minute for ETFs, even when the bonds inside rarely trade. Professional management supplies credit analysis, risk control and trading skill. Institutions use funds to reach sectors where they have no in-house expertise, to obtain leverage or short positions, and to rely on the fund's compliance work.

Open-end funds, ordinary mutual funds, issue and redeem shares every day at NAV, calculated once at the close, so the number of shares changes with investor demand. Closed-end funds issue a fixed number of shares that then trade on an exchange, and their price can sit well above or below NAV. A BlackRock study of what drives those premiums and discounts points to the fund's yield compared with its peers, changes in its dividend, its performance, the outlook for its sector, investor sentiment and the availability of similar products. Mutual funds come in share classes with different fee arrangements: Class A with an up-front sales charge and lower ongoing fees, Class B with a charge on early exit, and Class C with higher annual 12b-1 marketing fees.

The cost that matters most is the expense ratio, the management fee plus 12b-1 fees and other annual costs, expressed as a share of assets. For bond funds, whose returns are modest, fees take a large bite: a 0.75% expense ratio on a fund yielding 3% consumes a quarter of the income before the investor sees any of it. Bond funds are sorted by what they hold, government, investment-grade, high-yield (at least 65% below BBB), inflation-protected, bank loan, multi-sector, convertible, and by duration: long-term above six years, intermediate from 3.5 to six, short-term from one to 3.5, and ultrashort below one. Tax-exempt funds hold national or single-state munis, the latter to give residents triple tax exemption.

Specialized funds stretch the menu. Inverse bond funds aim to move opposite to a bond index, profiting when yields rise. Leveraged bond funds aim for a multiple of an index's daily return, which compounds unpredictably over longer periods. Target-term trusts are closed-end funds that wind up on a set date, returning NAV to holders, so they behave more like a single bond with a maturity than like a perpetual fund. That last point matters: an ordinary bond fund never matures, so an investor who needs a specific sum on a specific date cannot rely on it the way they could rely on a bond held to maturity, since the fund's value on that date depends on rates then.

Exchange-traded funds now lead innovation in bond funds. They trade all day on an exchange, and a creation and redemption mechanism keeps their price close to NAV: large dealers called authorized participants can swap baskets of bonds for new ETF shares, or shares for bonds, whenever the price strays. Because redemptions are usually paid in bonds rather than cash, the fund rarely has to sell holdings and realize gains, making ETFs more tax-efficient than mutual funds. Passive bond ETFs usually cost 10 to 30 basis points less than comparable mutual funds. The first generation tracked indexes; a second generation is actively managed.

Bond ETFs have a weakness: they can only be as liquid as the bonds inside them. During the 2008 crisis, high-yield bond ETFs traded at prices 5 to 6 percentage points away from their NAVs as authorized participants stepped back and the underlying bonds stopped trading. Supporters argue that the ETF price was the more honest number, a real-time price for bonds whose official valuations were stale, while critics see a gap that can trap investors who need to sell in a panic. Fabozzi's point is that the ETF wrapper adds trading convenience, not liquidity that doesn't exist in the market underneath; a fund full of bonds that rarely trade will struggle when everyone heads for the door.

Plain English

How does an ETF's price stay near the value of its bonds? Say a bond ETF's bonds are worth $100 a share but the ETF is trading at $101 because buyers are keen. A dealer buys $100 worth of the bonds, hands them to the fund in exchange for a new ETF share, and sells that share for $101, pocketing $1. Dealers keep doing this until the price falls back to about $100. If the ETF trades at $99, they do the reverse.

The machine runs smoothly only if dealers can actually buy and sell the bonds. In a panic, when nobody will buy the bonds at any sensible price, dealers stop, and the ETF's price can sink well below the stated value of its bonds. That's what happened to high-yield ETFs in 2008.

Hedge funds run bond strategies with leverage and short selling. Convergence trades bet that historical relationships will reassert themselves, such as the spread between on-the-run and off-the-run Treasuries. Distressed investing buys bonds of troubled issuers at deep discounts: hedge funds bought Detroit pension bonds at 41 cents on the dollar, and when Puerto Rico sold $3.5 billion of 8% bonds at a price of 93, an 8.727% yield, just after a junk downgrade, hedge funds took about 70% of the issue, betting that forced selling by investment-grade holders had created value. Hedge funds charge a management fee plus an incentive allocation, a share of profits, which pushes managers toward strategies that earn small, steady gains most of the time and occasionally lose heavily.

Real estate investment trusts (REITs) are the last vehicle in the chapter. An equity REIT owns properties, a mortgage REIT owns mortgages or mortgage securities, and a hybrid owns both; about 90% of traded REITs are equity REITs. Like closed-end funds, REIT shares trade on exchanges at prices that can differ from NAV. Their appeal is tax: a REIT that keeps most of its assets and income in real estate and pays out at least 90% of its taxable income as dividends can deduct those dividends, so it pays little or no corporate tax and its income is taxed once, in the shareholders' hands. Mortgage REITs give investors access to an illiquid asset class, broad diversification and professional credit selection, often with leverage that magnifies both income and risk.

Part IV turns from describing bonds to valuing them. It begins with how bonds actually trade and how hard that has become, then builds the two great valuation machines of the bond market: the binomial interest-rate tree for bonds with embedded options and Monte Carlo simulation for mortgage securities, both producing the option-adjusted spread. It applies option thinking to convertible bonds, and finishes with the analysis of corporate credit, first the traditional way, by reading the business, the covenants and the financial statements, and then through the mathematical models of default.

Chapter 18: Bond Liquidity & Trading

Liquidity sounds simple, the ability to sell quickly at a fair price, but Fabozzi breaks it into five dimensions. Tightness is low transaction cost, a narrow gap between bid and offer. Immediacy is the speed with which an order can be filled. Depth is the presence of many buyers and sellers. Breadth is enough volume to absorb large trades. Resiliency is the market's ability to recover from a shock without lasting price damage. A market can score well on some and badly on others: Treasuries score well on all five, while a small corporate issue may offer a quote but collapse in price if anyone tries to sell a large block.

Trading costs come in two kinds. Explicit costs, the bid-ask spread and commissions, are visible. Hidden costs are often larger: the market impact of a big order moving the price against the trader, timing costs from delay, and the opportunity cost of trades never made. The trade itself has four stages: the source of the order and its execution happen in the front office, and clearance and settlement happen in the back office, where operations staff make sure the securities and cash actually change hands. Straight-through processing automates the whole chain, cutting errors and settlement risk, and a large part of the bond market's modernization has been the spread of automation from the back office to the trading desk.

Pricing is its own problem because most bonds do not trade on most days. Fund managers still need a value for every holding every day to compute NAVs, measure performance and manage risk, so they rely on pricing services such as Bloomberg, ICE and Refinitiv for evaluated prices, model estimates rather than actual trades. The main technique is matrix pricing: take the prices of comparable bonds that did trade and adjust for differences in credit quality, maturity, coupon and liquidity. The stakes are real: a pricing error of 25 basis points of yield on a $100 million position with a duration of about one, or a quarter-point error in price on a longer bond, means a mismark of around $250,000, distorting a fund's reported value, its performance and its measured risk.

The traditional market rested on dealers acting as principals: a client called a dealer, the dealer bought the bonds into its own inventory, and later found a buyer. Since 2008, higher capital requirements have made that inventory expensive to hold and less profitable, and dealers have cut it sharply. Electronic platforms have stepped into the gap, in five types: auction platforms for new issues and block sales, cross-matching systems that pair buyers and sellers anonymously, interdealer platforms where dealers trade with each other, multi-dealer platforms such as Tradeweb and MarketAxess where several dealers compete to fill a client's order, and single-dealer platforms run by individual banks.

The newest model is all-to-all trading, in which dealers, asset managers, hedge funds and other investors can all trade with each other on one venue; buy-side firms now supply about half the liquidity on such platforms, and portfolio trading lets a manager move a whole basket of bonds in one transaction. Two protocols dominate: the request for quote (RFQ), in which a client asks several dealers for prices, still the main method, and order-driven trading, in which orders are matched against posted prices, which is growing. None of this has made dealers obsolete, as an industry study concluded.

“Major corporate bond dealers are not going anywhere and will continue to be at the center of risk transfer in this market. Their balance sheets, data, expertise, and role as new-issue underwriters ensure they are critical partners for asset managers and hedge funds. However, these same dealers have reduced their capital commitment to this business, creating a liquidity gap that still needs filling.” — Greenwich Associates study

Regulation has added transparency. TRACE, the Trade Reporting and Compliance Engine, requires dealers to report corporate bond trades so that investors can see real transaction prices, ending an era in which only dealers knew where bonds traded. The SEC's Rule 22e-4 requires mutual funds to run liquidity risk management programs and sort their holdings into tiers by how quickly they could be sold without moving the price. Tools have followed: the Liquidity Cost Score estimates the round-trip cost of trading each bond in an index, so that managers can budget for liquidity as they budget for risk. Transparency, though, has not closed the structural gap left by the dealers' retreat.

Even with TRACE, a reliable price is hard to get. Reported trades are unreliable guides in fast-moving markets, and a price for a $100,000 trade says little about where a $20 million block would clear; Konstantinov and Pojarliev stress that order size moves the price as a rule, not an exception. Dealer quotes can be biased, and one technology firm found that dealers were unwilling or unable to quote illiquid bonds about 90% of the time. Independent pricing services such as Bloomberg's BVAL, IHS Markit and ICE Data Services fill the gap with documented methods. Behind the scenes, settlement happens by delivery versus payment, cash and securities moving simultaneously between custodian banks so that neither side is left exposed. And the cost of trades never made, the shortfall when a manager cannot execute a planned trade, is real but unobservable.

Plain English

Why does a bond fund care so much about liquidity it may never use? A bond fund promises daily redemptions in cash. If a wave of investors sells, the fund must sell bonds that same week, and it will sell the easiest ones first. Those are the bonds with tight spreads and lots of buyers, like Treasuries. What's left behind is the stuff that's hard to sell, so the remaining investors end up owning a less liquid fund than before.

A liquidity cost score puts a price on that. If a bond costs 1.5% to buy and sell again, a manager knows a forced sale of $10 million of it costs about $150,000 just in trading frictions, and can decide whether the extra yield is worth it. The regulators' liquidity tiers exist so funds face that question before a crisis, not during one.

The chapter's larger message is that bond liquidity is a resource that can disappear exactly when it is needed. In calm markets, quotes are plentiful and electronic platforms make trading look easy. In stressed markets, dealers with less capital step back, the evaluated prices of rarely traded bonds become guesses, and funds that promised daily liquidity, from mutual funds to ETFs, find that the bonds inside them cannot be sold at anything near their marked value. For portfolio managers, that argues for treating liquidity as a risk to be measured and budgeted alongside duration and credit, for holding enough truly liquid assets to meet redemptions, and for recognizing that a yield premium on an illiquid bond is payment for that risk, not free extra return.

Chapter 19: Embedded Options Analysis

Primer

This chapter treats bonds as packages of options, so a little option vocabulary helps. A call option is the right, not the obligation, to buy something at a set price (the strike) by a set date; a put option is the right to sell. An option is worth more the more the underlying price can swing, because the owner gets the good outcomes and can walk away from the bad ones. That is what volatility means here: how much interest rates are expected to move. Higher volatility makes every option more valuable, to whoever owns it.

Before tackling options, Fabozzi fixes how spreads are measured. The traditional yield spread compares a bond's yield to maturity with the yield of a single Treasury of similar maturity, one point on the curve. The static spread, or zero-volatility spread, is the constant spread that, added to every Treasury spot rate, makes the present value of the bond's cash flows equal to its price. It compares the bond with a portfolio of zero-coupon Treasuries matching each cash flow. In his example, a 25-year corporate bond with an 8.8% coupon costs $87.08 while the same cash flows discounted at Treasury spot rates are worth $96.61; a 100 basis point spread gives $88.55, 110 gives $87.80, and 120 basis points gives exactly $87.08. The traditional spread was only 109 basis points, an 11-point understatement that grows with the curve's steepness.

The key to valuing bonds with options is to take them apart. A callable bond equals an option-free bond minus the call option the investor has sold to the issuer. A putable bond equals an option-free bond plus the put option the investor has bought. A convertible equals a straight bond plus a call option on the issuer's stock. It follows that higher interest-rate volatility, which makes options more valuable, lowers the value of a callable bond and raises the value of a putable one. It also follows that a callable bond's two weaknesses, reinvestment risk when it is called and price compression as the market anticipates the call, are the costs of the option the investor has sold.

Plain English

Callable bond = plain bond minus a call option. In Fabozzi's tree example later in the chapter, a 5.25% bond without a call would be worth $102.075. The same bond callable at $100 in a year is worth $101.432. The difference, $0.643, is the value of the call option you, the bondholder, have given the issuer. You are paid for it through a slightly higher yield on the callable bond.

Now suppose markets expect interest rates to swing more wildly. The plain bond's value doesn't change much, but the issuer's call option becomes more valuable, because a big drop in rates, the situation where the issuer calls, is now more likely. So the callable bond, plain bond minus a now-bigger option, is worth less. A putable bond works the other way: you own the option, so more volatility makes your bond worth more.

Below a certain yield, a callable bond shows negative convexity: its price rises less when yields fall than it drops when they rise, because the likelihood of a call caps the upside. That does not mean a callable bond can never trade above its call price. Fabozzi's example is a 10-year bond with a 13% coupon, callable in one year at 104, when one-year rates are 5%. Investors treat it as a one-year bond that will surely be called: it will pay a $6.50 coupon in six months and $110.50 (the $104 call price plus the last coupon) in a year, worth $111.52 discounted at 5%. Paying $111.52 for a bond that will be called at $104 is perfectly rational, since the coupons over the year more than make up the difference.

To value the option properly, Fabozzi builds a binomial interest-rate tree, the arbitrage-free model associated with Black, Derman and Toy. Each year the one-year rate can move to a higher or a lower value, with equal probability, the two related by rH = rL × e2σ, where σ is the assumed volatility. The tree is calibrated so that it prices the issuer's on-the-run bonds exactly. In his example, a first guess of 4.5% for the lower rate in year one values the two-year on-the-run bond at $99.567, below its $100 market price, so the rate is too high; the search settles on 4.074%, and with 10% volatility the higher rate is 4.074% × e0.20 = 4.976%. The same search for year two, using the three-year bond, gives a lower rate of 4.530%. A bond is then valued by working backward from maturity: at each node, its value is the average of the two discounted values one step ahead plus the coupon.

Options enter the tree as simple rules at each node. For a callable bond, if the value computed at a node is above the call price, the issuer would call, so the value there is replaced by the call price. For a putable bond, if the value falls below the put price, the investor would put, so the value is raised to the put price. In Fabozzi's example, a 5.25% bond callable at par in one year is worth $101.432 against $102.075 for the same bond without the call, so the call option is worth $0.643; with a put at par instead, the bond is worth $102.523, and the put $0.448. The tree handles bonds callable on many dates or a single date; mortgage securities, whose cash flows depend on the whole path of rates, need the simulation of Chapter 20.

Plain English

A rate tree is a map of where interest rates might go, one year at a time. Today the one-year rate is known. Next year it is either higher or lower, with even odds; the year after, each of those branches splits again, and so on. How far apart the branches spread is set by volatility: the more rates are expected to swing, the wider the tree.

To value a bond, start at the far end of the tree, where you know the bond pays $100 plus its last coupon, and walk back toward today. At each point, the bond is worth the average of its two possible values one year later, discounted at that point's rate, plus any coupon. The call is just a cap: wherever the tree says the bond is worth more than $100, the issuer would call it, so you write $100 instead. Walk all the way back, and the number at the start is the callable bond's fair value, with the cost of the call built in.

Volatility drives the results. For four 10-year 5% bonds that differ only in when the call can first be exercised, the option-free bond's value does not change with the assumed volatility, while every callable bond falls in value as volatility rises, and those with longer protection before the first call sit closer to the option-free value. That exposes the main danger of the method, model risk: the answer depends on a volatility assumption that may be wrong, so careful analysts run the model under several volatility levels and see how much the answer moves. Fine time steps raise accuracy for short bonds but are slow for 30-year bonds, and cash flows that fall between nodes must be interpolated.

The tree produces the key measure of the chapter, the option-adjusted spread (OAS): the constant spread that, added to every rate in the tree, makes the model value equal the market price. It is "option-adjusted" because the cash flows in the tree already reflect when the option would be exercised, so the OAS is the spread left after paying for the option. What it measures depends on which curve built the tree: built from Treasuries, the OAS includes the issuer's credit spread; built from the issuer's own curve, it shows only whether the bond is cheap or rich relative to the issuer's other bonds. The option cost in spread terms is the static spread minus the OAS, positive for callable bonds and negative for putable ones.

Plain English

Why not just compare yields on callable bonds? Say a callable corporate bond offers a static spread of 180 basis points over Treasuries, and a similar noncallable bond offers 150. The callable looks 30 points cheaper. But part of that 180 is payment for the call option you're giving the issuer, which will cost you money if rates fall. If the model says the option is worth 50 basis points a year, the OAS is 180 − 50 = 130.

On that option-adjusted basis, the callable bond is actually 20 points worse than the noncallable one. OAS strips out the option so that bonds with different features can be compared on one scale. The catch, as the chapter keeps saying, is that the 50 comes from a model with a volatility assumption, so the OAS is only as good as that assumption.

The same tree gives honest risk measures. Effective duration is computed in five steps: find the OAS; shift the on-the-run curve up by a small amount; rebuild the tree; add the same OAS to every rate; and reprice the bond. Repeating with a downward shift gives the second price, and the usual formula, (P− − P+) / (2P0Δy), does the rest. Effective convexity follows from the same three prices, and the gaps from the standard measures can be enormous: a principal-only mortgage strip can have a standard convexity near zero but an effective convexity of 2,155, meaning a 100 basis point move produces about a 21.6% price change from convexity alone. The general rule is simple: if a bond's cash flows don't change with rates, use modified duration; if they do, use effective duration.

“Duration is a by-product of a pricing model. If the pricing model is poor, the resulting duration estimate is poor.” — Frank J. Fabozzi

Chapter 20: RMBS Analysis (Monte Carlo)

Primer

Monte Carlo simulation means answering a question about an uncertain future by playing it out many times at random. Instead of assuming one path for interest rates, the computer generates hundreds or thousands of plausible paths, works out what the bond pays along each one, and averages the results. It is the tool of choice whenever the answer depends on the whole route taken, not just on where you end up, which is exactly the problem with mortgages.

The binomial tree fails for mortgage securities because their cash flows are path-dependent. Whether a borrower prepays this month depends not only on today's rate but on the whole history of rates since the loan was made: a pool that has already lived through a refinancing wave is burned out and will prepay less at the same rate than a fresh pool. A tree that only knows where rates are at each node cannot capture that history. CMO tranches are doubly path-dependent, since each tranche's cash flow this month depends on how much of the other tranches is still outstanding, which itself depends on every month's prepayments so far. The answer is Monte Carlo simulation.

The simple alternative, a static cash flow yield at one prepayment speed, inherits all the flaws of yield to maturity and adds a third: it assumes reinvestment at the cash flow yield, assumes the security is held to its final payment, and assumes prepayments arrive at a single constant speed. Vector analysis softens the last assumption by letting speeds change over time, say 165 PSA for three years and then a faster or slower speed. A static spread can also be computed against the whole Treasury curve. But every static method assumes rates will not change, an odd assumption for a security whose cash flows are driven entirely by changes in rates. Monte Carlo was built to fix exactly that.

The simulation runs in a few steps. First, generate 512 to 1,024 random paths of monthly interest rates, consistent with today's term structure and an assumed volatility; each month on each path produces a discount rate and a mortgage refinancing rate. Second, feed each path's refinancing rates into a prepayment model to produce that path's monthly cash flows, adding defaults and recoveries for nonagency deals. Third, discount each path's cash flows at that path's own rates. The theoretical value is the average of the path values, with the paths adjusted so that they correctly price zero-coupon Treasuries, which keeps the model arbitrage-free. The OAS is the spread that, added to every rate on every path, makes that average equal the market price.

Plain English

Picture running the next 30 years a thousand times. In one run, rates drift up and homeowners almost never refinance; the mortgage bond pays slowly for decades. In another, rates plunge in year three and half the pool refinances within months. In a third, rates fall and rise and fall again, so the pool burns out partway through. For each run the computer works out every monthly payment the bond would make and what those payments are worth today.

The bond's model value is the average across all thousand runs. If the bond trades below that value, it looks cheap; the OAS says by how much, in yield terms. Just as useful is the spread of results: if the thousand values are tightly bunched, the bond's outcome hardly depends on what rates do; if they range widely, you own a lot of prepayment risk, whatever the average says.

Fabozzi insists that the average is not enough. Two tranches from the same deal can have the same OAS and very different risks: in his example, a PAC bond has a value of 88 with a standard deviation of 1 across paths, while a support bond has a value of 77 with a standard deviation of 10. The PAC's value barely depends on the path; the support bond's depends on it heavily. An analyst should therefore look at the whole distribution of path values and of average lives, not just the OAS. The option cost, the static spread minus the OAS, is also useful because it changes less than the OAS when the market moves slightly, and variance reduction techniques can cut the number of paths needed to price a tranche within a tick, although very sensitive tranches can still need more than 1,024 paths.

The asset manager's real task, Fabozzi says, is to follow the value and the risk from the collateral into the tranches. Every deal contains some tranches with high value and low risk, the ones an investor wants, and, unavoidably, others with low value and high risk, because the total of both is fixed by the collateral. Finding which is which requires a good structuring model that reproduces exactly how the deal allocates cash. His FactSet illustration shows the method on a Freddie Mac sequential-pay CMO: the front tranche A has an effective duration of only 0.02, while the Z-bond's is 11.24 and its OAS, at 228.4 basis points, the highest in the deal, and only the VB tranche has positive effective convexity, 2.99.

The same analysis on a single pass-through is revealing. For a Fannie Mae 3.5% 30-year pool from 2019, with a gross WAC of 4.38%, 345 months remaining, an average FICO score of 742 and an LTV of 81%, the static spread is 84.53 basis points and the OAS 90.25 under a lognormal rate model or 92.98 under a normal model, an option cost of about 28 to 30 basis points. Its modified duration of 2.564 compares with an effective duration of 2.171 or 2.580 depending on the rate model, and its effective convexity is negative, −0.609 or −0.098. The spread between the two models' answers is itself a warning: even for the plainest agency pool, the risk measures depend on modeling choices, and two firms can quote different durations for the same security.

Mortgage analysts use more measures than the standard ones. Prepayment sensitivity is the change in price, in basis points, for a 1% rise in prepayment speed: a pass-through priced at 106.10 at 300 PSA and 106.01 at 303 PSA has a sensitivity of −9, typical of a premium bond hurt by faster prepayments. Beyond model durations (modified, cash flow and effective), there are market-based durations measured from actual price behavior: empirical duration from regressions, hedging duration from price models, and Douglas Breeden's coupon curve duration, which reads duration off the prices of neighboring coupons; a 4% pass-through gives a coupon curve duration of 4.14 for a 50 basis point decline. That last method works only for generic pass-throughs. Total return analysis remains the final test of performance.

Total return, the method of Chapter 3, is how a manager finally judges an RMBS position, because neither a static yield nor an OAS says whether the investment will meet the portfolio's goals over its horizon. The total dollars come from the projected cash flows, the interest on reinvesting them and the price at the horizon, and the last is the hard part. The horizon price of a CMO tranche depends on its quality at that date, and quality can change: prepayments can turn a PAC into something closer to a sequential tranche, or widen its effective collar. A common approach is constant-OAS total return, which prices the bond at the horizon at the same OAS it was bought at; active managers instead take a view that OAS will widen or tighten and run the analysis under rate shocks of, say, plus and minus 150 basis points.

Chapter 21: Convertible Bonds

A convertible bond gives the investor the right to exchange it for a fixed number of the issuer's shares, the conversion ratio; an exchangeable bond can be swapped for shares of a different company. The conversion price is par divided by the conversion ratio: a $1,000 bond convertible into 50 shares has a conversion price of $20 a share. A convertible is worth at least the higher of two values: its conversion value, the shares' market value, and its straight value, what it would be worth as an ordinary bond. If it traded below straight value, investors would buy it as a bond; if it traded below conversion value, they would buy and convert at once, so arbitrage keeps it above both floors.

The analysis of a convertible starts from a few ratios. The market conversion price is the convertible's price divided by the conversion ratio: a bond at $950 convertible into 50 shares buys stock at an effective $19 a share. With the stock at $17, the market conversion premium is $2 a share, the price of the embedded call option. The premium payback period divides that premium by the extra income the bond pays over the stock's dividend per share; at $1 a year, the premium is recovered in two years, ignoring the time value of money. The premium over straight value shows how far the bond could fall before hitting its bond floor, though that floor moves with interest rates and with the issuer's credit.

Plain English

The trade-off of a convertible, with the chapter's numbers. You can buy 50 shares at $17 ($850) or one convertible for $950 that turns into 50 shares whenever you like. The extra $100, $2 a share, is what you pay for the protection.

You give up some of the upside and keep most of the protection, because if the stock collapses the convertible is still a bond that pays interest and returns $1,000 at maturity. The catch is the word "still": if the stock collapses because the company is in trouble, the bond value falls too, and the floor you were counting on isn't where you thought.

Several special features have appeared. With net share settlement, the issuer pays par in cash on conversion and only the excess in shares; before accounting rules changed in 2008, about $120 billion of the $171 billion U.S. convertible market had it. Contingent convertibles (CoCos), in this corporate sense, can be converted only after the stock has traded above about 130% of the conversion price for a set period; by 2003 they made up over 70% of new issues. Contingent payment convertibles pay extra interest if the stock reaches a threshold. Issuers can also call convertibles to force conversion once the stock is high, ending the investor's premium, and a takeover can leave the holder with a low-coupon bond in a company whose shares no longer trade.

Convertible traders borrow the language of options. Delta measures sensitivity to the stock price, from 0 for a bond that behaves like straight debt to 1 for one that moves with the stock; it is also the hedge ratio, so a convertible with a delta of 0.60 and a conversion ratio of 50 is hedged by shorting 30 shares. Gamma is the change in delta as the stock moves, the equity version of convexity, highest when the stock is near the conversion price, and it forces frequent rebalancing of a hedge. Vega is sensitivity to the stock's implied volatility; when implied volatility is below what the stock actually does, the embedded option is cheap.

Putting the pieces together, a first approximation of a convertible's value is its straight value plus the value of a call option on the shares, priced with Black-Scholes or a binomial model and driven above all by the stock's expected volatility. The approximation misses the issuer's call: if a bond convertible into shares worth $1,700 is called at $1,030, the investor's best move is to convert, and any premium above conversion value vanishes. Black-Scholes cannot handle that interplay, but a binomial model can value the investor's conversion option, the issuer's call and any put at once, and can link stock prices to interest rates. The market itself is diverse, covering cash-pay bonds, zero-coupon LYONs, original-issue-discount bonds, convertible preferreds and mandatory convertibles, which convert automatically at maturity.

Convertibles fall into four profiles. Equity-sensitive convertibles are deep in the money, with deltas above about 75%, and move with the stock. Balanced convertibles carry conversion premiums of roughly 15–40% and deltas of 55–75%, and appeal to both bond and equity funds. Busted convertibles are so far out of the money that they trade like ordinary bonds and are valued on yield and spread. Distressed convertibles are busted bonds whose issuers are near default. Because analytical duration captures only the bond part, empirical duration, measured by regression on actual price behavior, is essential.

“Increased debt market volatility has driven home the point of duration risk inherent in any security with a fixed income component, including converts. The increased volatility of the spreads has heightened investor sensitivity to the reliability of the fixed income floor or bond value of the convert.” — Mihir Bhattacharya

Convertible arbitrage is a whole hedge fund strategy, and Stefanini identifies eight versions. In cash flow arbitrage, the classic trade, the fund buys the convertible and shorts the stock in proportion to delta, earning the bond's coupon plus interest on the short sale proceeds minus the stock's dividends, with little exposure to the stock's direction. Volatility trading profits from the stock moving a lot in either direction, by rebalancing the hedge as delta changes. Gamma trading positions for expected changes in delta. The others are credit arbitrage, skewed arbitrage, carry trades, refinancing plays and late-stage restructuring plays. The ideal candidate is liquid, has a low conversion premium, high convexity, cheap implied volatility, a volatile stock that is easy to borrow, and little or no dividend.

Plain English

The classic convertible hedge, step by step. A fund buys a convertible that turns into 50 shares and has a delta of 0.60, meaning that for a $1 move in the stock, the bond moves about $30 (0.60 × 50). To cancel that, the fund sells short 30 shares. Now if the stock rises $1, the bond gains about $30 and the short loses $30; if it falls $1, the reverse. The fund is roughly indifferent to the stock's direction.

What it earns is the bond's coupon, plus interest on the cash from the short sale, minus any dividends it owes on the borrowed shares. And because the bond has positive gamma, big moves help: after a large rise the bond's delta goes up, so the fund sells more stock high; after a large fall delta drops, so it buys stock back low. Choppy stocks make this trade money; quiet ones don't.

The deeper lesson is that a convertible is a hybrid, and each half can surprise. The equity half gives upside but its value depends on the stock's volatility, which can collapse. The bond half gives a floor, but the floor depends on interest rates, which can rise, and on the issuer's credit, which tends to deteriorate just when the stock is falling and the floor is needed most. Convertible investors therefore need both a credit analyst's view of the issuer and an option trader's view of volatility, and the rise of hedge funds as the main buyers has made convertible prices sensitive to how easily those funds can borrow stock and finance their positions.

Chapter 22: Corporate Credit Analysis

Primer

Credit analysts lean on a few income-statement terms. EBIT is earnings before interest and taxes, roughly what the business earns from operations before paying its lenders and the government. EBITDA also adds back depreciation and amortization, accounting charges that don't use cash this year, so it is closer to the cash the operations produce, though it ignores the money needed to replace worn-out equipment. Capex, capital expenditure, is that spending on plant and equipment. Free cash flow is roughly the cash left after running the business and paying for capex, the money actually available to service debt.

Corporate credit analysis covers three areas: the covenants that limit what management can do, the collateral available if the company defaults, and the company's ability to pay, which depends on its business, its governance and its finances. The aim is to judge all three parts of credit risk, default, spread widening and downgrade. Fabozzi warns that many experienced investors skip the first step, understanding the business model, and go straight to the ratios. Before any financial analysis come three questions: What does the company do? Where does its revenue come from? Where does it incur its costs? The Sino-Forest case at the end of the chapter shows what happens when nobody asks them.

Industry analysis provides the context, and Fabozzi uses Michael Porter's five forces: rivalry among existing competitors, the threat of new entrants, the bargaining power of suppliers, the bargaining power of buyers, and the threat of substitutes. An industry with fierce rivalry, low barriers, powerful customers and easy substitutes is a hard place to earn steady cash. Analysis must also be relative: 20% annual revenue growth sounds strong until you learn that the industry is growing 45%. Analysts weigh the industry's sensitivity to the economic cycle, its growth prospects, its dependence on research spending, its market structure, its labor relations, and the direction of regulatory change, which matters more than whether regulation exists at all.

Corporate governance is about the agency problem between managers and owners, and it has a twist for bondholders. Remedies include giving managers equity, tying pay to performance, clawing back bonuses after restatements, appointing independent directors, separating the chairman and chief executive, and keeping the audit committee independent; S&P scores governance from 10 down to 1, and Moody's judges management on strategy, financial philosophy, conservatism, track record, succession and controls. But shareholders benefit from risk, since their upside is unlimited, while bondholders' upside is capped at par and coupon. So governance that is "shareholder-friendly," buybacks funded with debt, special dividends, leveraged acquisitions, is often bondholder-unfriendly.

Covenants are the bondholder's defense. Affirmative covenants require the company to do things, such as maintain insurance and file reports; negative covenants forbid things, such as taking on debt beyond a limit, making restricted payments to shareholders, pledging assets to other lenders or selling key assets. Maintenance tests require ratios to be met at every reporting date, while incurrence tests apply only when the company takes an action such as borrowing more, so they are weaker. Subsidiaries are classed as restricted, inside the covenant net, or unrestricted, outside it, and the carve-outs and exceptions often reveal what management intends to do.

“Covenants provide insight into a company’s strategy. As part of the credit process, one must read covenants within the context of the corporate strategy. It is not sufficient to hire a lawyer to review the covenants because a lawyer might miss the critical factors necessary to make the appropriate decision. Also, loopholes in covenants often provide clues about the intentions of management teams.” — Robert Levine, high-yield portfolio manager

The financial analysis rests on a handful of ratio families. Interest coverage, EBIT divided by gross interest expense, shows how many times operations cover interest; the EBITDA version is always higher but hides the need to replace equipment, and fixed-charge coverage adds leases and other fixed obligations. Leverage compares debt with capitalization, EBITDA or EBIT, with operating leases counted as debt. S&P's cash flow hierarchy steps down from operating cash flow to free operating cash flow after capex, to discretionary cash flow after dividends, to prefinancing cash flow after debt repayment, separating maintenance capex from growth capex. Working capital, the maturity schedule of debt, bank lines and their material-adverse-change clauses, and the value of assets relative to debt complete the picture.

Plain English

Two ratios do most of the work. A company earns $500 million a year before interest and taxes (EBIT) and pays $100 million of interest: coverage of 5 times. Profits could fall 80% and it would still cover its interest. Another company earns the same $500 million but pays $400 million of interest: coverage of 1.25. A 20% drop in profits and it can't pay its lenders from operations.

Leverage looks at the stock of debt instead of the flow of interest. A company with $2 billion of debt and $500 million of EBITDA has leverage of 4 times: it would take four years of cash earnings, before any spending, to pay off its debt. Investment-grade companies usually sit well below that; high-yield ones often above it. And since EBITDA ignores the cost of replacing equipment, an analyst checks free cash flow too, because that is what actually pays debt.

For high-yield bonds especially, Fabozzi argues for thinking like an equity analyst, a view associated with Stephen Esser: as a company's equity value rises, so does the cushion of equity beneath its debt, and the bonds become better credits. An equity view can confirm or contradict the traditional credit analysis and tell the analyst where to dig. Risk and reward in junk bonds are lopsided: the most a bond can return is its coupon and the pull to par, while a default can cost most of the principal, so extra yield from junior debt is rarely worth the extra risk. Only when a bond's price falls far below its intrinsic value does the asymmetry reverse, and a low price becomes the margin of safety.

The two case studies show the method at work. Sino-Forest called itself a leading commercial forest plantation operator, but it was really a middleman buying and selling trees through four or five layers of intermediaries, never dealing directly with suppliers or customers and hiring others to run even its own plantations. Despite fifteen years of double-digit revenue growth and EBITDA margins of 69%, it never produced positive free cash flow. Its products were commodities, its rivals many, its barriers to entry low. Valued at C$7.2 billion in March 2011, it filed for bankruptcy in March 2012, and bondholders expected to recover 10–15%. All three rating agencies had rated it BB and downgraded only after the stock collapsed.

Sirius XM shows covenants at work. The satellite radio company was a near-monopoly with 26 million subscribers, 85% of revenue from subscriptions, EBITDA margins near 33%, low maintenance capex and about $1 billion of annual free cash flow, a fine credit by the numbers. But its controlling shareholder, Liberty Media, liked leverage of 4 to 5 times, and the company wanted to raise leverage from 2.5 to 4 times to return cash to shareholders. Only its 5.25% bonds of 2022 carried a restricted-payments covenant blocking payouts above 3.5 times leverage. The company granted those bonds security, earning them investment-grade ratings that automatically suspended the covenant; the bonds jumped $4 and outperformed other unsecured bonds by 150–200 basis points, and $1.5 billion of new debt followed within a month.

Chapter 23: Credit Risk Modeling

The last chapter of Part IV moves from judgment to mathematics. Credit risk models try to turn what is known about a company, and what markets are saying through prices, into probabilities of default and fair credit spreads. Fabozzi presents two families. Structural models start from the company's balance sheet and explain default as the moment the value of its assets falls below what it owes. Reduced-form models skip the explanation and treat default as a random event arriving at some rate, which they read off market prices. Each has a use: structural models offer economic intuition, reduced-form models fit the market, and a third group tries to combine the two.

The structural approach began with Robert Merton in 1974. Shareholders own the firm's assets but owe its debt, so at the debt's maturity they will pay off the debt only if the assets are worth more than the debt; otherwise they hand the firm to the creditors. Equity is therefore a call option on the firm's assets, with a strike price equal to the face value of the debt. The debt, in turn, equals the assets minus that call, or equivalently a risk-free bond minus a put option on the assets. Option pricing then gives the value of the debt, its credit spread and its probability of default from the firm's asset value, the asset volatility, the debt and the risk-free rate.

Plain English

Merton's idea with numbers. A company's assets are worth $100 million and it owes $80 million, due in one year. If the assets are still worth more than $80 million next year, shareholders pay off the debt and keep the rest. If they're worth less, shareholders walk away and the lenders get the assets. That's exactly how a call option works, with the debt as the strike price.

Now feed in a 5% risk-free rate and asset values that swing about 30% a year. Option math values the shares at about $26.5 million and the debt at about $73.5 million, a yield of about 8.4% on debt that would yield 5% if it were safe: a credit spread of roughly 340 basis points. The model also puts the chance of the assets ending below $80 million at about 22%. Make the assets more volatile, or the debt bigger, and the spread widens on its own, which is the model's whole insight: credit risk is the price of the shareholders' option to walk away.

Later structural models relaxed Merton's assumptions. Geske handled debt maturing on several dates by treating the equity as a compound option, an option on an option, with each repayment a decision whether to keep the firm going. Black and Cox let default happen at any time, as soon as asset value crosses a barrier, a first-passage model closer to how companies actually fail between payment dates. Shimko, Tejima and van Deventer added random interest rates to capture the interaction between credit risk and rate risk. All of them share one serious weakness: they need the market value of the firm's assets and its volatility, which cannot be observed, so they must be backed out of the share price by iterative calibration that is slow and sensitive to assumptions.

Despite those flaws, structural models offer something the alternatives lack: an economic story. They show credit risk as a function of two things management controls or investors can watch, leverage and asset volatility, and they can show how a new share issue or bond issue changes the risk of the existing debt. Their practical problems pile up, though. For coupon bonds, valuation becomes a compound option problem; for a subordinated bond, all the senior debt above it must be valued at the same time; for a portfolio, the correlation between issuers' asset values must be estimated. That makes them unsuitable for the frequent marking-to-market that trading desks need, which is why the market turned elsewhere.

Reduced-form models avoid that problem. Following Jarrow and Turnbull and Duffie and Singleton, they treat default as a random event arriving at a time-varying intensity, the conditional probability of default per unit of time, without asking why it happens. The intensity is calibrated from market credit spreads. Jarrow-Turnbull assumes recovery happens at maturity as a fraction of par; Jarrow, Lando and Turnbull extend it to rating migration using transition matrices; Duffie-Singleton lets recovery happen at any time as a fraction of the bond's pre-default price, a more flexible assumption that has become the standard. Because each maturity can be priced consistently, these models produce credit curves, term structures of default probability.

The mathematics behind reduced-form models is the Poisson process, the same tool used to model the arrival of rare random events. In each short interval of time, default either happens, with probability equal to the intensity times the length of the interval, or does not. If the intensity is constant, the time until default follows an exponential distribution, and the probability of surviving to a future date is e raised to minus the intensity times the time. Because default is treated as determined outside the model, exogenous, bonds of different maturities can be priced independently, unlike in structural models, where a long bond's fate depends on the firm surviving its shorter debts. The intensity can also be allowed to move with interest rates, so that credit and rate risk interact.

Plain English

A reduced-form model reads default risk straight off the spread. Say a company's bonds yield 2 percentage points more than Treasuries and investors expect to recover 40 cents on the dollar if it defaults, so a default costs them 60 cents. The market is then charging roughly 2% a year for an event that costs 60%, which implies a default intensity of about 2 ÷ 60 = 3.3% a year.

The model doesn't know or care why the company might fail. It just treats default as something that strikes at random at that rate, the way an insurer treats house fires. That makes it easy to fit to market prices and to compare bonds: if one company's spread implies a 3% default rate and your own analysis says 1%, its bonds may be cheap.

A third group, incomplete-information models, blends the two. In the Giesecke-Goldberg model, used by MSCI Barra, investors do not know exactly where the default barrier lies, a realistic assumption in a world of corporate fraud like Enron, WorldCom and Sino-Forest, where the true state of the balance sheet is hidden. Default then arrives as a surprise even in a structural model. The evidence on the competing tools is pointed. Jens Hilscher and Mungo Wilson find that credit ratings are poor measures of default probability and that simple reduced-form models forecast corporate failure better. In practice, more than 90% of sophisticated users choose the reduced-form approach, because it is quick to calibrate and works for thousands of issuers at once.

Fabozzi's illustration comes from Kamakura Corporation's analysis of Bank of America in June 2014. Comparing the credit spread on each bond with its model default probability gives a measure of reward per unit of risk. Of 431 bank bonds analyzed, Bank of America's 15 issues ranked in the bottom 17% on that measure, showing that even the most heavily traded names can be poor value. That is the practical payoff of the models: a bond whose spread is high relative to its default probability is potentially cheap and one whose spread is low is potentially rich. Structural models remain useful for fundamental analysis, showing how leverage and asset volatility drive risk, while reduced-form models dominate pricing, hedging and portfolio screening.

It covers the spectrum of strategies from indexing to active bets, how portfolios are built and their risk measured against a benchmark, the special problems of corporate bond portfolios, managing against liabilities rather than an index, and finally how performance is measured and explained. Chapter 24: Bond Portfolio Management Strategies

Bond strategies run along a spectrum from passive to active. Pure indexing tries to replicate a benchmark exactly. Enhanced indexing tracks the benchmark closely but allows small mismatches in risk factors to add a little return. Active management takes larger bets on rates, curve, sectors or credits based on the manager's views. Absolute return strategies aim for positive returns whatever the market does, and liability-driven strategies manage against a set of future payments rather than an index. Many investors combine them in a core-satellite structure: a low-cost indexed core plus active satellites in sectors where managers have an edge. Across studies, the allocation among asset classes remains the most important driver of results.

Bond benchmarks are flawed in ways stock benchmarks are not. An index's duration is set by issuers, who borrow at whatever maturities are cheapest for them, not by investors, so the benchmark's interest-rate risk is, in Fabozzi's phrase, a historical accident. Market-value weighting also creates the bum's problem: the more a company borrows, the bigger its weight in the index, so the index leans toward the most indebted issuers, the opposite of a stock index, where size reflects value created. Goltz and Campani found cap-weighted investment-grade indexes "fairly unstable" in their risk exposures from 1997 to 2010. Alternatives include issuer caps, GDP weighting for government bonds, duration weighting, laddered indexes and fundamental weighting by economic size.

Active strategies are bets on risk factors. Systematic factors affect the whole market and cannot be diversified away; nonsystematic factors are specific to a security or issuer. Systematic factors divide into term structure risks, the level, slope and curvature of the yield curve, and non-term-structure risks, such as sector spreads, credit quality and embedded options. An interest-rate expectations strategy simply moves duration above or below the benchmark's on a forecast of rates. Fabozzi is skeptical: the academic evidence does not show that managers can consistently forecast rates, and a manager behind the benchmark near year-end has an incentive to take a duration bet to "save the account," an agency problem clients should watch for.

Yield curve strategies position a portfolio along the curve. A bullet portfolio concentrates on one maturity; a barbell splits between short and long maturities; a ladder spreads money evenly across many maturities. In Fabozzi's example, a bullet of 10-year bonds yielding 9.25% and a barbell of 5-year and 20-year bonds with the same dollar duration differ in two ways: the barbell has more convexity, 71.78 against 55.45, but yields 25.2 basis points less. Over a six-month horizon, the bullet wins when yields move less than about 100 basis points in a parallel shift, and the barbell wins on bigger moves. When the curve flattens the barbell usually wins, and when it steepens the bullet usually wins.

Plain English

Why would anyone accept a lower yield for the barbell? Because of convexity. Both portfolios have the same duration, so a small parallel move in rates affects them about equally, and the bullet's extra 25 basis points of yield makes it the winner. But the barbell's price curve bends more, so on a big move in either direction it gains more or loses less than the bullet. The extra yield on the bullet is, in effect, what the market pays you for giving up that cushion.

So the choice is a bet on volatility and on the shape of the curve. Expect calm markets with rates moving little: own the bullet and collect the yield. Expect big moves, or a flattening where long rates fall relative to the middle: own the barbell. The ladder is the "I don't know" choice, spreading the bets across the whole curve. Fabozzi's lesson is that yield, duration and convexity alone don't tell you which will win; you have to run the scenarios.

Duration still dominates. Because parallel shifts and twists explain most Treasury returns, getting duration right matters more than anything else in a high-quality portfolio, and the book quotes a blunt verdict on it. For curve risk the tool is the key rate duration profile: in one example, a portfolio with a duration of 4.124 against a benchmark's 4.491 has less exposure to the 10- and 30-year points and more to the five- to ten-year sector, a curve bet that the single duration number hides. Yield spread strategies trade the spreads between sectors, such as an intermarket swap when a spread is out of line with history, and they must keep dollar duration unchanged so that the trade is a pure spread bet rather than a hidden rate bet.

Other active strategies work bond by bond. Individual security selection looks for issues that are undervalued, because they yield more than similar bonds or because their credit is likely to improve. A substitution swap exchanges one bond for a nearly identical one with a higher yield, exploiting temporary imbalances in fragmented markets, with the risk that the two bonds are not really identical and the yield gap is simply the price of worse convexity. In mortgages, managers hunt for mispriced pass-throughs, CMO classes and strips under their own prepayment forecasts, and combine them into packages that do better than a single security of similar duration. Rate views can also be expressed through embedded options: a manager expecting rates to fall avoids high-coupon callable bonds, whose upside is capped.

“There is no way for a portfolio manager to earn substantial returns on a fixed-rate bond portfolio without bearing risk. Duration is the primary source of risk and return. All else is a footnote—occasionally an important footnote, but a footnote nonetheless.” — Frank J. Fabozzi

Leverage magnifies everything. A manager with $50 million of capital who borrows $150 million holds $200 million of bonds; if they yield 5% and the borrowing costs 4.5%, the 50 basis point spread is earned four times over on the capital. Duration is magnified the same way: $100 million of capital invested in $400 million of bonds with a duration of 3 has a duration of 12 on the capital, so a 100 basis point rise wipes out 12% of it. Most bond leverage comes through repo, protected by haircuts, daily marking to market and tri-party custody. A leveraged manager must watch funding liquidity, the ability to roll the repo, market liquidity, the ability to sell, and correlations that appear only in a crisis.

“Leverage is a double-edged sword. The collapse of Long-Term Capital Management in 1998—with 25:1 leverage on a $4.7 billion equity base—demonstrated that even Nobel Prize–winning quantitative models cannot protect against illiquidity and correlated spread widening when leverage is excessive.” — Frank J. Fabozzi
Plain English

Leverage with the chapter's numbers. You have $50 million. You buy $200 million of bonds yielding 5%, borrowing $150 million at 4.5%. The bonds earn $10 million a year; the loan costs $6.75 million; you keep $3.25 million, a 6.5% return on your $50 million, instead of 5% without borrowing.

Now the bonds fall 2% in price. That's a $4 million loss, 8% of your capital, wiping out more than a year of the extra income. A bigger fall, or the lender asking for more collateral at the same moment, can force you to sell at the bottom, which is how LTCM went down. Leverage turns a small, steady edge into a big one, and a normal bad month into a dangerous one.

Strategies are tested on history, and testing has its own traps. Walk-forward backtesting, the most common, assumes the future will repeat the past exactly; resampling tests many reshuffled versions of history; Monte Carlo simulation generates synthetic futures, an approach Marco López de Prado favors. All three can be ruined by survivorship bias, testing only on bonds that survived, look-ahead bias, using information not available at the time, and cherry-picking the test period. Smart beta brings factor investing from stocks to bonds, but Felix Goltz of EDHEC warns there is a long way to go: a minimum-volatility bond portfolio simply ends up in short, high-quality bonds, which may not be what the investor wants at all.

Chapter 25: Bond Portfolio Construction

Portfolio construction starts from Harry Markowitz's mean-variance framework: choose the mix of assets that gives the highest expected return for each level of risk, measured by the variance of returns. In bonds, the framework has been used mainly for allocation across broad asset classes rather than for picking individual bonds, because estimating the expected returns, variances and correlations of thousands of bonds is impractical, and the estimates are noisy enough that the optimizer amplifies their errors, a problem known as estimation risk. Two further criticisms apply: returns are not normally distributed, so variance misses the skew and fat tails of credit losses, and a manager judged against a benchmark should care about risk relative to that benchmark, not total variance.

That relative risk is tracking error, the standard deviation of the active return, the portfolio's return minus the benchmark's. It is computed from a series of periods and annualized by the square root of time: a monthly tracking error of 9.30 basis points becomes about 32 basis points a year, a close tracker; one of 79.13 basis points a month becomes about 274 a year, a portfolio taking big bets. Backward-looking tracking error measures what happened; forward-looking tracking error, estimated from a risk model and today's holdings, measures what could happen, and it changes the moment a manager changes the portfolio, which makes it the tool for controlling risk. A passive portfolio aims for a forward tracking error near zero.

Plain English

Tracking error is the range around the benchmark you should expect to land in. Say a portfolio's forward-looking tracking error is 1 percentage point and the benchmark returns 10% this year. Roughly two years out of three, the portfolio should return between 9% and 11%. Nineteen years out of twenty, between 8% and 12%. Rarely, outside 7% to 13%.

So a client who hires an "enhanced index" manager and sees a tracking error of 3 points has hired someone making much bigger bets than advertised. And a manager can use the number before trading: if a planned trade would push tracking error past the client's limit, the risk model says so immediately.

Bond portfolios carry more idiosyncratic risk than stock portfolios. About 25 randomly chosen stocks remove most company-specific risk, but a bond portfolio needs many more names, and against a benchmark of 6,500 or so bonds, a practical portfolio of a few dozen positions is dominated by issuer risk. In a Barclays example, a 50-position portfolio measured against a benchmark of 5,693 bonds had 87% of its tracking error from idiosyncratic risk. The reason is the asymmetry of credit: a single issuer's default or downgrade can cost far more than any single bond can gain, so even a small overweight in one name adds disproportionate risk relative to a broadly diversified index.

The traditional way to build an indexed or enhanced portfolio is the cell-based approach. The benchmark is divided into cells by characteristics such as duration, maturity, sector, credit quality, call features and sinking funds; with two duration bands, three maturity bands, four sectors and four quality grades there are 2 × 3 × 4 × 4 = 96 cells. A passive manager buys bonds in each cell in proportion to the cell's weight in the index, so if corporates are 30% of the index, they are 30% of the portfolio. An active manager deliberately overweights or underweights cells, for example favoring corporates over Treasuries. Small portfolios, under about $100 million, cannot fill many cells without buying odd lots, so they use fewer cells and accept more tracking error.

Pure bond indexing, Fabozzi explains, is almost impossible. A bond index contains thousands of issues, many of which rarely trade and some of which cannot be found at any reasonable price. The index changes every month as new bonds are added and others are dropped for maturing, shrinking or being downgraded, which forces turnover and trading costs on anyone replicating it. Index prices come from pricing services, while the manager must trade at real prices, so even a perfect copy of the holdings would not match the index return. Coupons must be reinvested, and the index assumes reinvestment the manager cannot exactly match. Indexing in bonds therefore means sampling, accepting small, controlled differences from the benchmark.

Modern managers use multi-factor risk models instead. They describe each bond by its exposure to systematic factors, the level, slope and curvature of the Treasury or swap curve, swap spreads, volatility, sector and quality spreads, and attribute the rest to issuer- and issue-specific risk. Comparing a portfolio's factor exposures with the benchmark's, and combining them through the factors' variances and correlations, gives forward-looking tracking error broken down by source. The model can show, for instance, that an enhanced index portfolio with matching sector weights still has meaningful risk from key rate mismatches along the curve, from swap-spread exposure, or from a few concentrated credits. Sector allocation alone, Fabozzi stresses, is not enough to judge risk.

The output of such a model is a risk report. It lists the portfolio's and benchmark's characteristics side by side, the tracking error from each factor group in isolation and in total after correlations, the contribution of each asset class, and the portfolio's beta to the benchmark. Isolated risks do not simply add up: a curve exposure and a spread exposure that tend to move in opposite directions partly cancel, which is why the variance-covariance matrix of factors is at the heart of the calculation. For the manager, the payoff is control: every intended trade can be run through the model first, its effect on tracking error seen in advance, and trades that would take the portfolio outside its risk budget rejected before they are made.

Curve risk gets special attention in these reports. A portfolio's exposure can be measured against the Treasury curve or the swap curve, and the two give different pictures, so the model also tracks swap spread risk, the chance that swap rates move relative to Treasuries. Comparing the portfolio's key rate durations with the benchmark's, point by point along the curve, shows where it is long or short, and multiplying each mismatch by a hypothetical move at that point gives the return impact of, say, a steepening or a flattening. An enhanced index portfolio might match the benchmark's overall duration exactly yet be long the five-year point and short the thirty-year, a hidden curve bet that would pay in a flattening and cost in a steepening. The report's job is to make such bets visible and deliberate.

Chapter 26: Managing Corporate Bond Portfolios

Corporate bond portfolios are shaped by one fact: a bond's upside is capped at its coupons and par, while its downside can be most of its principal. That makes credit returns asymmetric. Removing the worst 10% of performers from a corporate bond index improves its return a great deal; removing the best 10% barely changes it, the opposite of a stock index, where a few big winners drive the total. The practical conclusion runs through the whole chapter: in credit, avoiding losers matters far more than picking winners. Diversification across issuers, careful credit analysis and selling deteriorating credits early are the core skills, more than finding the next upgrade.

Researchers have tried to apply equity-style factor investing to corporate bonds. Value favors bonds whose spreads are wide relative to fundamentals. Momentum favors bonds with strong recent excess returns, perhaps because news spreads slowly in illiquid markets. Low risk, or quality, finds that higher-quality bonds earn better returns for their volatility. Size looks for a premium in smaller issuers, with mixed evidence. Carry favors bonds with more spread per unit of duration. Caution is warranted: bonds mature, trade rarely and have lopsided payoffs, factor returns are more cyclical than in stocks, and wide trading costs can eat much of the premium.

Corporate bond benchmarks inherit the problems of Chapter 24 in concentrated form. Their duration is set by issuers, their risk drifts, and the bum's problem gives the heaviest weights to the heaviest borrowers. Their composition also changes constantly: new bonds qualify, old ones mature or are called, put or converted, and bonds drop out when they are downgraded, shrink or fall below the maturity cutoff, while others join when upgraded to investment grade. Goltz and Campani, studying U.S. and European indexes from 1997 to 2010, concluded that the swings in interest-rate and credit exposure were incompatible with investors' need for stable exposures, since an allocation decision made one year can mean something quite different a few years later without anyone deciding to change it.

The chapter's most useful risk measure is duration times spread (DTS), the spread duration multiplied by the credit spread. Research shows that spread changes are roughly proportional to the spread itself, so a bond at 400 basis points moves far more in a sell-off than one at 100, and the volatility of excess returns rises in line with DTS across quality groups, in U.S. investment grade, high yield and European credit alike. An A-rated bond with a 100 basis point spread and a duration of 5 has a DTS of 500; a BB bond with a 400 basis point spread and a duration of 2 has a DTS of 800 and more spread risk, despite its shorter duration. A portfolio's DTS is the weighted sum of its bonds'.

Plain English

Why multiply duration by spread? Because risky bonds' spreads move in bigger jumps. In a sell-off, spreads tend to widen by a similar percentage, not by a similar number of basis points. If all spreads widen by a fifth, the A-rated bond goes from 100 to 120 basis points, a 20-point move, and with a duration of 5 it loses about 1%. The BB bond goes from 400 to 480, an 80-point move, and with a duration of 2 it loses about 1.6%.

Spread duration alone said the A-rated bond (5) was riskier than the BB bond (2). DTS, 500 against 800, gets the order right: the BB bond lost more. That's why managers budget credit risk in DTS rather than in duration.

High-yield bonds behave partly like stocks, so their analytical duration misleads. When Treasury yields fall in a flight to quality, credit spreads usually widen, so the two partly cancel, and a high-yield bond's price reacts less to Treasury yields than its duration suggests. Fabozzi captures this with a duration multiplier, M = 1 + ρ(σs/σy), where ρ is the usually negative correlation between yields and spreads. Estimated multipliers run about 0.85 for A-rated bonds, 0.63 for BBB consumer cyclicals, and near zero or even negative for Ba and below, where bonds trade like equity. A BBB cyclical with a modified duration of 5 behaves as if its duration were 3.15, and getting this wrong can push a portfolio's duration well off target.

Credit managers make money through a set of relative value trades. Yield-spread pickup swaps into a higher spread, keeping dollar duration the same. Credit-upside trades buy likely upgrades before the market sees them; credit-defense trades cut exposure to deteriorating issuers and sectors. New-issue swaps move into new bonds, which often come cheap and trade more actively. Sector rotation shifts weights with the economic cycle; curve-adjustment trades reshape duration; and structure trades swap among bullet, callable and putable bonds when their options look mispriced. Each is a specific, testable view rather than a general hope that credit will do well.

Credit models can drive portfolio construction directly. Moody's Analytics uses the structural model of Chapter 23 to estimate each issuer's expected default frequency (EDF) and a fair-value spread reflecting default risk, recovery risk, the market's risk premium and maturity. A risk-based measure divides a bond's OAS by its expected loss, EDF times loss given default, giving spread per unit of expected loss; a valuation-based measure divides the gap between the OAS and the fair-value spread by the same expected loss. Ranking bonds on these measures and overweighting the most attractive produced, in tests from 1999 to 2009 that included trading costs, higher returns and lower volatility than the index: for investment grade, annual excess returns of about 1.1 to 3.1 percentage points depending on concentration, and for high yield, about 6.7 to 7.7.

Chapter 27: Liability-Driven Investing

Some investors care less about beating an index than about meeting promises: a pension fund owes benefits, an insurer owes policy payments, a lottery owes a winner. For them the benchmark is the liability itself, and the classic tool is immunization, introduced by Frank Redington in 1952. The idea uses the two opposing risks of Chapter 1. When rates rise, bond prices fall but coupons are reinvested at higher rates; when rates fall, prices rise but reinvestment earns less. If the portfolio's duration equals the time until the liability is due, the two effects offset, and the portfolio reaches the target value whichever way rates move, at least for a single parallel shift.

Plain English

Here is immunization working. You must pay $1,276.28 in five years for each $1,000 you have today, which is what $1,000 grows to at 5% a year. Rates are 5%, and right after you invest they jump to a new level and stay there. Here's what each choice is worth at year five:

The 5-year bond falls short when rates drop, because its coupons are reinvested at less. The 10-year bond falls short when rates rise, because you have to sell it at a lower price in year five. The bond whose duration matches the five-year horizon (here a 6-year bond with a high coupon) hits the target either way, even a few cents above, because the price effect and the reinvestment effect cancel.

Immunization has conditions. The portfolio's duration must equal the liability's horizon, the present value of the assets must be at least the present value of the liability, and the assets' cash flows should be spread around the liability date, with some before and some after. And it must be rebalanced: duration drifts as time passes and as rates move, and not always at the same speed as the horizon shortens, so the portfolio has to be adjusted periodically to keep its duration matched. The biggest weakness is the assumption of parallel shifts. If short and long rates move differently, a portfolio with the right duration can still miss its target, because its cash flows are spread across the curve differently from the liability.

That residual risk is measured by M-squared, the dispersion of the portfolio's cash flows around the liability date, weighted by their present values. A zero-coupon bond maturing exactly on the liability date has an M-squared of zero and is the perfect immunizing asset: no reinvestment, no sale before maturity, no exposure to the curve's shape. A bullet portfolio, with cash flows bunched near the horizon, has less immunization risk than a barbell with the same duration, whose cash flows are spread far before and after it. Ronald Ryan's approach takes the logic to its end, building the portfolio entirely from Treasury STRIPS chosen to match each projected payment, eliminating reinvestment risk altogether.

Real liabilities are a stream, not one payment. Multi-period immunization extends the method to a series of payments, with conditions on the duration and dispersion of the assets relative to the whole stream. Cash flow matching is the alternative: start with the last liability, buy a bond that pays it, subtract that bond's coupons from the earlier liabilities, then match the next-to-last, and so on back to the first. Matching needs no duration calculation and no rebalancing, and it has no immunization risk unless a bond defaults. But it costs more, typically 3–7% more than immunization, because requiring cash to arrive on exact dates rules out many cheaper bonds. A common compromise matches the near-term payments and immunizes the distant ones.

Contingent immunization combines active management with a safety net. The client accepts a minimum return below what full immunization would lock in today. The gap gives the manager a cushion: as long as the portfolio's value stays above the amount needed to immunize the remaining liability at current rates, the immunization floor, the manager can trade actively. If poor decisions or bad markets shrink the cushion to zero, the manager must immunize the portfolio immediately, locking in the minimum. The arrangement gives the client a guaranteed floor and the chance of more, and it needs constant monitoring of the gap between the portfolio's value and the floor.

Pension funds have pushed liability-driven investing furthest, because a defined benefit plan's liabilities behave like long bonds: when rates fall, their present value rises, and a plan holding stocks can find its funding ratio collapse even in a good year for markets. LDI portfolios therefore hold long-duration bonds and use swaps and futures to extend duration further, so that assets and liabilities move together, and they often shift from return-seeking to hedging assets as the plan's funding improves, a glide path. The goal moves from maximizing returns to keeping the plan's surplus stable, and the relevant risk becomes surplus volatility rather than the volatility of the assets alone.

Immunization has other limits worth knowing. The target it locks in is not simply the portfolio's yield: with an upward-sloping curve and coupons reinvested along it, the achievable return differs from any single yield figure, and only total return analysis shows it. Default and downgrade risk can break the promise, so immunized portfolios are built from high-quality bonds. Call and prepayment risk can break it too, since a called bond returns its principal early and changes the portfolio's duration overnight, which is why immunizers prefer noncallable bonds and avoid mortgages. And every rebalancing has a cost, so managers trade off the precision of frequent adjustment against the trading expense, rebalancing when the duration gap exceeds a tolerance rather than continuously.

Sponsors who want out entirely have several routes. They can freeze the plan, hard (no further benefits accrue), partial or soft (no new members), or terminate it. They can transfer the risk: a lump-sum buyout pays participants cash for their benefits; an annuity buy-in has the plan buy a group annuity from an insurer as an asset while keeping the liability; an annuity buy-out moves the liability to the insurer entirely, so participants become the insurer's policyholders. A longevity swap hedges the risk that pensioners live longer than assumed. Unfunded pensions, Fabozzi notes, are a growing source of stress for both corporate and municipal borrowers, as Detroit showed in Chapter 9.

Chapter 28: Performance Measurement

Fabozzi separates performance measurement, calculating the return a manager earned, from performance evaluation, judging whether the manager added value against a benchmark and how. A good process must be accurate, recognizing the timing of cash flows as they actually happen; informative, explaining results in terms of the manager's decisions; and simple enough for managers, clients and trustees to understand. For a single period the return is easy: the change in market value plus any distributions, divided by the starting value. A portfolio worth $25 million at the start and $28 million at the end, having paid out $1 million, returned 16%. The difficulty begins over longer periods, when clients add and withdraw money.

The answer is to compute returns over short subperiods, monthly or quarterly, and average them, but there are three ways to average. The arithmetic average is the simple mean, and it can mislead badly. A portfolio that rises from $28 million to $56 million in one month (+100%) and falls back to $28 million in the next (−50%) shows an arithmetic average of 25% a month, although the investor made nothing. The arithmetic average is properly interpreted as the average amount that could be withdrawn each period while keeping the starting value intact, which is not what clients usually want to know. It is always at least as high as the time-weighted return, and the gap grows with the volatility of the subperiod returns.

The time-weighted return, or geometric return, compounds the subperiod returns: [(1 + R1)(1 + R2)…(1 + RN)]1/N − 1. In the example it gives exactly zero, as it should. For monthly returns of 4%, 6%, 2% and −2%, the arithmetic average is 2.5% and the time-weighted 2.46%, close because the returns vary little. The time-weighted return measures the growth of a dollar invested at the start, and because it ignores the timing of client contributions and withdrawals, which the manager does not control, it is the right measure for judging a manager. The dollar-weighted return, the internal rate of return of all the cash flows, reflects that timing, so two managers making identical decisions can show different dollar-weighted returns.

Fabozzi's example makes the point. Managers A and B run $10 million and $200 million and invest identically, earning 20% in the first month and 50% in the second. At the start of the second month the plan sponsor withdraws $4 million from A. A's dollar-weighted return comes out well below B's, because A had less money at work during the stronger second month, though their decisions were exactly the same. The dollar-weighted return still has a use, describing how a client's money grew, but not for judging the manager. Returns over several periods are then annualized by compounding: a 2% average monthly return is (1.02)12 − 1, or 26.8% a year, and a 3% average quarterly return is (1.03)4 − 1, or 12.6%.

Plain English

Why time-weighted for managers and dollar-weighted for clients? Two clients hire the same manager for the same two years. The manager loses 10% in year one and gains 30% in year two. Client A invests $1 million at the start and leaves it. Client B invests $1 million at the start and adds another $5 million at the end of the bad first year.

Both clients got the same manager and the same decisions, so the manager's time-weighted return is the same for both: 0.90 × 1.30 = 1.17, about 8.2% a year. But client B put most of the money in just before the good year, so B's own dollar-weighted return is far higher than A's. B's good luck in timing says nothing about the manager. That's why managers are compared on time-weighted returns, and why the global performance standards require them.

Reporting standards exist because performance reporting was once abused, with managers showing their best accounts and best periods. The Global Investment Performance Standards (GIPS) of the CFA Institute set rules for how returns are calculated, how accounts are grouped into composites, and how results are presented, so that clients can compare managers fairly. Choosing a benchmark is the next decision, and it shapes everything that follows. Broad indexes such as the Bloomberg Barclays U.S. Aggregate are the most common; pension funds and insurers may use custom liability benchmarks built from their projected payments; and some clients use normal portfolios, customized benchmarks holding the securities a manager normally chooses from, in typical weights.

Normal portfolios matter because the wrong benchmark makes skill impossible to see. A manager who specializes in short, high-quality corporates will look brilliant against a long Treasury index in a year when rates rise and dreadful in a year when they fall, and neither verdict says anything about skill. A normal portfolio controls for the manager's style, so that the remaining difference reflects decisions rather than the style itself. Bailey and Tierney warned that benchmarks can also be gamed: a manager allowed to help pick the benchmark has every reason to choose one that is easy to beat. Single-number measures such as the Sharpe and information ratios cannot catch this, because they say how well a manager did relative to something, not why, which is the job of attribution.

With a benchmark in place, performance attribution explains where the active return came from. The interest-rate management effect captures gains or losses from duration and curve positioning, parallel shifts, twists and butterflies. The sector allocation effect comes from overweighting or underweighting Treasuries, agencies, corporates, MBS and ABS. The security selection effect reflects the choice of individual bonds within sectors, including their own spread changes. The trading effect captures the value of trading during the period compared with simply holding the starting portfolio. Attribution models may be sector-based, factor-based or a hybrid, and the aim is to separate skill from luck and find the sources of return a manager can repeat.

Finally come risk-adjusted measures. The Sharpe ratio divides excess return over cash by total volatility, a single number for comparing portfolios, though an easy benchmark, a "slow rabbit," can make ordinary managers look skilled. The information ratio, active return divided by tracking error, measures skill per unit of active risk; above 0.5 is usually considered excellent, and most active bond managers achieve less than 0.3. The numbers are sobering: average active fixed-income alpha before fees is only about 44 basis points a year, so fees and trading costs can consume most of what active management adds, which is why the attribution of that small edge matters so much.

The last part covers the derivatives that bond managers use to change their risk without trading the bonds themselves: interest-rate futures, options on bonds and futures, swaps and their relatives, caps, floors and swaptions, and credit default swaps. Chapter 29: Interest-Rate Futures

Primer

A futures contract is an agreement to buy or sell something at a fixed price on a future date, traded on an exchange. Nobody pays the full price up front. Each side posts a deposit, the margin, and every day the exchange moves gains and losses between the two accounts according to that day's closing price, called marking to market. If your losses eat your deposit below a set level, you must add cash the next day or your position is closed. So a futures position gives you the full price exposure of the bonds for the cost of a small deposit, which is what makes futures such a cheap tool for changing a portfolio's risk.

A futures contract is a standardized agreement to buy or sell a security at a set price on a future date, traded on an exchange. Its key feature is the clearinghouse, which becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer, so traders never face each other's credit risk and can close a position without finding the original counterparty. Both sides post initial margin, a good-faith deposit rather than a loan, and positions are marked to market daily at the settlement price. If an account falls below its maintenance margin, the trader receives a variation margin call that must be met in cash, usually within a day, or the position is closed. The daily settling keeps losses from piling up unseen.

The main U.S. contracts are on Treasuries and short rates. The Treasury bond future is based on $100,000 of a hypothetical 20-year bond with a 6% coupon and is quoted in 32nds of a point: a price of 97-16 means 97½% of par, or $97,500, and one tick of 1/32 is worth $31.25. Treasury note futures cover 2-year ($200,000), 5-year and 10-year ($100,000) notes, and the Ultra bond future covers bonds with at least 25 years left. For short rates, Eurodollar futures on a $1 million three-month LIBOR deposit, quoted as 100 minus the rate, with $25 a basis point, were among the most traded contracts in the world; they are being replaced by SOFR futures, one-month and three-month, among the fastest-growing products in CME history.

Delivery is where Treasury futures get subtle. The seller can deliver any Treasury bond from a list of eligible issues with at least 15 years to maturity, so each bond's invoice price is adjusted by a conversion factor meant to put them on a level footing. The adjustment is imperfect, so one bond is always cheapest to deliver, the cheapest-to-deliver (CTD), identified as the one with the highest implied repo rate, the return from buying it now and delivering it into the future. The seller also holds a timing option, choosing when in the delivery month to deliver, and a wild card option, announcing delivery after the futures market closes at the day's settlement price while cash bonds keep trading. The CTD shifts with rates: low yields favor high-duration bonds, high yields low-duration ones.

Futures prices are tied to cash prices by arbitrage. The theoretical futures price equals the cash price plus the cost of financing the bond until delivery minus the coupon income earned meanwhile, the cost of carry. If the futures price is higher, a trader can buy the bond, finance it in the repo market and sell the future, locking in a riskless profit, a cash-and-carry trade; if it is lower, the trader can do the reverse. At fair value the implied repo rate equals the actual financing rate. The delivery options complicate the arithmetic, since the seller's choices are worth something, so actual futures prices sit a little below the simple theoretical price.

Because a Treasury future's price follows its cheapest-to-deliver bond, the future's own interest-rate sensitivity comes from the CTD. A common approximation sets the future's dollar duration equal to the CTD's dollar duration divided by its conversion factor. That is why the contract counts in the examples below depend on which bond is cheapest to deliver, and why they must be revisited when the CTD changes: if a big move in rates switches the CTD from a short, low-duration bond to a long one, each futures contract suddenly carries more interest-rate risk, and a hedge that was exactly right yesterday is now too large. Managers who use futures heavily track the CTD and its likely successors as closely as they track rates themselves.

For portfolio managers, the most common use of futures is to change duration quickly and cheaply. The number of contracts needed is the target dollar duration minus the current dollar duration, divided by the dollar duration of one contract; a positive number means buying futures to lengthen, a negative number selling to shorten. In Fabozzi's example, a $48 million portfolio with a duration of 2.97 must match a benchmark at 3.68. Its dollar duration must rise from $1,425,600 to $1,766,400, an increase of $340,800, and with five-year note futures at about $5,000 of dollar duration per contract, the manager buys 68 contracts instead of selling short bonds and buying long ones, avoiding the trading costs and market impact.

Plain English

Why do futures change duration so easily? Buying a bond future is like owning the bond with borrowed money: you gain if bond prices rise and lose if they fall, but you've only put up a small deposit. So adding futures adds interest-rate exposure without spending cash or selling anything.

In the example, the portfolio is too short: it would gain about $1.43 million for a one-point fall in rates when the benchmark's equivalent would gain about $1.77 million. Each five-year note future adds roughly $5,000 of that sensitivity, so 68 of them close the $340,800 gap. Doing the same thing in cash bonds would mean selling millions of dollars of short bonds and buying long ones, paying the bid-ask spread on both sides. To shorten duration instead, you sell futures.

Hedging a specific bond with futures needs a hedge ratio: the price value of a basis point of the bond being hedged divided by that of the CTD, times the CTD's conversion factor, times a yield beta that captures how much the hedged bond's yield moves for each move in the CTD's. The hedge leaves basis risk. The futures price and the cash price do not converge perfectly, because of the delivery options and changes in the CTD. Cross-hedging a corporate bond with Treasury futures assumes a stable spread, which fails exactly when hedges are needed most, since in a credit scare spreads widen as Treasury yields fall. And a hedge at a different maturity is exposed to curve twists. Hedge effectiveness is often measured by the R-squared of a regression.

Futures serve many other portfolio jobs. A manager expecting new cash can buy futures now to lock in today's rates, an anticipatory hedge, and unwind them as the cash arrives and is invested. A manager can change the mix between asset classes by selling bond futures and buying stock index futures without touching the underlying holdings. Futures can also create synthetic securities: a portfolio of Treasury bills plus long bond futures behaves like a portfolio of long bonds, and if the futures are cheap relative to the bonds, the synthetic version earns more. In each case the futures market's low costs and deep liquidity make it the quickest way to act, and the cash portfolio can be adjusted later at leisure.

Chapter 30: Interest-Rate Options

Interest-rate options trade on exchanges and over the counter, and the most popular exchange-traded form is the option on a futures contract, all of them American-style, exercisable at any time. Fabozzi gives three reasons for their popularity: futures carry no accrued interest to complicate settlement; delivery squeezes are less of a problem, since the supply of deliverable futures is ample; and futures prices are easier to find than the prices of individual cash bonds. Exchange-traded options on the bonds themselves are rare, and institutions that need tailored strikes, dates or underlying bonds use over-the-counter options from dealers. Exercising a futures call gives the holder a long futures position at the strike price plus the difference in cash.

An option's price has two parts. Intrinsic value is what it would be worth if exercised now: for a call, the amount by which the underlying price exceeds the strike, if any. Time value is the rest, the value of the chance that the option will become more valuable before it expires. Six things move the price of a bond option: the current price of the underlying, the strike price, the time to expiration, the expected volatility of interest rates, the short-term rate, and the coupon the underlying bond pays. A longer life and higher volatility raise both calls and puts, since they give the price more room to move; a coupon raises puts and lowers calls, because the holder of the bond collects it and the holder of the option does not.

Pricing bond options with the Black-Scholes model, built for stocks, runs into three problems. It allows the price of the underlying to rise without limit, but a bond's price is limited by its remaining cash flows and converges to par at maturity; a call on a zero-coupon bond struck at $100.25 can never pay off, yet Black-Scholes gives it a value of $2.79. It assumes a constant short-term interest rate, the very thing whose changes make bond options valuable. And it assumes constant price volatility, while a bond's volatility shrinks as it approaches maturity. The proper approach is an arbitrage-free model such as the binomial tree of Chapter 19, which respects the yield curve and the term structure of volatility.

Plain English

Why can't the stock-option formula price a bond option? A stock could, in principle, double or triple by next year. A zero-coupon bond that pays $100 at maturity can never be worth more than $100, and on its last day it is worth exactly $100. So an option to buy that bond for $100.25 at maturity is worthless: you'd be paying more than the bond could ever be worth.

Black-Scholes doesn't know that. It treats the bond's price like a stock's, free to wander anywhere, so it sees some chance that the price ends above $100.25 and assigns the option a value of $2.79. A bond model built on interest rates, which pulls the price to par as maturity nears, gets the right answer: zero.

Option traders measure risk with the Greeks. Delta is the change in the option's price for a small change in the underlying's, near 0 for options far out of the money and near 1 for those deep in it; it is also the hedge ratio. Gamma is the change in delta, highest for at-the-money options, the option's convexity; high gamma means a hedge must be adjusted often. Theta is time decay, the value an option loses each day as expiration nears, fastest for at-the-money options in the final weeks. Vega, or kappa, is sensitivity to implied volatility; owners of options gain when volatility rises. As Fabozzi puts it, strategies in this market come down to buying and selling volatility.

Options let managers reshape a bond position's payoff. A protective put, buying a put on a bond or bond future, works like insurance: if rates rise and prices fall, the put pays; if prices rise, the manager keeps the upside, less the premium. A covered call, selling a call against a bond already owned, earns the premium in exchange for giving up gains above the strike, and by put-call parity it is equivalent to selling a put; it does best when rates are stable or rising gently. A collar combines the two, buying a put for a floor and selling a call for a cap so that the call premium pays for some or all of the put; when the premiums match exactly it is a zero-cost collar.

Plain English

A collar on a bond portfolio, in plain terms. You own bonds worth $100 and you're worried rates will rise. You buy a put at $95: if the bonds fall below $95, the put makes up the difference, so you can't lose more than $5. The put costs money, so to pay for it you sell a call at $105: if the bonds rise above $105, you give up the gains beyond that.

If the call brings in as much as the put costs, the protection is free in cash terms. It isn't free in outcomes: you've swapped any gain above $105 for protection below $95. Your bonds' value is now boxed in between $95 and $105, which is exactly what a pension fund or insurer worried about a bad year may want.

Options also carry great leverage. Fabozzi's example: if yields on a 20-year bond fall 200 basis points, the bond returns about 23%, but the same money spent on calls returns about 670%. If yields stay put or rise, though, the calls expire worthless and the whole investment is lost. That asymmetry is both the attraction and the danger, and he insists there is no best strategy, only trade-offs across scenarios: buying options pays when moves are big, selling them pays when markets are calm, and every position should be tested across the range of possible rate outcomes before it is put on, the same scenario discipline as total return analysis.

The one input to an option model that cannot be observed is volatility, so traders reverse the process and solve for the implied volatility that makes the model price equal the market price. Implied volatility differs across strikes: options far in or out of the money often carry higher implied volatility than at-the-money options, a volatility smile, because markets price in extreme moves more than the models assume, and a systematic slope across strikes is called a skew. For futures options, price volatility can be translated into yield volatility by dividing by the CTD's duration. Practitioners usually quote at-the-money implied volatility, while remembering that it assumes both the market and the model are right, which is rarely true at the same time.

Putting options to work on a real portfolio requires sizing. A manager protecting a corporate bond portfolio with puts on Treasury bond futures must first pick a strike, which sets the floor, then work out how many puts give the right protection. That number comes from the same hedge ratio used with futures: the price sensitivity of the portfolio relative to the CTD bond, adjusted by the CTD's conversion factor and by the yield beta between the portfolio's yields and Treasury yields. The protection is only as good as those relationships: if corporate spreads widen while Treasury yields fall, as they do in a credit scare, the Treasury puts may pay nothing while the portfolio falls. Fabozzi's point is that options hedge the risk they are written on, and everything else remains basis risk.

Chapter 31: Swaps, FRAs, Caps & Floors

In an interest-rate swap, two parties exchange interest payments on a notional principal that never changes hands: one pays a fixed rate, the other a floating rate such as SOFR, and only the net difference is paid each period. The fixed-rate payer gains when rates rise, since the floating payments it receives grow while its fixed payments do not; the fixed-rate receiver gains when rates fall. The fixed rate, the swap rate, is set so that the present value of the fixed payments equals the present value of the expected floating payments, computed from forward rates, so a new swap has no value to either side. The gap between the swap rate and the Treasury yield of the same maturity is the swap spread.

A swap can be seen in two ways. It is a package of forward rate agreements, one for each payment date, each locking in a rate for one future period; the fixed-rate payer gains on each FRA whose realized rate exceeds its forward rate. Or it is a package of cash instruments: the fixed-rate payer has, in effect, issued a fixed-rate bond and bought a floating-rate note, and the receiver has done the reverse. The second view gives the swap's risk at once. A floater has almost no duration, so a receiver's duration is close to that of a fixed-rate bond of the same maturity, about 8 for a 10-year swap at 4%, and a payer's is the negative of it. That is why swaps are the simplest tool for changing duration.

A single forward rate agreement shows the building block. In an FRA, two parties agree today on a rate for a future period, say three months starting three months from now, on a notional amount. When the period begins, the agreed rate is compared with the actual reference rate, and one side pays the other the difference times the notional and the period's length, usually discounted because it is paid at the start rather than the end. If an FRA on $10 million fixes 4% for a 90-day period and the rate turns out to be 5%, the buyer of the FRA receives about $25,000, the 1-point difference for a quarter of a year, less a small discount. A swap is simply a strip of such agreements at one fixed rate.

Plain English

A swap with simple numbers. A bank and an insurer agree on a 5-year swap on $10 million. The bank pays the insurer a fixed 4% a year; the insurer pays the bank whatever the floating rate turns out to be each period. No $10 million ever moves; it's just the yardstick for the payments.

If the floating rate is 3% in a year, the bank owes $400,000 and the insurer owes $300,000, so the bank simply pays the insurer $100,000. If the floating rate is 6%, the insurer pays the bank $200,000. The bank, paying fixed, wins when rates rise; the insurer, receiving fixed, wins when they fall. For the insurer, receiving fixed is like owning a 5-year bond bought with borrowed money at the floating rate, which is why swaps let a manager add or remove duration without buying or selling bonds.

Swap spreads reflect several forces. They carry the credit risk of the banking system, since LIBOR was an unsecured bank rate. They respond to supply and demand for fixed and floating payments, from companies swapping new bond issues and from mortgage investors hedging. They move with Treasury supply: heavy issuance pushes Treasury yields up and narrows swap spreads, while scarcity widens them. And they move with flights to quality, when Treasury yields drop faster than swap rates. The relationships can break down: in 2015, long-dated swap spreads turned negative, with swap rates below Treasury yields, something traditional arbitrage reasoning said could not last, reflecting the cost of holding Treasuries on bank balance sheets under new capital rules.

Swaps have many uses. A bank with fixed-rate loans funded by floating-rate deposits can receive floating and pay fixed to match its assets and liabilities, and an insurer with floating-rate assets and fixed annuity obligations can do the reverse. A manager can raise duration by receiving fixed or lower it by paying fixed, with the notional sized by dividing the desired change in dollar duration by the swap's dollar duration per dollar of notional. Swaps create synthetic assets, turning a floating-rate bond into a fixed-rate one or the reverse. And they make structured notes possible: an issuer can sell an inverse floater to an investor who wants it and swap its own payments back to cheap fixed-rate funding.

An interest-rate cap is a series of call options on a rate, called caplets, one for each reset date: whenever the reference rate is above the cap rate, the seller pays the buyer the difference times the notional. A floor is a series of puts, floorlets, paying when the rate falls below the floor rate. A collar combines a long cap with a short floor, the floor premium offsetting part of the cap's cost. Banks buy caps to limit the cost of floating-rate funding; insurers buy floors to protect the minimum rates they guarantee on annuities. There are even options on caps, called captions, which give the right to buy a cap at a set premium.

Plain English

A cap is insurance on a floating rate. A company has borrowed $50 million at SOFR plus 2% and fears rates will rise. It buys a 5% cap on $50 million. If SOFR is 4% at a reset, the cap pays nothing. If SOFR is 6%, the cap pays the 1-point excess over 5% on $50 million for that period, about $500,000 a year, so the company's borrowing cost never goes above 5% plus its 2% margin.

Like any insurance, the cap costs a premium up front. To make it cheaper, the company can sell a floor at, say, 2%, agreeing to pay the difference if SOFR drops below 2%. That combination, a collar, keeps its rate between about 2% and 5% (plus the margin) for much less cash.

Swaptions are options to enter a swap. A "3 into 10" swaption gives the right, in three years, to enter a 10-year swap. A payer swaption, the right to pay fixed, gains when rates rise and is economically a put on a fixed-rate bond; a receiver swaption, the right to receive fixed, gains when rates fall and is a call on a fixed-rate bond. Mortgage lenders who have promised borrowers a rate before the loan closes buy payer swaptions to protect against rates rising in the meantime. Issuers can sell noncallable bonds and buy receiver swaptions to build a synthetic callable bond, often more cheaply than paying for a call in the bond itself. Pension funds buy receiver swaptions against falling rates, which raise the value of their liabilities.

Underneath all of these products is the forward curve of Chapter 6. A swap's fixed rate is an average of forward rates; an FRA locks in a single forward rate; caps and floors are options on future floating rates, priced around those same forwards; and swaptions are options on future swap rates. That shared foundation is what lets dealers hedge one product with another and investors switch between them to express the same view most cheaply. It also explains why the move from LIBOR to SOFR was such a large undertaking: the reference rate sits inside hundreds of trillions of dollars of swaps, caps, floors and floaters, and every one of those contracts needed a new rate and a spread adjustment.

Chapter 32: Credit Default Swaps

A credit default swap (CDS) is insurance against a borrower's default. The protection buyer pays a regular premium, the CDS spread, on a notional amount; the protection seller pays out if a credit event hits the reference entity. Neither side needs to own the borrower's bonds, so CDS outstanding can far exceed the bonds themselves. Selling protection is economically like owning the bond with borrowed money, an unfunded long position in credit; buying protection is a synthetic short. Since the 2009 "Big Bang" standardization, contracts pay fixed coupons of 1% for investment-grade names or 5% for high-yield, with an upfront payment settling the difference between that coupon and the fair spread.

Plain English

A CDS is fire insurance on a company's debt. You buy $10 million of five-year protection on a company at 1% a year: you pay $100,000 a year. If the company goes bankrupt and its bonds end up worth 40 cents on the dollar, the seller pays you $6 million, the 60% that was lost. If nothing happens for five years, the seller keeps the $500,000 of premiums.

The fixed coupons explain the upfront payment. If the market thinks that company's protection is really worth 2.5% a year but the standard contract pays only 1%, the buyer pays the seller the difference up front, roughly 1.5% a year times the contract's effective life of about 4.5 years, or near 6.75% of the notional. Standard coupons made contracts interchangeable, which is what let them be cleared and traded like futures.

What counts as a credit event is set by ISDA definitions. The list includes bankruptcy, failure to pay, restructuring, acceleration of the debt, cross-default, and, for sovereigns, repudiation or a moratorium; some contracts also refer to downgrades. Restructuring is the most contentious, since extending maturities or cutting coupons may or may not hurt bondholders, and the market has used several versions of the clause, from full restructuring to none. In 2019 ISDA added a "narrowly tailored credit event" rule after the Hovnanian case, in which a hedge fund arranged a technical missed payment to trigger CDS it owned while the company received favorable financing, a manufactured default. CDS on asset-backed securities use pay-as-you-go terms covering interest shortfalls and write-downs.

Contracts settle in one of two ways. In physical settlement, the buyer delivers eligible bonds and receives par, and since any of several bonds can be delivered, the buyer picks the cheapest, an option that is worth something to the protection buyer. Today most contracts settle in cash through an auction run under ISDA rules, in which dealers submit bids and offers to fix a single recovery price; the seller pays par minus that price. Recoveries vary enormously: the Lehman Brothers auction set a price of just 8.625 cents on the dollar, J.C. Penney's 0.125, while Washington Mutual's was a surprisingly high 57. Those numbers are what protection sellers actually paid out, and they show how wide the range of losses given default can be.

Index CDS package many names into one contract. The CDX.NA.IG covers 125 North American investment-grade companies and the CDX.NA.HY 100 high-yield companies, and new series are launched every six months as names are added and dropped. When a name in an index defaults, the contract continues on a smaller notional with that name removed, instead of ending as a single-name CDS does. For a small portfolio that cannot hold hundreds of bonds, one index trade gives broad, diversified credit exposure. Regulation can change these markets quickly: trading in the iTraxx SovX Western Europe index of 15 sovereigns collapsed after the EU banned naked short positions in sovereign CDS in 2012.

Indexes can also be sliced into tranches defined by attachment and detachment points. The equity tranche, 0–3%, absorbs the first 3% of losses in the index and is highly leveraged, about 33 times; mezzanine tranches cover 3–7%, 7–10% and 10–15%; the senior tranche 15–30%; and the super-senior tranche 30–100%, which has almost never lost money. The equity tranche is quoted as an upfront payment like an option, the others as running spreads. Tranches let traders take precise views: selling equity protection is a bet that few names will default, while buying equity protection and selling mezzanine is a bet on how defaults will cluster, a correlation trade.

Plain English

Why is the equity tranche "33 times leveraged"? It covers only the first 3% of losses in an index of 125 companies. Each company is 0.8% of the index; if one defaults with 40% recovery, the index loses about 0.48%. For the whole index that's tiny, but for the equity tranche, whose entire size is 3% of the index, it's a sixth of its value gone. About six such defaults wipe it out.

The super-senior tranche is the opposite: it only loses money after 30% of the whole index has been lost, which would take something like 60 of the 125 companies defaulting at that recovery rate. Same index, same companies; the tranches just decide who absorbs which losses, exactly like the layer cake of a CLO.

CDS have four advantages over cash bonds, Fabozzi argues. They offer leverage, since selling protection needs little capital; they make shorting credit easy, whereas borrowing a corporate bond to sell it short is hard and expensive; they lock in a fixed financing rate for the life of the contract, while repo rates change and repo can be withdrawn; and that financing is permanent, lasting the full term. A single-name CDS also isolates credit risk from interest-rate risk, which a cash bond bundles together. Valuation equates the present value of the premiums, paid only while the name survives, with the present value of the expected payout, which produces implied default probabilities and a term structure of them across maturities.

The CDS-bond basis, the CDS spread minus the cash bond's spread, measures how the two markets price the same risk. A positive basis means protection is expensive relative to the bond; a negative basis means it is cheap, and investors can buy the bond and buy protection to earn the difference. The basis exists because of funding costs, since bonds require cash and CDS do not; the cheapest-to-deliver option held by protection buyers; counterparty risk in the CDS, reduced by collateral posting; and the different investors in the two markets. One quirk affects municipal CDS: municipal bond interest is tax-exempt, but CDS premiums are not, so tax-exempt investors cannot use selling protection as an equivalent substitute for owning munis.

The users of CDS are as varied as the uses. Banks buy protection to hedge loans they cannot sell without upsetting a client. Portfolio managers buy protection to cut exposure to a name quickly or sell it to add exposure without hunting for scarce bonds, and use index CDS to adjust a whole portfolio's credit risk in one trade. Hedge funds trade the basis, the curve of spreads across maturities and the tranches. The 2008 crisis showed the dark side: huge one-way protection sales, concentrated in a few institutions, turned CDS into a channel of contagion, and the response was standardized contracts, central clearing and collateral rules, which have made the market safer while leaving its core function, the pricing and transfer of default risk, intact.

Trading & Investing Lessons

Know Your Duration Before Anything Else

Duration explains most of a high-quality bond portfolio's return, so the first question about any bond fund or position is how far its price moves for a 1-point change in rates. A long Treasury fund with a duration of 17 can lose a sixth of its value in a year when yields rise one point, a real risk for anyone who thinks of bonds as the safe part of a portfolio. Match duration to when you need the money, the immunization idea, and short-term bond funds behave like savings while long ones behave like a leveraged bet on falling rates.

Read the Spread, Not Just the Yield

A corporate bond's yield is a Treasury yield plus a spread, and the two move for different reasons. In a risk-off sell-off Treasury yields often fall while spreads widen, so high-yield funds can drop even as Treasuries rally. When credit spreads are tight relative to their history, investors are being paid little for default and downgrade risk; when they blow out, as in 2008 or March 2020, forced selling can leave bonds well below fair value, the fallen-angel effect on a market-wide scale.

Callable Bonds and MBS Sell You an Option

Extra yield on callable bonds and mortgage securities is payment for an option the investor has sold. It looks attractive in calm markets and costs money when rates move a lot, because the upside is capped by calls and refinancing while the downside is not. Before chasing yield in agency MBS or callable corporates, compare OAS rather than nominal spread, and expect the position to lag plain Treasuries in a sharp rally.

The Yield Curve Is a Market Signal

An inverted curve has preceded most U.S. recessions, and the curve's shape also tells an investor what can be locked in: forward rates are the break-even rates for choosing longer bonds over rolling short ones. If you think rates will fall below what the forwards imply, extend duration; if not, stay short and collect the front-end yield. The choice between bullet and barbell positions is a bet on volatility and curve shape, not just on the level of rates.

Liquidity Disappears When You Need It

Corporate bonds trade rarely, dealers hold far less inventory than before 2008, and bond ETFs can trade well below the stated value of their holdings in a panic. Size positions in less liquid bonds and funds so that you never have to sell them in a crisis, and treat the extra yield on illiquid bonds as payment for that risk rather than free return.

Leverage Turns Carry Into Danger

Borrowing at 4.5% to hold bonds at 5% quadruples a small edge on capital, and a 2% price drop then costs 8% of equity. LTCM's collapse shows how leveraged carry trades unwind together, when funding and market liquidity vanish at the same time. Any strategy that earns a small spread on borrowed money, from bond carry trades to mortgage REITs, should be judged by how it behaves in the worst month, not the average one.

Key Takeaways

Duration is a price sensitivity measure, not a time measure

Despite common misinterpretation, duration measures approximate percentage price change for a 100bp yield change. A CMO tranche can have duration of 40 while underlying mortgages are 30-year—making no sense as a time measure. An option can have duration of 20 with only one year to expiration.

No truly risk-free assets exist

Even U.S. Treasuries carry inflation, liquidity, and potential default risk over long horizons. Peter Fisher (former Treasury undersecretary): “The idea of risk-free sovereign bonds is best thought of as an oxymoron.” The global shortage of safe assets has driven down natural interest rates by ~50 basis points.

Convexity has measurable value—and a price

In volatile markets, positive convexity becomes extremely valuable: price appreciation exceeds depreciation for equal yield changes. In stable markets, selling convexity via callable bonds can enhance yield. The key question: how much should investors pay? Answer depends entirely on expected rate volatility.

Forward rates are hedgeable rates, not predictions

Academic studies consistently show forward rates are poor predictors of future spot rates, yet they remain central to bond analysis for the right reason: they represent rates investors can lock in through bond selection. Even if you expect rates to rise, you should choose the instrument whose expected rate exceeds the forward rate.

LIBOR’s demise requires a massive market transition

Over $375 trillion in financial products tied to LIBOR must transition to SOFR. SOFR is overnight, backward-looking, and secured (Treasury repo), versus LIBOR’s forward-looking, unsecured bank lending rate. Spread adjustment required because SOFR lacks LIBOR’s credit risk component.

Corporate bond liquidity has collapsed

Dealer inventory down 80% since 2007 while issuance up 50%. Some 38% of corporate bonds never trade; 23% trade only a few times. The average stock trades 3,800 times per day; the most liquid investment-grade corporates trade only 85 times. This creates systemic liquidity risk.

Municipal default rates are severely underestimated

Official rating agency data shows 0.1% cumulative defaults, but broader studies reveal 2,521 defaults from 1970–2011—roughly 50x the official statistics. The narrow definitions used by agencies systematically exclude unrated issues.

Mortgage prepayment modeling is enormously complex

Refinancing, housing turnover, and cash-out refinancing must be modeled separately. The S-curve shows how prepayment response increases then plateaus. Burnout effects mean pools with rate exposure see declining prepayments as willing refinancers exit. Monte Carlo simulation with 512–1,024 paths required for proper MBS valuation because cash flows are path-dependent.

Total return analysis is superior to YTM

The five-step horizon analysis procedure explicitly models reinvestment rate assumptions and horizon yield changes. YTM assumes reinvestment at the same rate and holding to maturity—both unrealistic. Total return enables scenario analysis across multiple interest-rate environments.

Key rate duration captures real yield curve risk

Traditional duration only measures parallel shifts, yet Frank Jones found that parallel shifts and twists together explain 91.6% of Treasury returns and butterfly shifts another 3.4%, so the shape of the curve matters. Key rate duration quantifies exposure at 11 specific maturity points. Three portfolios with identical duration of 4.26 can have completely different responses to 30-year rate changes.

CMO structures redistribute risk, they don’t eliminate it

Sequential-pay, PAC, Z-bonds, and floater/inverse floater structures enable precise risk allocation across investor preferences. But support bonds absorb massive variability. When support bonds are depleted, PAC bonds become “busted” and lose all protection.

Effective duration is required for embedded options

Modified duration fails for callable, putable, or prepayable bonds because it assumes fixed cash flows. Differences can be dramatic: a bond may have modified duration 7 but effective duration 50 when option effects dominate. Higher volatility lowers callable bond value but raises putable bond value.

Avoiding losers matters more than picking winners in credit

Truncated index studies show removing the worst 10% of corporate bonds improves returns—the opposite of equities, where removing the best 10% destroys returns. Credit investing has capped upside (coupons + par) but deep downside (principal loss minus recovery). This asymmetry drives the entire credit strategy.

Duration Times Spread (DTS) is superior to spread duration alone

DTS = spread duration × credit spread. Excess return volatility increases linearly with DTS across different quality segments, making it the proper measure of credit risk exposure. A BBB bond with spread of 200bps and duration 5 has DTS of 1,000—comparable risk to a BB bond with spread 400bps and duration 2.5.

Green bonds and carbon policy enable climate finance

CPPBs tie coupons to emissions performance, creating market-based incentives. Knut Kjaer (founder, Norway sovereign wealth fund) argues debt markets give investors greater impact than equity markets: 100 fossil fuel-intensive companies borrow in debt markets, but only 40 are listed on exchanges.

EBITDA is a dangerous metric used in isolation

It excludes capital expenditures that are absolutely required. Sino-Forest had 69% EBITDA margins and 10.4× interest coverage yet never generated positive free cash flow in 15 years. The superior measure is EBITDA minus maintenance capex.

Fallen angels outperform peer high-yield bonds

Bonds downgraded from investment-grade suffer forced selling (index deletion, mandate restrictions) that pushes prices below fundamental value. They outperform peer HY bonds by 6.63% over two years after downgrade.

OAS constancy is theoretically unjustifiable for MBS

IO + PO = pass-through must hold in dollar terms, but their OAS levels cannot all remain constant under rate changes. This is a fundamental limitation of standard MBS analytics that practitioners must acknowledge.

The “bum’s problem” undermines bond benchmarks

Market-capitalization-weighted bond indexes overweight the most indebted issuers. Unlike equities where market cap reflects value, in bonds more debt means more weight—rewarding riskier behavior. Alternatives include issuer caps, GDP-weighting, and duration-weighting.

Covenants are among the most important credit considerations

The Sirius XM case study demonstrates: 5.25% 2022 bonds with high-yield covenants outperformed other unsecured bonds by 150–200bps solely due to covenant protection. Loopholes in covenants reveal management intentions.

Repo market is the plumbing of the bond market

Repurchase agreements are collateralized loans that fund dealer inventories, enable leverage for portfolio managers, and create the financing advantage that bids up on-the-run Treasury issues. When repo markets seize (as in 2008), the entire bond market freezes.

Inverse floaters amplify duration beyond maturity

A $20M inverse floater created from $100M collateral with leverage L=4 has duration = (1+L) × collateral duration = 40, even if the underlying mortgages are only 30-year. This proves duration cannot be a time measure—it’s purely a price sensitivity metric.

PAC bonds offer false security when support bonds deplete

PAC bondholders enjoy prepayment protection within a collar (e.g., 90–300 PSA) only as long as support bonds remain outstanding. Once support bonds are exhausted by faster-than-expected prepayments, the PAC structure “busts” and becomes sequential-pay with no protection.

Z-bonds eliminate reinvestment risk by design

Accrual tranches receive no current interest—all interest accrues and compounds, then diverts to accelerate paydown of earlier tranches. Investors concerned about reinvesting coupon payments at unknown future rates find Z-bonds inherently appealing.

CMO tranches on the same collateral have wildly different durations

In a FactSet example, a sequential-pay Freddie Mac deal shows class A with 0.02 effective duration and the Z-bond with 11.24 effective duration—over 500x difference—from the same underlying mortgage pool.

Tax-equivalent yield magnifies muni advantages for high earners

A 3.4% tax-exempt municipal yield equals 5.23% taxable at 35% marginal rate, but 6.18% at 45%—making the same bond 18% more valuable to the higher-bracket investor. Triple tax exemption (federal + state + local) for in-state bonds amplifies further.

Covered bonds have never defaulted in 250+ years

The German Pfandbrief model (dating to 1769) provides dual recourse—claim on the issuer plus a ring-fenced cover pool of high-quality assets. This structural protection has produced a zero-default track record unmatched by any other fixed-income instrument class.

Contrarian Ideas

Duration misunderstood as time

The widespread interpretation of duration as “weighted average life” is misleading. A CMO tranche can have duration 40 with 30-year underlying mortgages. A call option can have duration 20 with one year to expiration. The correct interpretation: approximate percentage price change for 100bp yield shift.

Safe assets don’t exist

The foundational assumption of a risk-free rate is increasingly questioned. A Ferreira-Shousha study (1970–2018) shows net supply of sovereign safe assets for 11 advanced economies is a key driver of natural interest rates, explaining 20% of their variance. Global safe asset accumulation has reduced rates by 50bps.

Forward rates don’t predict

Despite being central to bond analysis, forward rates consistently fail as predictors of future spot rates. But they are not meant to predict—they are hedgeable rates. Even if you expect rates to rise to 5.60%, you should still choose the 1-year instrument if your expected rate is below the 5.75% forward rate.

Municipal defaults severely underreported

Official 0.1% cumulative rates exclude unrated issues and use narrow definitions. Broader studies find 2,521 defaults from 1970–2011—roughly 50x official statistics. Detroit’s 2013 bankruptcy tested absolute priority doctrine.

Convexity overpriced in calm markets

Investors systematically overpay for convexity protection during low-volatility periods. With small yield changes, high-convexity and low-convexity bonds offer approximately the same price change. Sophisticated investors sell convexity (callable bonds) in calm markets to harvest yield premium.

Negative rates can be economically rational

Despite seeming to violate basic finance, negative rates serve multiple purposes: stimulate borrowing, weaken currency for exports (Bank of Japan 2016), penalize excess reserves to encourage lending. As of September 2019, ~25% of investment-grade bonds in developed markets had negative yields.

Debt markets more powerful than equity for climate

While ESG activism focuses on shareholders, bond investors can directly penalize carbon-intensive companies through higher credit spreads. 100 fossil fuel-intensive companies borrow in debt markets; only 40–41 are listed on exchanges. Actions in debt markets affect funding costs directly.

EBITDA is a dangerous metric

Sino-Forest had 69% EBITDA margins yet never generated positive free cash flow in 15 years. All three CRAs rated it BB category. EBITDA excludes capex that is absolutely required—the better measure is EBITDA minus maintenance capex.

Fallen angels outperform

Bonds downgraded from investment-grade to high-yield suffer forced selling (index deletion, mandate restrictions) that pushes prices below fundamental value. They outperform peer HY bonds by 6.63% over two years after downgrade.

OAS constancy is theoretically unjustifiable

IO + PO = pass-through must hold in dollar terms, but their OAS levels cannot all remain constant under rate changes. This means at least one of the three securities must be mispriced under the standard OAS framework.

Credit ratings are inaccurate default probability measures

Simple reduced-form models dominate credit ratings for forecasting corporate failure. The Sino-Forest case shows all three major CRAs unanimously failed to warn investors—they rushed to downgrade only after the stock had already plunged.

The 1970–2000 rate environment was the anomaly

Bond market observers debate when yields will return to “normal” highs of 5%+, using 1970–2000 as baseline. But this period (Volcker tightening, double-digit inflation, 20% fed funds rate) was historically exceptional, not representative.

Nonrefundable ≠ noncallable

A common investor error. Nonrefundable merely means the issuer cannot use lower-cost debt proceeds to retire the bond. The issuer can still call it using other funds (asset sales, equity issuance). Noncallable provides strictly greater protection than nonrefundable—a critical distinction in high-yield covenants.

DIP financing undermines secured creditors

Debtor-in-possession lenders in Chapter 11 bankruptcy receive priority even over existing secured creditors, plus higher interest rates, because they provide essential rescue capital. The “absolute priority rule” that theoretically protects seniority is frequently violated in reorganization through plan negotiations and bargaining dynamics.

Monoline insurers were the market’s hidden fragility

Pre-2007, ~50% of new municipal bonds carried monoline insurance, and no insured muni had ever defaulted or been downgraded. The monolines’ involvement in subprime MBS destroyed their credit ratings overnight, removing the cornerstone of municipal bond credit enhancement and permanently altering the market structure.

Option cost for MBS tranches is more stable than OAS

When market prices change slightly, a tranche’s option cost (static spread minus OAS) remains relatively constant while OAS itself fluctuates. This makes option cost a more reliable metric for monitoring relative value over time—a useful computational shortcut for portfolio managers.

Proposals & Frameworks

Adopt total return framework over YTM

The five-step horizon analysis explicitly models reinvestment assumptions and yield changes rather than relying on yield-to-maturity’s unrealistic conditions (reinvestment at same rate, hold to maturity).

Use key rate duration for real-world risk management

Traditional duration assumes parallel shifts; key rate duration quantifies exposure at 11 maturity points. Three portfolios with identical duration 4.26 had completely different risk profiles (barbell: 0.675, bullet: ~0, ladder: 0.594 for 30-year key rate).

Implement effective duration for complex securities

Modified duration fails for embedded options. Use the approximation formula: (P₋ − P₊) / [2P₀Δy], which works for any bond or derivative including MBS, callable/putable bonds, and options.

Transition from LIBOR to SOFR with spread adjustments

SOFR lacks LIBOR’s credit component; legacy contracts require robust fallback provisions. The transition affects $375 trillion in financial products.

Develop comprehensive liquidity assessment

Incorporate trading frequency, dealer inventory trends, bid-ask spreads, and market impact costs. With 38% of corporates never trading, liquidity scoring is essential for portfolio construction.

Apply CMO structural analysis to all ABS

Understanding sequential-pay waterfalls, PAC/support mechanics, coverage tests, and cash flow redirection applies universally across all structured credit products including CLOs, CMBS, and auto ABS.

Integrate climate risk into credit analysis

Carbon emissions, policy exposure, and transition risk should be standard credit components. Carbon policy performance bonds (CPPBs) with coupon penalties for missing emission targets are a concrete mechanism.

Use OAS methodology for fair value comparison

Option-adjusted spread enables apples-to-apples comparison by removing the spread component attributable to embedded options. Option value in spread terms = static spread − OAS.

Match portfolio duration to investment horizon

Immunization requires duration matching and periodic rebalancing; M² quantifies residual risk from cash flow dispersion. Zero-coupon bond at the liability date has M² = 0 (perfect immunization).

Distinguish spread duration from modified duration

Credit portfolios need spread duration to quantify sensitivity to spread changes independent of Treasury rate movements. Floating-rate bonds can have near-zero interest-rate duration but large spread durations.

Always begin credit analysis with the business model

Before financial ratios, understand how the company makes money. The three fundamental questions: What does it do? Where do revenues come from? Where does it incur costs? Sino-Forest shows that strong financial metrics on a flawed business model guarantee eventual disaster.

Read covenants within the context of corporate strategy

Covenants are not just legal provisions—they reveal management intentions. Loopholes, carve-outs, and covenant suspension clauses can dramatically shift bondholder risk. The Sirius XM case is the textbook example.

Employ constraint-tolerant investing

Retaining fallen angels, maturity violators, and low-liquidity bonds rather than mechanically selling them outperforms strict index replication by 6–7bps annually (Ng & Phelps, 1990–2009). Predictable forced-selling patterns from index rules create systematic value for flexible investors.

Use DTS (Duration Times Spread) for credit risk measurement

DTS = spread duration × credit spread. It captures both the magnitude of spread compensation and the sensitivity to spread changes in a single metric. Excess return volatility increases linearly with DTS across all quality segments, making it superior to spread duration alone.

Apply vector analysis to MBS, not static PSA

Static PSA assumes constant prepayment speeds—unrealistic for securities whose cash flows are driven by rate movements. Vector analysis allows speeds to change over time (e.g., 165 PSA for months 1–36, then different speeds), while Monte Carlo simulation with 512–1,024 paths captures the full path dependency of MBS cash flows.

What Didn't Fit

Interest Rates Are Mean-Reverting

Chapter 5 includes an empirical finding that is easy to miss amid the theory: over long periods, interest rates tend to revert toward their average. Extreme highs, like the 14% long Treasury yields of the early 1980s, and extreme lows, like the 0.62% ten-year yield of July 2020, have historically been followed by moves back toward the middle. Mean reversion is built into many interest-rate models, including the ones used to build the trees and simulations of Part IV. It is one of the few persistent patterns in rate behavior, though it says nothing about timing. For an investor it argues against extrapolating the latest trend in rates far into the future.

The Media Effect on Prepayments

Prepayment models capture the refinancing incentive, burnout and seasonality, but Chapter 12 points to a factor they struggle with: the media effect. When falling mortgage rates make headlines, homeowners who were not watching rates hear about refinancing and act, pushing prepayments above what the models predict. The effect is strongest when rates reach new lows that attract news coverage. It means two refinancing waves with the same rate incentive can produce different speeds. For mortgage investors, it is one more reason prepayment forecasts carry real uncertainty and why model results should be stress-tested.

The Five Largest Bond ETFs

As of 2020, the largest bond ETFs were iShares Core U.S. Aggregate (AGG) at $78.7 billion, Vanguard Total Bond Market (BND) at $59.4 billion, iShares Investment Grade Corporate (LQD) at $55.9 billion, Vanguard Intermediate-Term Corporate (VCIT) at $38.1 billion and Vanguard Short-Term Corporate (VCSH) at $30.7 billion. Three of the five hold corporate bonds, a market where individual bonds rarely trade. That concentration is why the liquidity mismatch discussed in Chapters 17 and 18 matters: the ETF trades all day while many of its bonds trade only occasionally. The dominance of passive vehicles in fixed income is also recent, building mostly over the last decade. For an individual investor, these few funds are the practical way to own the bond market, which makes understanding what they hold, and how they behave under stress, more important than ever.

Hedging a Procter & Gamble Bond

Chapters 29 and 30 use a single Procter & Gamble bond to compare hedging tools. With futures, the ratio of the bond's price value of a basis point to the CTD's, adjusted by the conversion factor, gives a hedge ratio of 1.157, or about 116 Treasury bond futures for $10 million of the bond. Adjusting for the fact that the corporate yield moves only about 0.90 times as much as the Treasury yield cuts the hedge by a tenth. With options, buying puts on futures sets a floor on the bond's value for the cost of the premium, while selling calls earns premium but caps the sale price. Neither is better in every scenario, and the example shows how each hedge's outcome depends on how rates and the spread move.

Synthetic Securities

Chapter 29 shows that a long bond plus a short position in futures on that bond behaves like a short-term riskless security, since the futures hedge removes the rate risk. Rearranging the same relationship creates a synthetic bond from bills plus long futures, or a synthetic future from bonds and borrowing. If one of the three is mispriced relative to the others, an investor can hold the cheapest combination. Managers use the same idea to shift between stocks and bonds with stock index and bond futures, without disturbing the underlying holdings. It is the futures version of the arbitrage that ties STRIPS to coupon Treasuries.

Implied Default Probability From CDS Spreads

Chapter 32 gives a quick approximation for reading default risk off a CDS spread: the annual default probability is roughly the spread divided by one minus the recovery rate. A 500 basis point spread with a 40% assumed recovery implies about 8.33% a year. With a 60% recovery, the same spread implies 12.5%, because each default costs less and so more defaults are needed to justify the price. The rule ignores the risk premium investors demand, so it overstates true default odds, but it is a useful sanity check when comparing a spread with an analyst's own view of the credit. It is the same arithmetic as the reduced-form models of Chapter 23, compressed into one line.

Cash Matching Variants

Chapter 27 describes refinements to pure cash flow matching. In symmetric cash matching, the portfolio may borrow briefly to meet a liability before a bond payment arrives, widening the set of usable bonds and lowering the cost. Combination matching, also called horizon matching, cash-matches the first few years of liabilities, where certainty matters most, and immunizes the rest. Both approaches trade a little precision for a meaningful saving, given that strict matching costs 3–7% more than immunization. They show how practitioners blend the two methods rather than choosing one.
