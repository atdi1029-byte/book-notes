## Chapter 1: Options Trading and the Individual Investor

### Options Are for Every Investor [MUST KNOW]
- Options can be used by any investor regardless of experience,
  trading style, or risk profile
- Can be applied to individual stocks, indexes, **ETFs**
- Best reason: allow you to **both manage risk AND grow assets**
  simultaneously
- Requires mental "rewiring" — new thinking about markets

### Investing vs. Trading: A Critical Distinction [MUST KNOW]
- ***Investing***: uses time and compounding to build wealth over
  long periods (buy-and-hold stocks, rental property)
  - **Not always ideal for options** — options have a time limit
  - Exception: **LEAPS** (long-term options) are viable
- ***Trading***: shorter-term, targeted, closed-end proposition
  - May hold positions for minutes, hours, days, or weeks
  - Options work for both short-term trading AND protecting
    longer-term investments

> "Options trading is a cautious and very precise exercise." — Duarte

### Pre-Trading Checklist [MUST KNOW]

Before starting any trading program, know three things:
1. Your **risk profile**
2. Your **financial situation**
3. Your **time commitment possibilities**

Six-step checklist:
1. **Check your financial balance sheet** — living expenses, debt,
   insurance; build a net worth statement
2. **Set realistic goals** — don't trade beyond your experience;
   don't risk too much per trade
3. **Know your willingness to take risks** — many options strategies
   have built-in safety nets that actually decrease risk vs. stocks
4. **Become a good analyst** — must analyze both overall market
   trend AND individual securities; technical + fundamental skills
5. **Paper trade before deploying** — simulates live conditions,
   saves money and headaches (see Chapter 7)
6. **⚠ WARNING: Never trade with money you aren't willing to lose**
   — don't trade with car payments or rent money

### Understanding Options [MUST KNOW]
- Options based on stocks, stock indexes, and ETFs
- **Calls** — gain value when underlying stock **rises**
- **Puts** — gain value when underlying stock **falls**
- Options can: limit portfolio risk, protect individual positions,
  generate income through spreads and writes

**Risk management framework — understand:**
1. What conditions (market-wide and individual security) to analyze
2. Using proper trade mechanics when creating a position
3. Recognizing and following trading rules and requirements
4. Understanding what variables make any position gain/lose value

### Option Essentials [MUST KNOW]
- A **listed** option = contractual agreement with standard terms
  governed by the same rules across all contracts
- **Buying an option** → purchasing specific **rights**
- **Selling an option** → acquiring specific **obligations**
- All guaranteed by the **OCC** — counterparty risk eliminated

**Two critical risks unique to options:**
1. **Time value decay** — options lose value as expiration nears;
   stocks don't have this
2. **Leverage** — option prices change in larger percentage moves

**Call rights:**
- Buy a specific quantity of underlying stock (exercise)
- By a certain date (expiration)
- At a specified price (the **strike price**)

**Put rights:**
- Sell a specific quantity of stock by a certain date at a
  specified strike price
- If you own the right to sell at $60 and stock drops below $60,
  those rights become more valuable

**Two key pricing facts:** [MUST KNOW]
- Options with **more time until expiration are more expensive**
- Options with **more attractive strike prices are more expensive**

**Basic trading rules:**
- Plan your exit from a position **before you enter** — simple but
  required; will save money and heartache if position moves against you
- Manage emotions via a trading plan with easy-to-follow rules that
  covers different scenarios

### How Options Are Used [MUST KNOW]
**Three ways to reduce risk:**
1. **Hedging** — option value goes opposite the underlying; keeps
   total position value high when underlying falls; cheaper and
   lower dollar-risk than short selling
2. **Leverage** — options cost less than stocks; $500 option ≈
   $5,000 stock investment in profit potential; less capital, similar
   return potential
3. **Income generation** — through spreads and writes

### Differentiating Option Styles [SHOULD KNOW]
- **American-style options** — most stock options; can be exercised
  any time before expiration; last trading date and exercise date
  are the same
- **European-style options** — most index options; last trading date
  and exercise date **can differ**
- **Always know which style you are using before you trade**

### ETFs and Options [SHOULD KNOW]
- ETFs trade like stocks during market hours (unlike mutual funds
  which settle at close)
- Most popular optionable ETFs: **SPY** (S&P 500) and **QQQ**
  (Nasdaq-100)
- ETFs on commodity indexes let you participate in commodity markets
  without trading futures

### Reducing Directional Bias [MUST KNOW]
- **Directional bias**: profits tied to price direction (long needs
  up, short needs down)
- Option combination strategies can profit whether underlying rises
  OR falls, AND in **sideways markets**

### Controlling Emotions [MUST KNOW]
- Emotional trading is the path to big losses
- Solution: **rules** and an **anticipatory trading plan**

**Key elements of a good trading plan:**
- **Access to proper equipment** — technology, mobile, backup
- **Knowledge of time commitment** — can't day-trade if you can't
  devote 2–3 hours at a stretch
- **Access to good information** — real-time quote/charting service
- **Flawless trade execution** — reliable online broker
- **Educational component** — work on technical AND fundamental
  analysis skills daily

## Chapter 2: Introducing Options

### Option Contract Basics [MUST KNOW]
- A **financial option** = contractual agreement between two parties
- This book covers **listed options** — standardized contracts on
  exchanges (not OTC)
- **Option contracts give the owner rights and the seller obligations**

**Core definitions:**
- **Call option:** Owner has the **right** to buy; seller has the
  **obligation** to sell — a specific number of shares at a
  specific price by a predetermined date
- **Put option:** Owner has the **right** to sell; seller has the
  **obligation** to buy — a specific number of shares at a specific
  price by a specific date

**Use options for:**
- Benefiting from upside moves for less money
- Profiting from downside without short-selling risk
- Protecting a stock position or entire portfolio during downturns

**Two key risks to always remember:** [MUST KNOW]
- **Limited life:** If the anticipated move doesn't happen before
  expiration, you lose your entire initial investment
- **Wrong strategy = major losses:** Options strategies that take on
  more risk than necessary can cause losses that overwhelm winners

### Comparing Options to Other Securities [SHOULD KNOW]
- Options are **derivatives** — value derived from an underlying
- Stocks = partial ownership; bonds = debt instrument; options = no
  ownership of tangible assets, limited life
- **ETFs** track an index or sector; some use **swaps** for leverage
  (check prospectus — swaps misfired catastrophically in **2008**)
- Indexes = pools of assets; NOT directly tradeable; buy ETFs or
  mutual funds that track them

### Valuing Options [MUST KNOW]
**To value an option, you must know:**
- Type (call or put) and strike price
- Price of underlying security
- Past trading pattern (volatility) of underlying
- Time remaining until expiration

### Options Mechanics [MUST KNOW]
- Not all stocks have options; those that do feature multiple strike
  prices and expiration dates — the **option chain**
- **Open interest:** Number of existing contracts for each strike;
  higher = more liquid
- **Option symbol format:** underlying + yymmdd + C/P + strike×100
  - Example: AAPL170505C00148000 = AAPL May 5, 2017 $148 Call

**Option multiplier:** Most stock options deliver **100 shares** per
contract; multiply quoted price × 100 = premium paid

### Three Expiration Cycles [MUST KNOW]

| Cycle | Months |
|-------|--------|
| I | January, April, July, October |
| II | February, May, August, November |
| III | March, June, September, December |

- Each option features at least current month + following month
- **LEAPS** (Long-term Equity Anticipation Securities) typically
  expire only in January and June
- Strike price increments: $0.50, $1, $2.50, up to $100 depending
  on popularity and moneyness

> WARNING: Options lose time value at an accelerating rate as
> expiration nears. **Never hold an option you plan to sell with
> 30 days or less to expiration.**

### Expiration Decision Time [MUST KNOW]
- Train yourself to know what you'll do with open positions
  **well before expiration**
- At expiration, in-the-money options are automatically exercised
- Choices: exercise, close prior to auto-exercise, or let expire
  worthless
- **a.m. settlement:** last trading day = day BEFORE expiration
- **p.m. settlement:** last trading day = same day as expiration
- **Almost always better to close an ITM option before auto-exercise**

### Opening and Closing Positions [MUST KNOW]
Standard order language:
- **Buy to Open** — create new long position
- **Sell to Close** — exit existing long position
- **Sell to Open** — create new short position
- **Buy to Close** — exit existing short position

Open interest displayed with **one-day delay** (prior day's action).

### Assignment Risk [MUST KNOW]
- **Covered call:** Own shares when selling call = covered; if
  assigned, you sell your shares
- **Naked call:** Don't own shares when selling call = unlimited
  risk (stock can rise indefinitely)
- **Short put assigned:** Obligated to buy stock at strike; if you
  have no offsetting position, you'll own stock purchased above
  market value

## Chapter 3: Trading Places: Where the Action Happens

### U.S. Options Exchanges [SHOULD KNOW]
- CBOE, Nasdaq NOM, ISE, NYSE ARCA, PHLX, BATS
- All linked electronically; most orders auto-routed to best price
- Full list at **OCC** (www.theocc.com)

### Market Participants [MUST KNOW]
- **Brokers:** Issue option approvals in levels reflecting risk;
  need margin agreement for complex strategies
- **Market makers:** Provide liquidity; required to take other side
  of trade at quoted level; keep markets orderly
- **OCC (Options Clearing Corporation):** Guarantees option sellers
  meet obligations; options on multiple exchanges have same terms;
  website: www.optionsclearing.com
- **OIC (Options Industry Council):** Investor education org;
  www.optionscentral.com

**Trading rule:** Concentrate on contracts that are more actively
traded — easier to enter AND exit.

### Contract Pricing Rules [MUST KNOW]
- Options trade in increments of **$0.01, $0.05, and $0.10**
- Premium = quoted price × **100** (multiplier)
- Example: option at $2.80 → you pay **$280** + commissions

### Three Main Risks of Options [MUST KNOW]
1. **Expiration risk:** ITM options auto-exercise at expiration,
   converting low-cost option to high-cost stock position; manage
   by exiting long options **at least 30 days before expiration**
2. **Leverage:** Small percentage move in stock = large percentage
   move in option; can result in 100% loss on a small adverse move
3. **Time value:** For buyers, time value is a **headwind** (must
   move more to break even); for sellers, a **tailwind**

### Key Costs Unique to Options [MUST KNOW]
- **Liquidity:** Lower liquidity → wider spread → higher costs;
  lean toward higher open interest, higher volume options
- **Time:** More time until expiration = more expensive; every day
  you own, the option's time value shrinks (**Theta**)
- **Volatility:** More volatile underlying = more expensive option;
  **Vega** measures volatility's impact

**Slippage** = money lost due to bid-ask spread; minimize by
trading liquid options.

### Option Greeks [MUST KNOW]

- **Delta:** Expected change in option value per **$1 change** in
  underlying stock price
  - Calls: 0 to +1.00; Puts: 0 to −1.00
  - ATM delta ≈ ±0.50 (regardless of past volatility)
- **Gamma:** Expected change in **delta** per $1 change in
  underlying — how fast delta shifts
- **Theta:** Option's expected **daily decline due to time decay**
- **Vega:** Expected change in option value due to changes in
  **implied volatility**
- **Rho:** Impact of **interest rate changes** on option value
  (smallest factor; least coverage)

> TIP: Use CBOE option calculators (www.cboe.com) to get Greek
> values by entering stock price, strike, time, and option quote.

**Table 3-1 example — ABC at $20, moves to $21, 20 strike:**

| Type | Moneyness | Value | Delta | Gamma |
|------|-----------|-------|-------|-------|
| Call at $20 | ATM | $1.10 | +0.50 | 0.1962 |
| Put at $20 | ATM | $1.00 | −0.50 | 0.1931 |
| Call at $21 | ITM | $1.60 | +0.70 | 0.1438 |
| Put at $21 | OTM | $0.50 | −0.30 | 0.1467 |

### Historical vs. Implied Volatility [MUST KNOW]
- **Historical Volatility (HV)** = measure of past price movement;
  also called **Statistical Volatility (SV)**
  - Calculated via annualized standard deviation of daily price
    changes; divide standard deviation by **0.0630** to annualize
  - Higher HV → expected bigger daily moves, less predictable
- **Implied Volatility (IV)** = volatility implied by current
  option price; includes HV but also supply/demand pricing
  pressures and psychological components
  - IV is more abstract and forward-looking; HV uses past data
  - A major one-day move has larger psychological impact on IV
    than its mathematical impact on 100-day HV
  - IV is **part of extrinsic value** only; plays no role in
    intrinsic value

**Option pricing model:**
- Compares theoretical value (using HV) to actual market price
  (using IV)
- Difference = premium or discount between expected and actual
- **Black-Scholes** (Fisher Black + Myron Scholes) = first option
  pricing model; earned them a **Nobel Prize in Economics**
- Two uses of calculator: (1) enter HV → get theoretical value;
  (2) enter market price → solve for IV

## Chapter 3 / 4: Option Values, Risk Graphs, and Risk Management

### Intrinsic and Extrinsic Value [MUST KNOW]
- **Intrinsic value:** Value if option exercised right now
  - Call: Stock Price − Strike Price (≥ 0)
  - Put: Strike Price − Stock Price (≥ 0)
  - Can never be less than zero
- **Extrinsic value (time value):** Option Price − Intrinsic Value
  - Added potential value based on time remaining + expected moves

### Option Moneyness [MUST KNOW]
- **In-the-money (ITM):** Intrinsic Value > 0
- **At-the-money (ATM):** Intrinsic Value = 0 (stock ≈ strike)
- **Out-of-the-money (OTM):** Intrinsic Value < 0; all extrinsic
  value; OTM options expire worthless 100% of the time if not
  reversed before expiration

### Leverage Example (Stock vs. Option) [MUST KNOW]
**Setup: Stock ABC at $43; $40 strike call = $4; stock moves to $47**

| | Stock (50% margin) | Call Option |
|---|---|---|
| Initial investment | $2,150 | $400 |
| Gain | $400 (+18.6%) | $300 (+75%) |

**Severe downside: stock drops to $30**

| | Stock (50% margin) | Call Option |
|---|---|---|
| Loss | ($1,300) = 60% | ($400) = 100% |

**Key insight:** Option loss % higher, but **capped** in dollar
terms. Stock on margin can generate margin calls and uncapped
further losses. Real power of options = **limited loss nature**.

### Breakeven Formulas [MUST KNOW]
```
Call Breakeven = Call Strike Price + Call Premium Paid
Put Breakeven = Put Strike Price − Put Premium Paid
```
- Call profits: unlimited above breakeven (mirrors long stock)
- Put profits: limited but high (stock can only fall to zero)

- Long stock + long put = insurance against major downside
- Minimum loss = (Purchase Price − Strike Price) + Put Premium
- Unlimited profit potential above new breakeven
- Creates risk-reward profile similar to a long call option

### Worst-Case Scenario Thinking [MUST KNOW]
> "Rule #1: don't lose money. Rule #2: remember rule #1."
> — Warren Buffett

- Before analyzing potential gains, examine the downside first
- Being unprepared for worst-case scenarios costs money
- Primary focus: limiting losses; profits naturally follow
  well-designed risk-limited strategy

### Three Basic Combination Positions (Stock + Options) [MUST KNOW]
1. **Married long put** — long stock + long put (protection)
2. **Covered short call** — long stock + short call (income)
3. **Collar** — long stock + long put + short call (capped risk/reward)

**Option-Only Combinations** — driven by market conditions:
- High IV, low IV, sideways movement, directional movement
- Build by overlaying individual risk graphs to see combined
  risk/reward profile

## Chapter 5: Analyzing Mood Swings in the Market

### Macro Factors [SHOULD KNOW]
- **Low/falling interest rates** = generally bullish for stocks
- **Rising rates** = eventually negative for stocks
- Global central banks (Fed, PBOC, ECB) all affect markets now;
  Fed remains most influential

### Market Breadth [MUST KNOW]
- **Market breadth** = heartbeat of the market; tracks internal
  components of advances and declines
- Components: advancing/declining issues, new highs/lows, up/down
  volume, issues above/below moving averages
- **Bearish divergence:** Market advances but declining stocks
  outnumber advancers in number AND volume

### Advance-Decline (A-D) Line [MUST KNOW]
**Formula:** A-D Line = # Advancing Issues − # Declining Issues
(cumulative daily running total)

- Visual inspection more important than specific values
- Track whether line trends up, down, or sideways vs. the index
- **Historical fact:** Every significant decline since 1987 has been
  preceded by a **negative divergence** in the NYSE A-D Line

[PAGE IMAGE: page_089.png]

**A-D Line caveat:** Index can rise even when decliners outnumber
advancers if large-cap components dominate

**Refine A-D with three indicators:**
- **20-day SMA** — line above SMA = up; below SMA = searching for
  direction; smooths short-term noise
- **RSI** — low readings mark market bottoms; high readings mark
  tops or downside reversals
- **ROC** — direction change warns of A-D trend change; most
  effective when RSI and ROC confirm each other

[PAGE IMAGE: page_091.png]

**A-D Ratio:** Adv/Dec oscillates; below 1.0 when decliners lead;
extreme lows provide **timely** bottom signals; extreme highs
**confirm** reversals after the fact

### Sentiment Analysis [MUST KNOW]
- **Sentiment** = overriding bullish/bearish bias of the market
- Greed drives advances; fear drives declines — cycle repeats
  regardless of economic backdrop
- Call volume typically exceeds put volume (market has bullish
  bias); when fear rises, **put volume increases**

**Three keys to using sentiment tools:**
1. Know how the indicator is constructed
2. Understand its historical extremes and implications
3. Recognize market structure changes that affect indicator levels

### Put-to-Call (P:C) Ratios [MUST KNOW]
- Created by **Martin Zweig** — divided put volume by call volume
  (Zweig predicted and profited from the **1987 crash**)
- **Contrarian measures:** Extremely low P:C = bearish;
  Extremely high P:C = **bullish** (excessive fear = reversal due)

**CBOE Equity P:C Ratio:**
- Total CBOE stock option volume; readings above **0.90–1.0**
  suggest increased fear and oversold conditions

**CBOE Index-Only P:C Ratio:**
- More sophisticated traders; readings above **2.4** (≈ 3 SD above
  mean) signal near-term tops

**ISE Sentiment Index (ISEE):**
- Inverse format (call/put, not put/call); customer buys only
  (excludes market makers)
- Readings below **85** (2 SD below mean) = extreme bearishness →
  contrarian buy signal
- Updated every 20 minutes during trading day

### SPY Put/Call Ratio with 5-Day SMA [MUST KNOW]
- When smoothed P:C ratio crosses above +1SD:
  near-term bottom signaled **6 of 8 times** in test period
- Use 5-day SMA to smooth noisy daily ratio data

[PAGE IMAGE: page_102.png]

### Put Volume Indicator (PVI) [SHOULD KNOW]
- Created by **John Bollinger**; daily put volume ÷ 10-day SMA of
  put volume; extreme readings = excessive fear or complacency
- High PVI readings often preceded acceleration of selling before
  a subsequent reversal; NOT an immediately contrarian signal

[PAGE IMAGE: page_104.png]

### VIX: Spelling Fear the Wall Street Way [MUST KNOW]
- **VIX** = blended implied volatility calculated from specific
  S&P 500 Index option contracts; published daily by CBOE
- **Climbing VIX** = bearish conditions in SPX and broader market
- **Declining VIX** = neutral to bullish conditions
- Overly bearish → high VIX → fear exhausted → reversal → VIX
  declines; **look for VIX reversals to confirm market bottoms**
- Related indexes: **VXN** (Nasdaq), **VXO** (OEX/former VIX calc)

[PAGE IMAGE: page_107.png]

### Applying Breadth and Sentiment Tools [MUST KNOW]
- Most tools: weakness = bearish; **extremely bearish** = bullish
  (contrarian)
- Use 2–3 option sentiment tools; not 5–6 of same type (indicator
  burnout); combine complementary, not redundant signals
- Calculate mean ± SD to identify extreme readings; use Excel:
  `=stdev(data)`
- Qualitative judgment needed: don't include all historical data;
  conditions change over time

## Chapter 6: Sector Analysis: Technical and Fundamental

### Chart Types [MUST KNOW]
- **Line chart:** Plots closing prices only; great big-picture
  view; no gap or intra-day information
- **OHLC bar chart:** Open (left tab), high/low (vertical bar),
  close (right tab); long bar = volatile day; close near high =
  strength; close near low = weakness; shows gaps
- **Candlestick chart:** Most used by professionals; thickened body
  between open and close; patterns describe bull/bear battle;
  best on daily charts; also shows gaps

**Best practice:** Use both — longer-term line charts for trend,
OHLC/candlestick for intra-period action and volatility

### Time Horizon for Sector Analysis [MUST KNOW]
1. **Monthly charts** → long-term major trends
2. **Weekly charts** → intermediate-term trends on indexes/sectors
3. **Daily charts** → short-term minor trends on sectors

### Support and Resistance [MUST KNOW]
- **Support:** Area where buyers return to a falling trend; two
  touches establish the level; third touch confirms
- **Resistance:** Area where sellers return to a rising trend; same
  construction rule
- **Longer** a price serves as support/resistance → **stronger** it is
- Use for: entry/exit points, price targets, stop-loss levels,
  risk-reward calculation
- **Previous support often becomes resistance** (and vice versa)
  after a break

### Trend Analysis [MUST KNOW]
- **Uptrend:** Rising line connecting **higher lows**; also
  characterized by **higher highs**; third test confirms
- **Downtrend:** Declining line connecting **lower highs**; also
  characterized by **lower lows**
- Use trendline breaks as entry/exit signals; consider two
  trendlines (closing data + market lows) to create a **channel**

### Moving Averages [MUST KNOW]
- **SMA (Simple MA):** Equally weights all closes for period
- **EMA (Exponential MA):** Greater weight to recent data; more
  responsive; preferred for trading decisions
- Standard settings:
  - **20-day** → short-term trends
  - **50-day** → intermediate-term trends
  - **200-day** → long-term trends; break = significant
- 200-day on weekly chart = **40-week MA** (5 trading days/week)

### Relative Ratios and Sector Rotation [MUST KNOW]
- **Relative ratio line** = primary security ÷ secondary security
  - Rising = primary outperforming; falling = underperforming
- **Sector rotation:** Cash flows between sectors as economic
  conditions change; favor outperforming, avoid underperforming
- **10 Select Sector SPDR ETFs** + SPY cover the entire S&P 500
  (XLF, XLP, XLI, XLV, XLK, XLU, XLY, XLRE, XLB, XLE)

### Volatility Tools [MUST KNOW]

**Statistical Volatility (SV / Historical Volatility):**
- Annualized standard deviation of closing prices
- Plots a line for trend and comparison

**Average True Range (ATR)** — developed by **Welles Wilder:**
- True Range = largest of: (1) High−Low today; (2) Close
  yesterday−High today; (3) Close yesterday−Low today
- ATR = exponential MA of TR; captures price gaps
- Lagging indicator but: sharp ATR rise often accompanied by
  increase in IV for the stock's options

- ATR was a **better timing indicator** of future action than HV

### Bollinger Bands [MUST KNOW]
- Developed by **John Bollinger**
- Structure: 20-period SMA ± two standard deviations
- Bands **contract** in low volatility; **expand** in high volatility
- **Bandwidth (BW)** = (Upper − Lower) ÷ SMA; when BW at 6-month
  low = **squeeze candidate** (pre-breakout consolidation)
- **%b** = where price is relative to bandwidth:
  - 0–100: within bands; <0: below lower band (bearish);
    >100: above upper band (bullish)
- Prices near band ≠ extended; often reflects strength and
  breakout momentum; look for pullbacks to MA for entries

### Price Projections [MUST KNOW]
- Price projections identify exits for BOTH losses and profits
  before establishing a position
- Methods: support/resistance, trendlines, regression channels,
  Fibonacci retracements/extensions

**Critical rules:**
- **Potential exit** = profit-taking; can be adjusted based on
  changing conditions (extend gains OR take early profits)

### Implied Volatility Rules of Thumb [MUST KNOW]
- **HV (Historical Volatility)** = past actual price movement
- **IV (Implied Volatility)** = forward-looking, derived from
  option prices; circular relationship with HV
- **High IV** → correctly predicts large future moves most of time
- **Low IV** → correctly predicts small future moves most of time
- **Low IV + technical setup** = excellent entry points
- **Extended high IV** = excellent exit points
- IV 30% on $100 stock → stock may trade between $70–$130 next year
  (based on 1 standard deviation = 68.2% of outcomes)

### IV Crush [MUST KNOW]
- **IV crush** = IV was high, then drops significantly after
  uncertainty resolves (earnings report, product launch, etc.)
- **Dangerous:** Selling high-IV options hoping for an IV crush —
  the underlying price itself may move significantly after the
  event, overpowering the benefit of declining IV

### How IV Varies [MUST KNOW]
**By expiration:** Different expirations can have different IV;
higher IV in one month = stock expected to move more during that
expiration period

**By strike price:**
- ATM IV is usually the **lowest**
- **Skew charts** (Chapter 15) show IV by strike price
- The deeper ITM an option is, the **less IV matters** to total
  price (more intrinsic = less time value = less IV sensitivity)

**Short option strategies sweet spot:**
- Sell options with **30–45 days to expiration** — combines
  accelerating time decay with meaningful remaining time value
- Time-value decay is NOT linear — accelerates like a waterfall
  approaching expiration

| Real feedback via P&L | No emotional preparation for real losses |
| Incorporates all trade costs | No assignment risk experience |
| Identifies unconsidered issues | Doesn't address margin requirements |
| No account losses | No trade execution skill building |

### Trading Systems [MUST KNOW]
- **Trading system** = specific, rigidly defined entry AND exit rules
- When using: establish positions for **all** buy signals; exit on
  **all** exit signals; no discretion
- Two best things: minimizes emotions; allows **backtesting**
- Adding discretion destroys both advantages

**Good trading system characteristics:**
- Profitable across variety of markets and conditions
- Outperforms buy-and-hold
- Manageable **drawdowns**
- Diversifies trading tools
- Suits your style and time availability

### Backtesting Procedure [MUST KNOW]
- **Backtest** = uses past data to determine if system generates
  stable profits; must cover bullish, bearish, AND sideways periods
- **Drawdown** = cumulative account losses from consecutive losing
  trades; key risk management metric

**9-step backtesting process:**
1. Identify strategy basis
2. Identify entry and exit rules
3. Identify market and backtest period
4. Identify account assumptions (allocations)
5. Test system, evaluate results
6. Identify filters to minimize losing trades
7. Add filter, test again, evaluate
8. Add risk management component
9. Test and evaluate final results

**Stability = key metric:** Winning trades' average profit should
exceed average loss of losing trades; no handful of trades driving
all profits; manageable drawdowns

### Trading as a Business [MUST KNOW]
- Losses are **operating expenses** — minimize but don't try to
  eliminate; manage risk instead
- **Strategy mastery** = right conditions in place AND position
  managed with discipline; can take years to evolve
- Look for the right setup rather than trading as often as possible

### Strategies Matched to Market Conditions [MUST KNOW]

| Market Condition | Strategies |
|-----------------|------------|
| Bullish, low volatility | ATM long call; OTM married put; ATM call debit spread |
| Bullish, high volatility | Covered calls; credit spreads |
| Bearish, low volatility | ATM long put; OTM put calendar spread; ATM put debit spread |
| Bearish, high volatility | ATM call credit spread; OTM naked call |
| Range-bound, high volatility | Butterfly; condor (2 short + 1 lower long + 1 higher long) |
| Range-bound, low volatility | ATM calendar spread; ATM diagonal spread |

**Biggest strategy determinant = your financial goal:**
some strategies target capital gains, some income, some hedging

### Longevity and Position Sizing [MUST KNOW]
- **Longevity** = patience, staying power, showing up every day;
  foundation is limited-loss strategies + paper trading
- Two position sizing methods:
  1. Maximum **dollar amount** per trade
  2. Maximum **percentage** per trade (preferred — scales with
     account)
- Options = leveraged position; **don't allocate same amount as
  stocks**
- Start with **one-option contract sizes** for new strategies;
  scale up as skills develop

### Trading Plan Guidelines [MUST KNOW]
- **Write it down** — verbal plans don't work; keep visible
- **Set realistic goals** — small account → start with covering
  a monthly restaurant outing
- **Keep excellent records** — trades, results, expenses
  (also useful at tax time)
- **Let plan evolve** — if profitable, consider increasing scope

### Managing Costs [MUST KNOW]
**Education:** Largest early cost = **learning curve** (wrong
entries, bad sizing, poor execution); declines with experience

**Trading costs:**
- **Commission** + **slippage** (bid-ask spread cost)
- Calculate commission + slippage % for different contract sizes
  (1, 5, 10 contracts) at different price points ($1, $5, $10)

**Margin costs:** Short option positions have margin requirements;
covered vs. naked is the primary consideration

**Taxes:**
- Options in retirement accounts defer taxes
- Establishing options on an existing position may trigger a tax event
- Cumulative costs must outpace buy-and-hold in the long run

### Understanding Option Orders [MUST KNOW]
- Options have no fixed float — contracts **created by marketplace**
  as needed; open interest tracked by OCC end-of-day
- Opening a new position **increases** open interest
- Closing a position **decreases** open interest

**Order entry table:**

| Position | Entry | Exit |
|----------|-------|------|
| Long Call | Buy to Open (BCO) | Sell to Close (SCC) |
| Short Call | Sell to Open (SCO) | Buy to Close (BCC) |

### Order Types [MUST KNOW]

| Order | Guarantees |
|-------|------------|
| Market order | Execution |
| Limit order | Price |
| Stop order | Execution |
| Stop-limit order | Price |

- **Limit orders:** Best for **entering** positions
- **Market orders:** Only type that **guarantees exit**
- **Stop orders:** Risk management tool; triggers market order at
  stop price OR when bid/ask reaches stop price
- **Day order** = current session only
- **GTC (Good 'til canceled)** = stays active until cancelled
  (or broker clears, often 60 days)

### Option Stop Order Challenges [MUST KNOW]
- Options stop triggered by: (1) trade at stop price OR (2) bid/ask
  reaching stop price — because option volume is much lower than
  stocks, the **quote frequently triggers** the stop, not a trade
- Worst case: underlying gaps against you at open, triggering stop
  well below your risk target — still better than no stop at all
- **OCO (One Cancels Other):** Allows stop-loss + profit-limit
  orders simultaneously; when one executes, other auto-cancels
  - **Without OCO:** Two live orders for same position is very
    dangerous — both can execute on a strong swing

### Combination Orders [MUST KNOW]
- Enter as single combined order (better price) OR leg in
  individually
- All-or-nothing execution: both legs execute or neither does

**Married put example** (ABC at $33.12, Jan 30 Put at $1.05):
- Buy 100 shares + Buy to Open 1 Jan 30 Put
- Limit Net Debit: **$34.17**

**Bull call spread example** (Buy $30 call at $3.30, sell $35 call
at $1.00):
- Net debit at quotes = $2.30; order at **$2.25** to save
- Buy to Open 1 Mar 30 Call + Sell to Open 1 Mar 35 Call for
  Net Debit **$2.25**, Good for Day

**Spread execution tip:** Reduce debit slightly or increase credit
slightly vs. market on spread orders — exchange traders like hedged
positions and will often fill

### Contingent/Conditional Orders [MUST KNOW]
- Trigger based on movement in the **underlying stock** (not the
  option price itself)
- Conditions: closing price, intraday price, % change, quote level
- Key distinction: stop = **active on exchange** (visible);
  contingent = **active on broker's system** (invisible to market)
- **Advantage of contingent:** Lets you use technical stock-level
  exit points for the option position
- **Disadvantage:** Much less certainty about where option will
  trade when triggered; use option calculator + Greeks to estimate

### Rolling Options [MUST KNOW]
- **Rolling out** = close current; open new same-strike, later
  expiration; gains time value; common near expiration
- **Rolling up** = change strike higher; avoid assignment OR capture
  high IV at higher strike
- **Rolling down** = change strike lower; avoid assignment OR
  capture high IV at lower strike
- Can combine: **roll out and up** or **roll out and down**

> REMEMBER: Keep it simple. Too many moving parts = missed signals
> + large losses in a hurry.

### Hedging with Index Options [MUST KNOW]
- **Correlation**: range –1 to +1; measures how closely portfolio moves with an index
- **Perfect hedge**: options combined delta equals –1.0 times the stock's +1.0 delta
- ATM puts have delta near –0.50; need two ATM puts per 100 shares for near-perfect hedge

**OEX Portfolio Hedge Example (portfolio = $225,000, OEX at 1040):**
[PAGE IMAGE: page_244.png]

- 2 × March 1040 puts @ $205 = $41,100 total cost
- 2 × 1040 × 100 = $208,000 protection value = **92.44% coverage**
- Delta approach: 2 × March 1050 puts (delta –0.549) = combined delta –1.10 → $231,000 effective protection
- As put moves deeper ITM, delta approaches –1.0 → hedge becomes more perfect over time

**Delta formulas for hedging:**
- Position Delta = (# contracts × option delta) + (shares × +1)
- For near-perfect hedge: position delta should approach zero

### Adjusted Options — Corporate Actions [MUST KNOW]
- **Adjusted options**: existing contracts modified when corporate actions occur during the option's life
- Triggers: stock splits, large cash dividends, mergers/acquisitions, spinoffs
- **Regular dividends** do NOT typically result in adjustment; **special dividends** do

**2:1 Split**: contracts doubled, strike prices halved, new symbol created
**3:2 Split**: multiplier changes to 150, strike price changes to 2/3 of original

**Corporate action deliverable packages:**
- Merger: 100 shares original + shares of acquired company
- Spinoff: 100 shares original + shares of new company
- Large cash dividend: 100 shares + cash amount
- Acquisition (absorbed): shares of acquiring company only

> TIP: Never create new positions using adjusted options you don't fully understand. No free money exists on Wall Street.

**Detecting adjusted options**: check CBOE (www.cboe.com) for symbol changes, contract multiplier changes, shares per contract, and style changes.

## Chapter 11: Increasing Profit Potential and Decreasing Risk

### Options Reduce Risk by Reducing Capital [MUST KNOW]
- Options allow leverage while **decreasing total amount at risk**
- Long stock vs. long call: call caps your loss at the premium paid; stock can lose its full value
- Tradeoff: call position has higher breakeven, slower profit accrual, and time constraint

> REMEMBER: Establish long option positions when **implied volatility (IV) is relatively low** to minimize cost and risk of IV decline.

- Long call: flat max loss line (premium paid), breakeven at ~$40
- Key: call has dramatically lower max loss but slower profit accrual and higher breakeven

**How option values change:**
- Increase/decrease with underlying price moves
- Increase/decrease with implied volatility changes
- **Decrease** as time passes (theta decay)

- **LEAPS** = Long-term Equity AntiCiPation Security; an option with 9 months to 3 years to expiration
- Expiration month is almost always **January**; new contracts created each May–July
- More expensive than short-term options but provide greater time for ITM movement
- LEAPS do NOT receive dividends — factor this into cost vs. stock ownership

### Predicting for Options Success [MUST KNOW]
Three factors required for successful directional options trade:
1. **Direction**: trade with the trend; or for contrarians, trade when momentum is weakening
2. **Magnitude**: estimate price projection using technical/fundamental tools; focus on higher-probability (higher delta) moves; take partial profits as move plays out
3. **Time**: identify time horizons first, then check option chains; best anchors are earnings releases and economic reports

> TIP: Don't get greedy. Consider taking partial profits when move is partially complete.

**Rule**: Stock can stay flat for months (just "dead money"); with options, time decay punishes you for being right on direction but wrong on timing.

### Vertical Spreads — Overview [MUST KNOW]
- **Vertical spread**: long + short option of the same type, same expiration month, different strike prices
- "Vertical" = how strike prices line up in the option chain
- Created for either a **debit** or a **credit**
- Both types have **limited risk AND limited reward**
- Each option leg is called a **leg** of the spread

**Four vertical spread types:**
| Strategy | Outlook | Type | Pay | Max Risk | Max Reward |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bull call spread | Bullish | Debit | Net debit | Initial debit | (Strike width × 100) – debit |
| Bear put spread | Bearish | Debit | Net debit | Initial debit | (Strike width × 100) – debit |
| Bear call spread | Bearish | Credit | Receive credit | (Strike width × 100) – credit | Initial credit |
| Bull put spread | Bullish | Credit | Receive credit | (Strike width × 100) – credit | Initial credit |

### Bull Call Spread (Debit) [MUST KNOW]
**Example:** ABC at $37.65; Buy 1 Jan 35 Call @ $4.20 / Sell 1 Jan 40 Call @ $1.50
- **Debit = Max Risk** = ($4.20 – $1.50) × 100 = **$270**
- **Max Reward** = ($40 – $35) × 100 – $270 = **$230**
- **Breakeven** = 35 + (4.20 – 1.50) = **$37.70**

### Bear Put Spread (Debit) [MUST KNOW]
**Example:** XYZ at $50.85; Buy 1 Jan 50 Put @ $2.75 / Sell 1 Jan 45 Put @ $1.30
- **Debit = Max Risk** = (2.75 – 1.30) × 100 = **$145**
- **Max Reward** = ($50 – $45) × 100 – $145 = **$355**
- **Breakeven** = 50 – (2.75 – 1.30) = **$48.55**

[PAGE IMAGE: page_263.png]

**Table 11-2: Debit Spread Formulas**
| | Bull Call | Bear Put |
|---|---|---|
| Risk | Initial debit | Initial debit |
| Reward | [(Higher – lower strike) × 100] – debit | [(Higher – lower strike) × 100] – debit |
| Breakeven | Long strike + (long price – short price) | Long strike – (long price – short price) |

### Bear Call Spread (Credit) [MUST KNOW]
**Example:** XYZ bearish; Buy 1 Jan 55 Call @ $0.95 / Sell 1 Jan 50 Call @ $3.20
- **Credit = Max Reward** = (3.20 – 0.95) × 100 = **$225**
- **Max Risk** = ($55 – $50) × 100 – $225 = **$275**
- **Breakeven** = 50 + (3.20 – 0.95) = **$52.25**

**Example:** ABC bullish; Buy 1 Jan 35 Put @ $1.70 / Sell 1 Jan 40 Put @ $4.10
- **Credit = Max Reward** = (4.10 – 1.70) × 100 = **$240**
- **Max Risk** = ($40 – $35) × 100 – $240 = **$260**
- **Breakeven** = 40 – (4.10 – 1.70) = **$37.60**

**Table 11-3: Credit Spread Formulas**
| | Bear Call | Bull Put |
|---|---|---|
| Risk | [(Higher – lower) × 100] – credit | [(Higher – lower) × 100] – credit |

## Chapter 12: Combination Strategies — Spreads and Other Wild Things

### Covered Calls [MUST KNOW]
- Sell calls against owned stock → credit received reduces cost basis; caps upside at short call strike
- Best scenario: sell calls with **30–45 days to expiration** when IV is relatively high
- Optimal when stock is **moving sideways** — earns income while waiting for directional move
- **Rule**: short call strike price should be **above stock purchase price**
- **WARNING**: Never sell a covered call on a stock you MUST hold — creates obligation to sell at strike. And never sell stock without first buying back the call — you'd have a naked short call

**Covered Call Formulas:**
```
Max Risk = (Stock Price × shares) – Call Premium
Max Reward = [(Call Strike – Stock Price) × 100] + Initial Credit
Breakeven = Stock Purchase Price – Call Price
```

**Example:** ABC at $37.72; sell 1 near-month 40 Call @ $0.50
- Max Risk = $3,722; Max Reward = $278; Breakeven = $37.22

[PAGE IMAGE: page_287.png]

### Collar Strategy [MUST KNOW]
- **Collar** = Long Stock + Long Put + Short Call
- Short call premium offsets (at least partially) the put cost
- **Limited-risk, limited-reward** — unlike covered call, provides genuine downside protection
- **Best use**: protect stock gains **late in the calendar year** without triggering a taxable sale
- The short call remains covered by the stock

**Collar Example:** 100 shares ABC @ $37.86; Buy 37.50 put @ $1.20; Sell 40 call @ $0.70
- Net Debit = (1.20 – 0.70) × 100 = **$50**
- Max Risk = [($37.86 – 37.50) × 100] + $50 = **$86**
- Max Reward = [($40 – $37.86) × 100] – $50 = **$164**
- Breakeven = $37.86 + $0.50 = **$38.36**

[PAGE IMAGE: page_292.png]

> WARNING: Once you sell the call in a collar, you are obligated to sell the stock if assigned. Don't collar a position unless you're willing and able to part with the shares.

### Calendar Spreads [MUST KNOW]
- Same type option, same strike price, **different expiration months** → created for a **net debit**
- Best when: short-term outlook is **neutral to bearish**, long-term outlook is **bullish** (call calendar) or vice versa (put calendar)
- Maximum risk = initial debit; rewards are estimated (difficult to calculate precisely)
- **Risk profile**: "tent/bow-tie" shape — profits most when stock is near strike at near-month expiration; losses if stock moves sharply in either direction

**Three scenarios at near-month expiration:**
1. Stock moved significantly higher than strike → consider bull call spread with remaining long call
2. Stock moved significantly below strike → evaluate rolling or exiting long call based on outlook
3. Stock near strike → sell another near-term call (create new calendar) or sell a higher call (bull call spread)

[PAGE IMAGE: page_298.png]

### Diagonal Spreads [MUST KNOW]
- Same type option, **different strike prices AND different expiration months**
- Combination of vertical + calendar spread; can be created for debit or credit
- Use in place of calendar when: short-term outlook is slightly more bullish (call diagonal debit) or slightly more bearish (call diagonal credit)
- More upside profit potential than calendar spread because long option has lower strike
- Risk = initial debit; reward and breakeven are variable estimates

**Diagonal Example:** Stock at $46.64; Short 1 near-month $50 Call @ $1.80; Long 1 three-month $47.50 Call @ $3.10
- Net debit (max risk) = **$130**; rewards variable based on stock price at near-month expiration

[PAGE IMAGE: page_300.png]

> WARNING: If the long option (the later-month leg) expires or is exited while the short option remains, you have a naked short — unlimited or high risk. Never let this happen.

## Chapter 13: ETFs, Options, and Other Useful Tricks

### ETF Basics [MUST KNOW]
- **ETF** = mutual fund that trades like a stock; designed to track an index or market segment
- Key power over indexes: **you can own an ETF** → enables combination strategies (protective puts, collars, covered calls)
- ETF volatility is less than its component stocks (declines in one offset by others)
- **Leveraged ETFs** (2X, 3X) use derivatives/swaps — very different risk profile; low option liquidity

**ETF vs. Index Options Differences:**
1. ETF options: underlying you can own → enables **combination strategies**
2. ETF options: **settled with shares** (not cash)
3. ETF options: **always American style** (no exercise-date risk like European index options)

### ETF Option Combination Strategies [MUST KNOW]
- **Protective put**: Long ETF + long put → limited downside, unlimited upside above cost
- **Covered call**: Long ETF + short call → reduced cost basis, capped upside
- **Collared position**: Long ETF + long put + short call → both sides bounded; lowest-cost protection approach

> TIP: Always check ETF liquidity: minimum 1 million daily shares for active trading. Wide bid-ask spreads = low liquidity = avoid.

### Volatility and ETF Options [MUST KNOW]
- **Historical volatility (HV)**: quantifies past price movement; use charts to compare across time periods
- **Implied volatility (IV)**: 12-month expected future volatility implied by current option prices; a "plug figure" in pricing models
- **Vega**: option Greek measuring expected change in option value for every 1% change in IV
- Compare current IV to historical IV levels — don't trade on a single reading in isolation

**Five key IV principles:**
1. Technical analysis is a core component of options trading
2. The underlying asset price sets the tone for option price
3. Let the underlying guide your strategy
4. **Buy options when IV is low** (risk is low)
5. **Sell options when IV is high** (risk is high)

## Chapter 14: Making Money Without Worrying About the Market's Direction

### Straddles and Strangles — Core Concept [MUST KNOW]
- Both combine a **long call + long put** to profit from a big move in **either direction**
- Profit from **increasing volatility**, not predicting direction
- **Straddle**: same strike price, same expiration (more expensive, lower breakevens required)
- **Strangle**: different strike prices, both OTM, same expiration (cheaper, larger move required)

### When to Use Straddles [MUST KNOW]
Three ideal setups:
1. **Sideways consolidation pattern** — big breakout expected
2. **Before earnings announcements** — large stock moves typical
3. **Before economic reports** — biggest swings when news surprises

- Buy straddles when **IV is relatively low** (time decay hits both legs — twice the normal exposure)
- Exit at least **30+ days before expiration** to avoid accelerating theta decay

### Straddle Risk/Reward [MUST KNOW]
- **Max Risk** = (Call Price + Put Price) × Multiplier × # Contracts
- **Downside Breakeven** = Strike Price – (Put + Call prices)
- **Upside Breakeven** = Strike Price + (Put + Call prices)
- Risk graph = **V-shape**: losses capped at combined premium (bottom of V), unlimited profit both directions

**GS Straddle Example (GS at $178):**
- Buy 2 GS Oct 180 Calls + 2 Oct 180 Puts → Net Debit = $20.10
- Max Risk = $4,020; Downside BE = $159.90; Upside BE = $200.10
- Exit plan: sell half of ITM leg when price moves 80% to target; close remaining on earnings day
- **Actual result**: GS hit $200.50 on Fed meeting day → sold 1 call @ $22.80; full exit on earnings day → **+$1,410 profit**

[PAGE IMAGE: page_346.png]

> TIP: If you purchase an even number of contracts, you can take partial profits while leaving remaining position for additional gains.

### Strangle Risk/Reward [MUST KNOW]
- Construction: OTM call + OTM put, same expiration, different strikes
- **Max Risk** = (OTM Call Price + OTM Put Price) × Multiplier × # Contracts
- **Downside Breakeven** = Put Strike – (Put + Call prices)
- **Upside Breakeven** = Call Strike + (Put + Call prices)
- Risk profile: **flat loss zone** between the two strikes (not a V — more like a U with a flat bottom)

**GS Strangle Example (GS at $178):**
- Buy 2 GS Oct 185 Calls @ $7.20 + 2 Oct 170 Puts @ $6.70 → Net Debit = $13.90
- Max Risk = $2,780; Downside BE = $156.10; Upside BE = $198.90
- Same exit strategy as straddle → **Actual result: +$1,710 profit** (strangle produced higher profit because of different strikes)

> WARNING: **Short strangle** = extremely dangerous (unlimited risk on call side)

### Delta Neutral Trading [MUST KNOW]
- **Neutral view** ≠ **Neutral position**: market analyst saying "hold" vs. a strategy with no directional bias
- **Position delta** = sum of all individual leg deltas
- `Position Delta = (Shares × +1) + (Contracts × Option Delta)`
- Near-perfect delta neutral: position delta ≈ 0
- **Gamma**: rate of change of delta per $1 move in underlying — always positive; highest for ATM options; increases as expiration approaches

**MCK Real-World Delta-Neutral Hedge:**
- 100 shares MCK at $180; bought 3 Nov 160 puts (delta ~0.32) when stock at $166 to approximate delta neutral
- Earnings miss: MCK opened at $129 from $159.89 close → puts surged to $29.70–$45.24 (657% gain)
- Sold stock at $129.80, puts at $35.53 → **net +$4,229 (+22.1%)**
- Without hedge: stock alone = **–$5,020 (–27.9%)**
- Key lesson: delta-neutral hedging converted a near-28% loss into a 22% gain

|---|---|---|---|---|

### Straddle Adjustment Methods [MUST KNOW]
- When delta drifts, restore neutrality by:
  - Buying more calls (+delta) or puts (–delta)
  - Selling calls (–delta) or puts (+delta)
  - Buying stock (+delta) or selling stock (–delta)
- Key factors: time to expiration, relative IV levels, trading costs
- Near 30-day mark: take profits rather than adjust
- **Adjusting is not the same as avoiding losses** — if position isn't working, exit it

## Chapter 15: Letting Volatility Show You the Way

### IV Analysis Framework [MUST KNOW]
- **IV determinants**: past HV, time to expiration, expected future movement from scheduled events, demand factors
- **HV vs. IV relationship**: view both on charts; when IV >> HV, options are expensive → favor selling; when IV ≈ HV or below → favor buying
- **IV seasonal tendencies**: recurring spikes from quarterly earnings cycles — Cisco example showed 8 predictable IV spikes over 2 years
- **Always view IV in historical context** — single reading alone is meaningless

### Volatility Skew Types [MUST KNOW]
- **IV smile**: normal pattern — ATM options have lowest IV, increasing moderately as strikes move away from ATM
- **Price skew**: options of same expiration but different strikes have significantly different IVs
  - **Forward price skew**: higher strike options have higher IV (selling opportunity for ratio spreads)
  - **Reverse price skew**: lower strike options have higher IV (selling opportunity for ratio backspreads)
- **Time skew**: options in later months have higher IV than those expiring sooner → opportunity for calendar/diagonal spreads
- Trading edge: sell the relatively high-IV option, buy the relatively low-IV option

> REMEMBER: Price skews provide ratio backspread opportunities; time skews provide calendar spread opportunities.

### Ratio Spreads — Overview [MUST KNOW]
- **Ratio spread**: uneven number of long vs. short options — **more short than long**
- Created for a **net credit** (selling more than buying)
- Unlike vertical spreads: extra uncovered short options create **unlimited risk (calls)** or **limited but high risk (puts)**
- Must define exit plan BEFORE entering — these require active management

### Call Ratio Spread [MUST KNOW]
- Long 1 lower strike call; Short 2 (or more) higher strike calls; same month
- Use when outlook is **neutral to slightly bearish**; capitalize on **forward price skew**
- Best when implemented for an **initial credit**

**Example:** XYZ at $115.70; Buy 1 XYZ 110 Call @ $7.70; Sell 2 XYZ 115 Calls @ $4.20
- Net Credit = (2 × $4.20 – $7.70) × 100 = **$70**
- Max Reward = Vertical spread reward + short call credit = $150 + $420 = **$570** (at $115 expiration)
- Upside Breakeven = **$120.70**
- Below $120.70: profitable; above: unlimited losses

[PAGE IMAGE: page_382.png]

> "The thing that should really catch your attention is that downward-sloping arrow displaying unlimited losses as prices rise. This is clearly an advanced strategy, and one I don't recommend as your first trade — or at all, in fact."

### Put Ratio Spread [MUST KNOW]
- Long 1 higher strike put; Short 2 lower strike puts; same month
- Best when outlook is **neutral to slightly bullish**; capitalize on **reverse price skew**
- Use **1:2 or 2:3 ratio**; implement for credit

**Example:** Long 1 XYZ 110 Put @ $4.70; Short 2 XYZ 105 Puts @ $3.10
- Net Credit = $150; Max Reward = **$650** (at $105 expiration)
- Downside Breakeven ≈ **$98.50**

[PAGE IMAGE: page_384.png]

### Ratio Backspreads — Overview [MUST KNOW]
- **Ratio backspread**: more long options than short; **limited risk with unlimited/high reward**
- Often created for a **credit** (short option reduces long option cost)
- Preferred over ratio spreads: same volatility edge with far better risk profile

### Call Ratio Backspread [MUST KNOW]
- Short fewer lower-strike calls; Long more higher-strike calls; same month
- Outlook: **strongly bullish** + anticipating explosive upward move
- Best with **reverse price skew** (lower-strike options have higher IV to sell)
- Greatest loss when stock closes at the **long call strike price** at expiration

**Formulas:**
```
Max Risk = [(# Short Calls × Difference in Strikes) × 100] – Initial Credit
Upside BE = Higher Strike + [(Diff in Strikes × # Short Calls) ÷ (# Long – # Short)] + Net Option Prices
Downside BE = Lower Strike + (Net Credit ÷ # Short Calls)  [only when credit received]
```

**ABC Example:** Stock at $69.90; Buy 3 ABC 75 Calls @ $0.60; Sell 2 ABC 70 Calls @ $2.45
- Net Credit = **$310**
- Max Risk = [(2 × 5) × 100] – $310 = **$690** (at $75 expiration)
- Upside BE = **$81.90**; Downside BE = **$73.10**
- Actual result: stock barely moved; closed short options @ $0.65 → **+$180 profit**

[PAGE IMAGE: page_394.png]
[PAGE IMAGE: page_396.png]

> "This trade is a perfect example of how trading options requires a change in perception — not being right and still making money because of a well-planned trade that was all about risk management first and the magnitude of profits second."

### Put Ratio Backspread [MUST KNOW]
- Short fewer higher-strike puts; Long more lower-strike puts; same month
- Outlook: **strongly bearish** + anticipating explosive downward move
- Best with **forward price skew** (higher-strike options have higher IV to sell)
- Greatest loss when stock closes at the **long put strike price** at expiration

**Formulas:**
```
Max Risk = [(# Short Puts × Difference in Strikes) × 100] – Initial Credit
Upside BE = Higher Strike – (Net Credit ÷ # Short Puts)  [only when credit received]
Downside BE = Lower Strike – [(Diff in Strikes × # Short Puts) ÷ (# Long – # Short)] + Net Credit
```

**XYZ Example:** Stock at $73.85; Buy 2 XYZ 65 Puts @ $2.75; Sell 1 XYZ 75 Put @ $7.20
- Net Credit = **$170**
- Max Risk = [(1 × 10) × 100] – $170 = **$830** (at $65 expiration)
- Max Reward (stock to zero) = **$5,670**
- Upside BE = **$73.30**; Downside BE = **$56.70**
- Stock at $55.08 at planned exit; long options sold $10.70, short options bought $20.20 → **+$290 total profit**

[PAGE IMAGE: page_399.png]

**Implementation tips for all ratio backspreads:**
- Seek **90 days to expiration** for more time
- Focus on stocks **$25–$75/share** (better credit availability)
- Keep ratios at **1:2 or 2:3** max; calculate net delta to minimize directional bias
- Exit with **30 days remaining** when in danger zone (between strike prices)
- **Never hold short ITM/ATM option into expiration weekend** — always buy it back

## Chapter 16: Trading Profitably When Markets Move Sideways

### Sideways Market Reality [MUST KNOW]
- A sideways (trendless) market is an **optionable trend** requiring special techniques
- Long calls and long puts both fail in sideways markets; need combination positions
- Rule: **sell premium when IV is relatively high; buy it when relatively low**

### Covered Call Sideways Strategy — Dell/XYZ Case Study [MUST KNOW]
- XYZ Technology at $24.00; stop-loss at $22.00; stock moves up then stalls
- After 10% gain to $26.50 with low IV: wait for IV to strengthen before selling calls
- Sold **Apr 27.50 Call @ $0.70** (delta 39.1) — reduces upside directional bias from +100 to +60.9
- April expiration: XYZ closed at $27.34; shares retained; cost basis drops to **$23.30**
- Next: Sold **May 27.50 Call @ $0.55** ahead of earnings; after earnings roll to **Jun 30.00 Call**
- June: XYZ closed at $23.98; sold stock Monday → net gain **$0.78/share** including call premiums
- Buy-and-hold comparison: **–$0.52/share loss**; covered call: **+0.8% gain** vs. **–2.2% loss**

**Covered Call Rules:**
- Do NOT place a standing stop-loss on the stock (would create naked short call)
- If you must exit the stock, buy the call back first
- When stock breaks out of consolidation sideways: sell call or roll up and out before being called away
- Roll short call "out a month and up a strike" if stock rises and assignment seems likely

### Sideways Strategy Shortlist [MUST KNOW]
- Long stock + covered call (limited-but-high risk)
- Call credit spread (slightly OTM) | Put credit spread (slightly OTM)
- Call calendar | Put calendar
- Call ratio spread (unlimited risk) | Put ratio spread (limited-but-high risk)
- **Long butterfly** | **Long condor** — next topics

### Butterfly Spreads [MUST KNOW]
- Profitable in **range-bound markets** — maximum profit when stock closes exactly at short strike at expiration
- Structure: **two vertical spreads** combined (bull spread + bear spread, same short strike)
- **Body** = two short options (ATM or near-ATM); **Wings** = two long options (one lower, one higher strike)
- Created for a **net debit**; risk = initial debit; reward = limited

**Call Butterfly — DIA Aug 103-106-109 Example:**
- Long 1 Aug 103 Call @ $4.00 / Short 2 Aug 106 Calls @ $1.45 / Long 1 Aug 109 Call @ $0.30
- Net Debit = [(4.00 + 0.30) – (2 × 1.45)] × 100 = **$140**
- Max Reward = $45 (bull call spread) + $115 (bear call spread credit) = **$160**
- Upper BE = 109 – 1.40 = **$107.60**; Lower BE = 103 + 1.40 = **$104.40**

[PAGE IMAGE: page_421.png]

**Four Expiration Zones (call butterfly):**
1. Below $103: all worthless → max loss
2. $104.40–$106: close bull call spread for profit; bear call spread expires worthless
3. $106–$107.60: close bull call spread + additional short call for profit
4. Above $109: close both spreads → max loss

**Actual DIA close-out:** DIA at 105.73; bull call spread closed @ $255; net gain = **$115**

### Iron Butterfly [MUST KNOW]
- Combines **bear call spread** + **bull put spread**, both credit spreads, same short strike
- Created for a **net credit** (credit = max reward); still limited risk/reward
- Margin required for both credit spreads
- Structure: long put (lowest) / short put = short call (middle) / long call (highest)

**MO Jul 60-70-80 Iron Butterfly:**
- Long 1 Jul 60 Put @ $0.05 / Short 1 Jul 70 Put @ $1.20 / Short 1 Jul 70 Call @ $1.30 / Long 1 Jul 80 Call @ $0.05
- Net Credit (Max Reward) = [(1.30 – 0.05) + (1.20 – 0.05)] × 100 = **$240**
- Max Risk = (80 – 70) × 100 – $240 = **$760**
- Upper BE = 70 + 2.40 = **$72.40**; Lower BE = 70 – 2.40 = **$67.60**
- Actual close-out: MO at $69.80; short put bought back @ $20 → **+$220 net gain**

### Condor Spreads [MUST KNOW]
- **Condor** = butterfly with **split center strike** using four different strike prices
- Combines two vertical spreads using **four strikes** vs. butterfly's three
- Compared to similar butterfly: **larger breakeven range** (higher probability) but **lower reward** and **larger max loss**

**Iron Condor**: bear call credit spread + bull put credit spread with short legs **at least one strike apart**

**DIA Iron Condor Example (stock at $106.48):**
- Long 1 Aug 103 Put / Short 1 Aug 106 Put / Short 1 Aug 107 Call / Long 1 Aug 110 Call
- Max Reward (credit) = [(0.85 – 0.15) + (0.85 – 0.30)] × 100 = **$125**
- Max Risk = (3 × 100) – $125 = **$175**
- Lower BE = 106 – 1.25 = **$104.75**; Upper BE = 107 + 1.25 = **$108.25**
- DIA closed at 105.73: short put bought @ $0.35 → **+$90** (less than butterfly profits because price fell just short of condor sweet spot)

## Chapter 17: Ten Top Option Strategies

All ten strategies share one common characteristic: **limited risk**.

**Strategy 1 — Married Put [MUST KNOW]**
- Long stock + long put; changes high/limited risk to limited risk; best in low IV, bullish market
- Max Risk: [(Stock Price + Put Price) – Put Strike] × 100; Reward: Unlimited

**Strategy 2 — Collar [MUST KNOW]**
- Long stock + long put + short call; limited risk AND limited reward
- Best when IV is low (buy put), then increases (sell call)
- Max Reward: [(Call Strike – Stock Price) + (Put Credit)] × 100

**Strategy 3 — Long Put Trader [MUST KNOW]**
- Replaces short stock; limited risk (premium paid), bearish directional play
- Max Reward = (Put Strike – Put Price) × 100; exit 30–45 days before expiry

**Strategy 4 — LEAPS Call Investor [MUST KNOW]**
- Long call >9 months; replaces long stock; misses dividends but much lower capital at risk
- Best in low IV; breakeven = Strike – LEAPS Price

**Strategy 5 — Diagonal Spread [MUST KNOW]**
- Short near-month option + long later-month option, different strikes
- "Roll credits" generated each near-month expiration reduce cost basis
- Best for neutral-to-trending outlook with IV time skew

**Strategy 6 — Bear Call Credit Spread [MUST KNOW]**
- Short lower-strike call + long higher-strike call, same month; created for credit
- Best in high IV with <45 days to expiration; replaces naked short call

**Strategy 7 — Straddle [MUST KNOW]**
- Long call + long put, same strike and expiration; profits from big move either direction
- Set up before earnings or economic reports; exit 30+ days before expiry
- Max Risk = net debit; two breakeven points (strike ± combined premium)

**Strategy 8 — Call Ratio Backspread [MUST KNOW]**
- Long higher-strike calls + fewer short lower-strike calls; unlimited upside reward
- Replaces bear call credit spread when you want to convert limited to unlimited reward
- Best with reverse IV price skew and strongly bullish outlook; implement for credit

**Strategy 9 — Put Ratio Backspread [MUST KNOW]**
- Long lower-strike puts + fewer short higher-strike puts; limited but high reward
- Replaces bull put credit spread; best with forward IV price skew and strongly bearish outlook

**Strategy 10 — Long Put Butterfly [MUST KNOW]**
- Bear put spread + bull put spread, same month; replaces short straddle with defined risk
- Time decay helps (unlike long puts alone); maximum profit when stock closes at short strike

## Chapter 18: Ten Do's and Don'ts in Options Trading

### Do: Focus on Managing Risk [MUST KNOW]
- Tell people you're a **risk manager**, not a trader
- Risk management rewires how your brain sees markets
- Diversify both **sectors** AND **strategies** — five trending trades in the same sector using the same strategy violates diversification even with proper position sizing
- Before every trade: "What if I'm wrong?"

### Don't: Avoid Losses [MUST KNOW]
> *Keep your losses small and let your winners roll.*
- **Avoiding losses makes them bigger** — one catastrophic loss you refused to take wipes out months of gains
- Taking small losses is a skill developed by experienced traders
- Reframe success: a rule-following losing trade is a success; an undisciplined winning trade is a failure
- Author's example: 30% biotech loss taught him to spot bad management and assess clinical trial risk — "That loss turned into an immeasurable advantage"

### Do: Trade with Discipline [MUST KNOW]
- Discipline means following rules **every single trade** — not most of the time
- **Early luck is dangerous** — creates false sense of "being right" that will eventually destroy the account
- Each trade requires: reasonable position size, defined max risk, defined entry/exit signals, executing when your plan requires it

### Don't: Expect to Remove Emotions [MUST KNOW]
- Goal is **managing emotions**, not eliminating them
- Things that elevate trading emotions: discretionary approach, trading during market hours, holding "revenge" positions in same stock, personal problems
- Countermeasures: systematic approaches, after-hours review, step away from specific stocks, **avoid trading when life isn't in order**
- Warning sign: waking up cranky or unable to sleep → emotions are managing you

### Do: Have a Plan [MUST KNOW]
- Write it in draft form; revisit at 3 months, then 6 months, then regular schedule
- Include: risk management rules, general IV guidelines, steps for each rule, separate sections for investments vs. trades
- **Expect the plan to change** — that's not failure, it's calibration

### Do: Be Patient [SHOULD KNOW]
- An imperfect plan beats no plan
- **Sometimes the best trading action is nothing** — waiting for the right setup or waiting to take profits
> TIP: Read *Reminiscences of a Stock Operator* by Edwin Lefevre — the seminal trading book on the mind of Jesse Livermore, one of the few who sold before the 1929 crash.

### Don't: Suffer from Analysis Paralysis [MUST KNOW]
- Endless data, backtesting, and optimization is a trap — at some point you must **go live**
- Paper trading won't fully prepare you; live trading with limited-risk strategies and proper position sizes is how real learning happens
- Confidence from following tested rules is what keeps you disciplined when the picture gets hazy

### Do: Take Responsibility [MUST KNOW]
- *It's all on you.* — Vic Sperandeo example: lost money because he answered the phone during a trade; his conclusion was it was his fault for not having a "no phone" rule
- Never blame brokers, news, others — shift responsibility = giving your success to someone else
- If execution issues persist: switch brokers; if time constraints: switch to strategies you can execute properly

### Don't: Stop Learning [SHOULD KNOW]
- No single strategy works every time; markets constantly change
- Start by mastering **one or two strategies**; add more as experience grows
- Continued education plan: self-study (books, periodicals), formal courses, annual specific goals

### Do: Love the Game [MUST KNOW]
- Successful traders love reading about trading, learning strategies, hearing other traders' stories
- **Making money as the singular driver will eventually produce large losses** — passion for the craft is what sustains performance
- Trading requires intense, focused work — not long hours, but concentrated ones
- Wall Street wisdom: trading is a **lifelong pursuit** that deserves healthy excitement
