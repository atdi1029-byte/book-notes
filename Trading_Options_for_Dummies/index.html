Glossary

Contract Basics

Call Option: A contract giving the owner the right to buy 100 shares of the underlying stock at a specified price by a specified date. The seller has the obligation to deliver those shares if assigned.

Put Option: A contract giving the owner the right to sell 100 shares at a specified price by a specified date. The seller is obligated to buy the shares if assigned.

Strike Price: The fixed price at which the option owner can buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying stock. Also called the exercise price.

Premium: The price paid for an option contract. Quoted per share, so multiply by 100 for total cost. A $2.80 option costs $280.

Expiration Date: The last date on which the option can be exercised. Options lose all value if out-of-the-money at expiration.

Open Interest: The total number of outstanding contracts for a particular strike and expiration. Higher open interest means better liquidity and tighter bid-ask spreads.

Multiplier: The number of shares per contract — almost always 100 for standard equity options.

Moneyness

In-the-Money (ITM): An option with intrinsic value. For calls, when the stock price is above the strike. For puts, when the stock price is below the strike.

At-the-Money (ATM): When the stock price equals or is very close to the strike price. ATM options have no intrinsic value but maximum time value.

Out-of-the-Money (OTM): An option with zero intrinsic value. For calls, when the stock price is below the strike. OTM options expire worthless 100% of the time unless the stock moves before expiration.

Option Pricing

Intrinsic Value: The amount an option is in-the-money. Call intrinsic value = stock price minus strike price. Can never be less than zero.

Extrinsic Value (Time Value): Option price minus intrinsic value. Represents the added premium for time remaining and expected volatility. Decays to zero at expiration.

Black-Scholes Model: The Nobel Prize-winning formula for calculating theoretical option values. Inputs: stock price, strike, time to expiration, interest rates, and volatility.

Implied Volatility (IV): The market’s forecast of future price movement, derived by plugging the actual option price back into the pricing model. Higher IV means more expensive options.

Historical Volatility (HV): The actual past price movement of the underlying, calculated as the annualized standard deviation of daily returns. Also called statistical volatility.

IV Crush: A sharp drop in implied volatility after an anticipated event (earnings, FDA ruling) resolves. Can cause option values to plummet even if the stock moves in the right direction.

The Greeks

Delta: Expected change in option value per $1 move in the underlying. Calls range from 0 to +1.00; puts from 0 to −1.00. An ATM option has delta near ±0.50.

Gamma: The rate at which delta changes per $1 move in the underlying. Highest for ATM options near expiration. Tells you how quickly your directional exposure shifts.

Theta: Daily time decay — how much value the option loses each day from the passage of time alone. Accelerates sharply in the last 30 days before expiration.

Vega: Expected change in option value for each 1% change in implied volatility. Higher for longer-dated options.

Rho: Sensitivity to interest rate changes. The least impactful Greek for most short-term traders.

Order Types

Buy to Open (BTO): Creates a new long position. Sell to Close (STC): Exits an existing long position. Sell to Open (STO): Creates a new short position. Buy to Close (BTC): Exits an existing short position.

OCO (One Cancels Other): Paired orders where execution of one automatically cancels the other. Essential for placing simultaneous stop-loss and profit-target orders on the same position.

Rolling: Closing a current option position and simultaneously opening a new one. Roll out (same strike, later expiration), roll up (higher strike), or roll down (lower strike).

Settlement & Exercise

American-Style: Can be exercised any time before expiration. All stock and ETF options are American-style.

European-Style: Can only be exercised on the designated exercise date. Most index options are European-style and settle in cash, not shares.

Cash Settlement: ITM index options pay the difference between index value and strike in cash rather than delivering securities.

Assignment: The process by which a short option seller is required to fulfill the obligation of the contract. Happens when the option buyer exercises.

LEAPS: Long-term Equity AntiCiPation Securities. Options with 9 months to 3 years until expiration, almost always expiring in January.

Strategies

Vertical Spread: Long and short options of the same type and expiration but different strikes. Limited risk and limited reward in all cases.

Calendar Spread: Same type and strike, different expirations. Profits from time decay differential between near and far-month options.

Straddle: Long call and long put at the same strike and expiration. Profits from a large move in either direction.

Strangle: Long OTM call and long OTM put, same expiration. Cheaper than a straddle but requires a larger move to profit.

Butterfly: Three-strike strategy with the body (two short options) at the middle strike and wings (long options) at the outer strikes. Maximum profit when the stock closes exactly at the body strike at expiration.

Iron Condor: Bear call credit spread plus bull put credit spread with at least one strike separating the short legs. Wider breakeven range than a butterfly but lower maximum reward.

Collar: Long stock plus long put plus short call. Limits both downside risk and upside reward. The short call premium offsets part or all of the put cost.

Ratio Backspread: More long options than short, created for a credit. Limited risk with unlimited (calls) or high (puts) reward potential. Preferred over ratio spreads because of the superior risk profile.

Market Analysis

Advance-Decline Line: A cumulative running total of advancing minus declining issues. Every significant decline since 1987 has been preceded by a negative divergence in the NYSE A-D line.

Put-to-Call Ratio: Put volume divided by call volume. A contrarian indicator: extremely high readings signal excessive fear and a potential bottom.

VIX: The CBOE Volatility Index, derived from S&P 500 option implied volatilities. Rising VIX signals fear; extreme highs often mark market bottoms.

Bollinger Bands: A 20-period SMA with bands at ±2 standard deviations. When bandwidth reaches a 6-month low, a volatility expansion (breakout) is imminent.

Rate of Change (ROC): Today’s price divided by the price N days ago, times 100. Used for sector ranking and momentum confirmation.

If You Only Remember 10 Things

  1. Risk management is the job — tell people you are a risk manager, not a trader, and the mental shift will change every decision you make.
  2. Your maximum loss is decided before you enter — define the worst-case dollar amount, write it down, and never revise it to extend losses.
  3. Buy options when IV is low, sell when IV is high — this single rule keeps you on the right side of the volatility premium more often than not.
  4. Time value accelerates its decay in the last 30 days — exit long options at least 30 days before expiration or the clock will eat you alive.
  5. Options give you the risk profile of stock ownership for a fraction of the capital — but they add a deadline that stocks don’t have.
  6. Covered calls are not free money — they cap your upside, and if you sell one on a stock you must hold, you may be forced to deliver shares you cannot afford to lose.
  7. A straddle profits from movement, not direction — buy one before earnings or economic reports when IV is low, and let the market decide which leg pays.
  8. Vertical spreads are the workhorse strategy — limited risk, limited reward, four flavors to match any outlook, and the building blocks for butterflies and condors.
  9. Never exit just one leg of a spread — closing the long leg alone creates a naked short position with unlimited or very high risk.
  10. Paper trade until the mechanics are automatic, then go live with one contract — the emotional education only happens with real money, but the technical education is free.

Chapter 1: Options Trading and the Individual Investor

Duarte opens with a declaration that options are not the exclusive province of sophisticated Wall Street professionals. Any investor, regardless of experience level, can use options on individual stocks, indexes, and ETFs to both manage risk and grow assets simultaneously. The crucial distinction he draws early is between investing and trading. Investing relies on time and compounding over long horizons — buy-and-hold, rental property, retirement accounts. Trading is a shorter-term, targeted, closed-end proposition where positions may last minutes, hours, days, or weeks. Options work for both, but their built-in expiration dates make them natural trading instruments. The exception is LEAPS, long-term options that bridge the gap between trading and investing with expirations stretching out to three years.

Before touching a single contract, Duarte insists on a six-step pre-trading checklist. First, check your financial balance sheet — living expenses, debt, insurance, and net worth. Second, set realistic goals that match your experience. Third, know your genuine willingness to take risks, not what you think you can handle when markets are calm, but what you can stomach when a position moves against you. Fourth, become a competent analyst in both technical and fundamental methods. Fifth, paper trade before deploying real capital. Sixth, and he underlines this one: never trade with money you are not willing to lose. Not car payments, not rent, not money that would change your life if it disappeared.

“Options trading is a cautious and very precise exercise.” — Joe Duarte

The core mechanics get a clean introduction here. Calls gain value when the underlying rises. Puts gain value when it falls. Both can be used to limit portfolio risk, protect individual positions, or generate income through spreads and writes. Duarte identifies three primary ways options reduce risk. Hedging lets an option gain value opposite to the underlying, keeping total position value high during a decline and costing less than short selling. Leverage means an option costing $500 can capture profit potential comparable to a $5,000 stock investment. And income generation through spreads and covered writes creates cash flow in sideways or mildly trending markets.

Two risks unique to options get flagged early and often. Time value decay means options lose value as expiration approaches, a headwind that stocks simply do not have. And leverage cuts both ways — the same multiplier that amplifies gains also amplifies percentage losses, even though dollar losses are capped at the premium paid. This duality is why Duarte keeps returning to the concept of planning your exit before you enter. A trading plan with easy-to-follow rules that covers different scenarios is not optional — it is the mechanism that manages the emotions that otherwise destroy accounts.

The chapter closes with an important distinction between American-style and European-style options. Most stock options are American-style, exercisable any time before expiration, with matching last trading and exercise dates. Most index options are European-style, where the last trading date and exercise date can differ — a detail that catches new traders off guard. Duarte also introduces directional bias as a concept: long positions need the stock to go up, short positions need it to go down, but certain option combinations can profit in either direction or in a sideways market entirely. This flexibility is what makes options qualitatively different from stocks, not just a leveraged version of the same bet.

The risk management framework Duarte establishes has four components. First, understand what conditions to analyze, both market-wide and for individual securities. Second, use proper trade mechanics when creating a position — this means knowing the order types, the margin requirements, and the assignment rules before you click “buy.” Third, recognize and follow the trading rules and requirements that govern options markets, including settlement procedures, expiration cycles, and the distinction between opening and closing transactions. Fourth, understand what variables make any position gain or lose value — the Greeks, time decay, implied volatility, and the underlying price all interact simultaneously.

ETFs get an early mention because they have become the most important vehicle for options trading outside of individual stocks. SPY (S&P 500) and QQQ (Nasdaq-100) are the most popular optionable ETFs, trading like stocks during market hours unlike mutual funds which settle at close. ETFs on commodity indexes let you participate in commodity markets without trading futures, and their options chains are often among the most liquid available. The combination of ETF flexibility and options leverage creates possibilities that Duarte spends much of the book’s second half exploring in detail.

Controlling emotions gets its own treatment because Duarte sees it as the single biggest threat to option traders. The solution is not willpower but structure: a written anticipatory trading plan with rules that cover different scenarios. Key elements include access to proper equipment and technology, realistic knowledge of your time commitment (you cannot day-trade if you cannot devote two to three hours at a stretch), access to a real-time quote and charting service, a reliable broker for flawless execution, and an educational component that keeps you working on both technical and fundamental skills daily. The plan is the firewall between you and the impulsive decisions that empty accounts.

Chapter 2: Introducing Options

The second chapter formalizes the contract mechanics. A financial option is a contractual agreement between two parties. Duarte focuses on listed options — standardized contracts traded on regulated exchanges, not the over-the-counter variety. The defining characteristic is asymmetric: the buyer acquires specific rights, while the seller assumes specific obligations. A call owner has the right to buy 100 shares at the strike price by the expiration date. A put owner has the right to sell 100 shares under the same terms. In both cases, the seller must deliver if the buyer exercises — and the Options Clearing Corporation (OCC) guarantees that the seller will meet the obligation, eliminating counterparty risk entirely.

Duarte builds the valuation framework piece by piece. To value any option, you need four inputs: the type and strike price, the current stock price, the past trading pattern (volatility) of the underlying, and the time remaining until expiration. Not all stocks have options; those that do feature multiple strike prices and expiration dates arranged in the option chain. Open interest — the number of existing contracts at each strike — tells you how liquid that particular contract is. The option symbol format encodes everything: underlying ticker, date (YYMMDD), call or put flag, and strike price multiplied by 1,000. So AAPL170505C00148000 is an Apple May 5, 2017 call at the $148 strike.

The three expiration cycles are a relic of the original exchange structure but still matter for understanding option availability. Cycle I includes January, April, July, and October. Cycle II covers February, May, August, and November. Cycle III fills in March, June, September, and December. Every option features at least the current month and the following month, and LEAPS typically expire only in January and June. Strike price increments vary from $0.50 to $100 depending on the underlying’s price and how actively it trades. A critical warning appears here that gets repeated throughout the book: options lose time value at an accelerating rate as expiration nears, and you should never hold an option you plan to sell with 30 days or less remaining.

The order mechanics section introduces the four foundational transactions. Buy to Open creates a new long position. Sell to Close exits that long position. Sell to Open creates a new short position. Buy to Close exits the short. Open interest updates with a one-day delay, showing the prior day’s activity. Assignment risk gets its first serious treatment here: a covered call means you own the shares when selling the call, so assignment simply means delivering your stock. A naked call means you do not own the shares, creating unlimited risk because the stock can rise indefinitely. A short put assignment obligates you to buy stock at the strike price, potentially well above the current market value.

The expiration decision deserves careful attention because the consequences of inaction are real. At expiration, in-the-money options are automatically exercised by the OCC through a process called exercise by exception. Your choices are to exercise deliberately, close the position before auto-exercise kicks in, or let it expire worthless. Duarte is emphatic that closing an ITM option before expiration is almost always better than allowing auto-exercise, because exercise converts a small, defined-risk option position into a much larger stock position that may exceed your margin capacity or risk tolerance.

Two settlement types create different timing dynamics. A.m. settlement means the last trading day is the day before expiration, while p.m. settlement means the last trading day is the same day as expiration. Knowing which applies to your specific option prevents the unpleasant surprise of discovering you can no longer trade a position you thought was still active. The overall message of the chapter is that options are derivatives with their own set of rules that do not map neatly onto stock trading experience. The sooner you internalize the mechanics of contract creation, assignment, exercise, and settlement, the fewer expensive surprises you will encounter.

The comparison between options and other securities draws useful distinctions. Stocks represent partial ownership in a company. Bonds are debt instruments with defined terms. Options convey no ownership of tangible assets and have a limited life. ETFs track indexes or sectors and trade like stocks, but some use swaps for leverage, and Duarte warns that swaps misfired catastrophically in 2008 — always check the prospectus. Indexes are pools of assets that are not directly tradeable; you buy ETFs or mutual funds that track them. Understanding where options fit in this hierarchy prevents the common beginner mistake of treating them as a substitute for stock ownership rather than as a fundamentally different instrument.

The chapter’s two-risk warning gets repeated because it defines the boundary conditions for every strategy. Limited life means that if the anticipated move does not happen before expiration, you lose your entire initial investment — 100% of the premium. And choosing the wrong strategy for the conditions can cause losses that overwhelm your winners. A trader who consistently buys options in high-IV environments, or who sells naked calls without understanding the unlimited risk, will eventually face a loss large enough to undo months of careful work. The cure is matching strategy to conditions, which is the subject of the book’s second half.

Chapter 3: Trading Places — Where the Action Happens

The institutional plumbing of options markets gets a thorough walkthrough. Six U.S. exchanges handle options: CBOE, Nasdaq NOM, ISE, NYSE ARCA, PHLX, and BATS, all linked electronically so most orders auto-route to the best available price. Three types of participants drive the ecosystem. Brokers issue option approvals in levels reflecting the risk of each strategy and require margin agreements for complex positions. Market makers provide liquidity by taking the other side of trades at quoted prices, keeping markets orderly even in thin contracts. And the OCC guarantees that every seller meets their obligation, making counterparty default a non-issue for listed options.

Pricing rules are straightforward but essential. Options trade in increments of $0.01, $0.05, or $0.10. The premium is always the quoted price multiplied by 100, so a $2.80 quote means you pay $280 plus commissions. Three risks dominate the landscape. Expiration risk means ITM options auto-exercise at expiration, converting a small option position into a large stock position — manage this by exiting at least 30 days before expiration. Leverage means a small stock move creates a large percentage move in the option, amplifying both gains and losses. Time value is a headwind for buyers and a tailwind for sellers, creating an asymmetry that shapes every strategy choice.

The Greeks get their formal introduction in this chapter. Delta measures the expected change in option value per $1 change in the underlying — calls range from 0 to +1.00, puts from 0 to −1.00, and an ATM option sits at approximately ±0.50 regardless of past volatility. Gamma measures how fast delta shifts, telling you how quickly your directional exposure changes. Theta quantifies the daily decline from time decay. Vega captures the impact of implied volatility changes. And Rho measures interest rate sensitivity, the smallest factor and the one that gets the least coverage. Duarte includes a concrete example: ABC at $20 with ATM calls and puts, showing how delta, gamma, and option values shift as the stock moves to $21.

The volatility discussion splits into two distinct concepts. Historical volatility (HV) measures past price movement as an annualized standard deviation of daily price changes. Higher HV means bigger daily moves and less predictable behavior. Implied volatility (IV) is the forward-looking measure embedded in the current option price — it includes HV but also captures supply-and-demand dynamics and psychological factors. A major one-day move has a larger psychological impact on IV than its mathematical impact on 100-day HV, which is why IV can spike dramatically on a single event. The Black-Scholes model, which earned Fisher Black and Myron Scholes a Nobel Prize, provides the framework for comparing theoretical value (using HV) to actual market price (using IV). You can use the calculator in two directions: enter HV to get theoretical value, or enter the market price to solve for IV.

Duarte emphasizes concentrating on actively traded contracts — higher volume and open interest mean easier entry and exit, tighter spreads, and less slippage. Slippage, the money lost due to the bid-ask spread, compounds across dozens of trades. A contract with open interest of 50 and a $0.40 spread costs significantly more per round trip than one with 5,000 open interest and a $0.05 spread. This is not theoretical: it directly reduces realized returns and is entirely within your control to minimize by choosing liquid options.

Three key costs are unique to options and must be factored into every trade. Liquidity cost shows up as wider spreads and worse fills on low-volume contracts. Time cost means every day you hold, the option’s time value shrinks via theta decay regardless of what the stock does. And volatility cost means more volatile underlyings have more expensive options, with vega measuring the sensitivity. These are not line items on your brokerage statement but they are real expenses that erode net returns. The traders who consistently profit minimize all three through disciplined contract selection and timing.

The Options Industry Council (OIC) provides free education, and the CBOE offers option calculators that compute all five Greeks from basic inputs. Duarte uses these calculators throughout the book for strategy evaluation. The combination of OCC guarantee (eliminating counterparty risk), exchange- based trading (providing transparency), and standardized terms (enabling comparison) makes listed options a remarkably well-structured market for anyone willing to learn the rules. The chapter lays the institutional foundation so that when strategies arrive in later chapters, you are not distracted by mechanical questions.

Rho — the interest rate Greek — gets the least coverage because it has the smallest practical impact for most retail traders. But in environments of rapidly changing rates, Rho can meaningfully affect longer-dated option values, particularly LEAPS. The chapter builds a complete picture of the five forces acting on option prices: underlying price (delta), speed of delta change (gamma), time passage (theta), volatility expectations (vega), and interest rates (rho). Understanding how they interact simultaneously is the prerequisite for everything in the strategy chapters.

Chapter 4: Option Values, Risk, and Reward

This chapter builds the analytical foundation for every strategy that follows. Intrinsic value is what you would get if you exercised the option right now: stock price minus strike for calls, strike minus stock price for puts, and it can never be less than zero. Extrinsic value (time value) is everything else — the portion of the premium that reflects time remaining and expected movement. As expiration approaches, extrinsic value erodes to zero, leaving only intrinsic value for ITM options and nothing for OTM ones.

The leverage comparison between stock and option positions is one of the clearest in the book. Stock ABC trades at $43. You buy the $40 call for $4. If the stock rises to $47, your stock position (on 50% margin) gains 18.6% while your call gains 75%. But if the stock drops to $30, the stock on margin loses 60% with potential margin calls and further losses, while the call loses 100% of a much smaller amount — $400 versus $1,300. The real power of options is not the amplified upside but the limited loss nature: your worst case is defined and known before you enter.

The breakeven formulas are simple but foundational. For calls: strike price plus premium paid. For puts: strike price minus premium paid. Call profits are theoretically unlimited above breakeven, mirroring a long stock position. Put profits are limited but substantial — the stock can only fall to zero. Risk graphs visualize these relationships, with stock price on the x-axis and profit/loss on the y-axis. The long call graph shows a flat loss line at the negative premium (your worst case regardless of how far the stock falls), then rises above zero once the stock exceeds strike plus premium. The married put — long stock plus long put — creates a risk profile that looks remarkably similar to a long call, providing insurance against major downside while preserving unlimited upside above the new breakeven.

Duarte introduces three basic combination positions that use stock plus options: the married long put (protection), the covered short call (income), and the collar (capped risk and reward on both sides). These three building blocks, combined with the ability to overlay individual risk graphs to see combined profiles, open the door to every strategy in the book’s second half. The key insight is that you are not choosing between risk and reward — you are choosing which risk/reward profile matches your outlook, your capital, and your tolerance for uncertainty.

The leverage comparison makes the case with numbers that stick. Stock ABC at $43 on 50% margin requires $2,150. The $40 call costs $400. If the stock rises to $47, the stock position gains 18.6% while the call gains 75%. But if the stock drops to $30, the stock on margin loses 60% with potential margin calls and further losses, while the call loses 100% of its much smaller outlay — $400 versus $1,300. The real power of options is not the amplified upside but the limited loss nature: your worst case is defined and known before entry, and no margin call can force you deeper into a losing position.

Worst-case scenario thinking is not pessimism — it is the foundation of Duarte’s entire approach. Before analyzing potential gains, examine the downside first. Being unprepared for worst-case scenarios costs money. The option-only combinations that the book develops later are all driven by market conditions: high IV versus low IV, sideways movement versus directional trends. You build these combinations by overlaying individual risk graphs to see the combined risk and reward profile, and the visual language of risk graphs becomes the primary analytical tool for the rest of the book.

The profiling of risk and reward deserves special emphasis because it introduces a concept most stock investors have never considered: you can choose your risk/reward shape. A long call has a different profile than a married put, which has a different profile than a collar, even though all three are bullish positions. The distinction is not academic — it determines how much capital you commit, how much you can lose in the worst case, how quickly your position responds to favorable moves, and whether time decay helps or hurts you. Strategy selection is profile selection, and the chapters that follow are essentially a catalog of available profiles.

The moneyness framework — in-the-money, at-the-money, out-of-the-money — is not just vocabulary but a sorting mechanism for strategy selection. ITM options have the highest delta and respond most closely to the underlying’s movement, but they cost the most because of their intrinsic value. ATM options sit at the sweet spot of delta near 0.50 and maximum time value, making them the default choice for directional trades. OTM options are the cheapest but have the lowest probability of finishing in the money — they expire worthless 100% of the time unless the stock moves before expiration. Every strategy in the book specifies which moneyness to use and why.

Chapter 5: Analyzing Mood Swings in the Market

Before picking a strategy, you need to read the market’s mood. Duarte starts with macro factors: falling interest rates are generally bullish for stocks, rising rates eventually negative. Global central banks — the Fed, PBOC, ECB — all affect markets now, though the Fed remains the most influential. But the real diagnostic tool is market breadth, which Duarte calls the heartbeat of the market. Breadth tracks the internal components of advances and declines: advancing versus declining issues, new highs and lows, up and down volume, and issues above or below key moving averages.

The Advance-Decline Line is the foundational breadth indicator. Calculate it by subtracting declining issues from advancing issues and keeping a cumulative daily running total. Visual inspection matters more than specific values — you are watching whether the line trends up, down, or sideways relative to the index. The historical record is emphatic: every significant decline since 1987 has been preceded by a negative divergence in the NYSE A-D Line. The index can still rise while decliners outnumber advancers if large-cap components dominate, which is why the divergence is the signal, not the absolute level.

Three indicators refine the raw A-D signal. A 20-day SMA smooths short-term noise — the A-D line above its SMA points up, below means direction is uncertain. RSI marks extremes: low readings at market bottoms, high readings at tops or reversals. And Rate of Change (ROC) warns of A-D trend changes before they are obvious, especially effective when RSI and ROC confirm each other. The A-D Ratio (advances divided by declines) oscillates around 1.0; extreme lows provide timely bottom signals while extreme highs tend to confirm reversals after the fact rather than predict them.

Sentiment analysis adds a psychological layer. Put-to-call ratios, originally created by Martin Zweig (who predicted and profited from the 1987 crash), are contrarian indicators. Extremely low ratios signal complacency and are bearish. Extremely high ratios signal excessive fear and are bullish. The CBOE Equity P:C ratio above 0.90–1.0 suggests oversold conditions. The CBOE Index-Only P:C ratio above 2.4 (approximately three standard deviations above the mean) signals near-term tops. The ISE Sentiment Index (ISEE) inverts the formula (call/put instead of put/call) and tracks only customer buys, excluding market makers; readings below 85 signal extreme bearishness and a contrarian buy signal.

The VIX — blended implied volatility from specific S&P 500 Index options — gets its own treatment. A climbing VIX means bearish conditions; a declining VIX means neutral to bullish. But the contrarian twist applies: when fear gets extreme, VIX spikes to levels that are unsustainable, exhaustion sets in, and the reversal begins. The trading edge is to watch for VIX reversals to confirm market bottoms rather than treating high VIX as a reason to stay out. Related indexes include the VXN for the Nasdaq and the VXO, the original VIX calculation method. Duarte counsels using two to three complementary sentiment tools rather than five or six of the same type, calculating mean plus or minus standard deviations to identify extremes.

The Put Volume Indicator (PVI), created by John Bollinger, divides daily put volume by its 10-day SMA. Extreme readings reveal excessive fear or complacency. But Duarte warns that high PVI readings often precede an acceleration of selling before a subsequent reversal — they are not immediately contrarian. The signal is that fear is building, not that it has peaked. Only when PVI reaches historically extreme levels and begins to reverse does the contrarian buy signal emerge.

The practical takeaway for options traders is that most breadth and sentiment tools follow the same pattern: weakness equals bearish, but extremely bearish readings flip to bullish on a contrarian basis. Duarte counsels using two to three complementary sentiment tools rather than five or six of the same type, which he calls indicator burnout. Calculate mean plus or minus standard deviations to identify extreme readings, using Excel’s STDEV function. Qualitative judgment matters too: do not include all historical data in your calculations because market structure changes over time, and levels that were extreme in 2005 may be normal in 2015.

The SPY Put/Call Ratio with a 5-day SMA smooths the noisy daily data and produces remarkably consistent signals. Chapter 6: Sector Analysis — Technical and Fundamental

Duarte walks through three chart types that every options trader must understand. Line charts plot only closing prices, giving a clean big-picture view but hiding gap and intraday information. OHLC bar charts display the open (left tab), high and low (the vertical bar), and close (right tab) — a long bar signals a volatile day, a close near the high shows strength, near the low shows weakness. Candlestick charts are the most widely used by professionals, with a thickened body between open and close and patterns that describe the bull/bear battle. The best practice is to use both: longer-term line charts for identifying the major trend, and OHLC or candlestick charts for reading intraperiod action and volatility.

Support and resistance levels are the backbone of technical analysis for options traders. Support is an area where buyers return to a falling trend — two touches establish the level, a third confirms it. Resistance works the same way on the upside. The longer a price serves as support or resistance, the stronger it becomes. A critical rule that experienced traders internalize: previous support often becomes resistance after a breakdown, and vice versa after a breakout. These levels serve as entry and exit points, price targets, stop-loss levels, and the foundation for risk-reward calculations. Trend analysis builds on top: uptrends connect higher lows, downtrends connect lower highs, and trendline breaks serve as entry and exit signals.

Moving averages get standard treatment: the SMA equally weights all closes, while the EMA gives greater weight to recent data and is preferred for trading decisions. Three standard settings form the hierarchy: 20-day for short-term trends, 50-day for intermediate, and 200-day for long-term — a break of the 200-day is always significant. On a weekly chart, the 200-day becomes the 40-week MA, a widely watched level for institutional investors. The relative ratio line (primary security divided by secondary security) becomes a powerful tool for sector rotation: a rising ratio means the primary is outperforming, and money flows from underperforming to outperforming sectors as conditions change.

Chapter 7: Practicing Before You Swing

Recognizing proper option pricing takes longer than learning basic strategies, and Duarte’s solution is daily tracking. Build a spreadsheet that records the underlying price, ITM/ATM/OTM call and put prices for multiple expirations, and the intrinsic value, time value, delta, gamma, and theta for each option. Watch these numbers change daily. After a few weeks, you start to develop an intuitive feel for how much an option “should” move given a particular stock move — a skill that no amount of formula memorization can replace.

The IV section distills practical rules of thumb. High IV correctly predicts large future moves most of the time. Low IV correctly predicts small moves most of the time. The sweet spot for entries is low IV combined with a technical setup — you get cheap options and a catalyst. Extended high IV marks excellent exit points. The relationship between IV and the stock is what Duarte calls circular: high IV tells you to expect a big move, the big move changes HV, and changed HV feeds back into new IV calculations. An IV of 30% on a $100 stock means the market expects the stock to trade between $70 and $130 over the next year, based on one standard deviation capturing 68.2% of outcomes.

IV crush deserves special attention. When IV has been elevated ahead of an event — earnings, an FDA ruling, a product launch — and the uncertainty resolves, IV drops sharply. This can cause option values to plummet even if the stock moves in the direction you predicted. Selling high-IV options hoping for a crush sounds attractive, but the underlying price itself may move dramatically after the event, overpowering the benefit of declining IV. The timing of IV crush is not a free lunch.

Paper trading gets a balanced assessment. The advantages are real: it provides P&L feedback, incorporates all trade costs, and identifies issues you had not considered. But it cannot replicate the emotional preparation for real losses, assignment risk, margin requirements, or the skill of live trade execution. Duarte views paper trading as laying mental grooves — not as real training, but as necessary preparation. The key takeaway is that paper trading should use real current quotes and record every trade as if money were on the line, including commissions and slippage.

Trading systems receive a strong endorsement. A system uses specific, rigidly defined entry and exit rules with no discretion. When a buy signal fires, you enter. When an exit signal fires, you close. Adding discretion destroys the two best things about a system: minimizing emotions and enabling backtesting. The nine-step backtesting process walks from strategy identification through filter addition through risk management integration, always testing across bullish, bearish, and sideways periods. Chapter 8: Designing a Killer Trading Plan

Duarte frames trading as a business. Losses are operating expenses — you minimize them but do not try to eliminate them. Strategy mastery means having the right conditions in place and managing the position with discipline, and it can take years to develop. The best traders look for the right setup rather than trading as often as possible, a concept he describes as hours of boredom combined with minutes of sheer panic or pleasure. The chapter includes a strategy-to-conditions matching table that becomes a practical reference: ATM long calls for bullish low-volatility markets, covered calls for bullish high-volatility, butterflies and condors for range-bound high-volatility, calendar spreads for range-bound low-volatility.

Longevity — the patience and staying power to show up every day — depends on limited-loss strategies and proper position sizing. Two methods work: a maximum dollar amount per trade or a maximum percentage of the account per trade, with the percentage method preferred because it scales naturally with account size. Because options are leveraged, you should not allocate the same dollar amount you would to a stock position. Start with single-contract sizes for every new strategy and scale up only as skills develop. The plan itself must be written down — verbal plans do not work — and it should cover risk management rules, general IV guidelines, steps for each rule, and separate sections for investments versus trades.

Order types get a practical treatment focused on options-specific challenges. Limit orders are best for entering positions because they guarantee your price. Market orders are the only type that guarantees execution, making them the right choice for exits when you must get out. Stop orders on options have a particular problem: because option volume is much lower than stock volume, the quote frequently triggers the stop rather than an actual trade, sometimes at a much worse price than intended. OCO (One Cancels Other) orders allow you to place a stop-loss and a profit-limit simultaneously — without OCO, having two live orders on the same position is dangerous because both can execute on a strong swing.

Rolling options is a skill every trader needs. Rolling out means closing the current position and opening a new one at the same strike but a later expiration, buying more time. Rolling up changes to a higher strike, useful to avoid assignment or capture high IV at the higher level. Rolling down goes to a lower strike. These can be combined: roll out and up, or roll out and down. The chapter closes with combination order mechanics — entering spreads as a single order (better pricing) versus legging in individually. The tip for spread execution: reduce the debit slightly or increase the credit slightly versus the market quote, because exchange traders like hedged positions and will often fill slightly favorable spread orders.

Managing costs gets a detailed treatment because options trading has cost layers that stock trading does not. The largest early cost is the learning curve — wrong entries, bad sizing, poor execution — which declines with experience but never disappears entirely. Trading costs include both commissions and slippage (the bid-ask spread cost), and Duarte recommends calculating the combined percentage at different contract sizes (1, 5, 10 contracts) and different price points ($1, $5, $10 options). Margin costs apply to short option positions, with covered versus naked being the primary consideration. Tax implications add another layer: options in retirement accounts defer taxes, but establishing options on existing positions may trigger taxable events.

The trading plan must be written down — verbal plans do not survive contact with a volatile market. Duarte recommends setting realistic goals (a small account should start with covering a monthly restaurant outing, not replacing a salary), keeping excellent records of every trade and its result, and expecting the plan to evolve over time. The plan should include separate sections for investments versus trades, because the time horizons, risk tolerances, and analytical frameworks differ. If the plan is profitable, consider increasing scope. If it is not, diagnose what is failing rather than abandoning the systematic approach entirely.

Chapter 9: Getting to Know Different Option Styles

This chapter unpacks the differences between stock options, ETF options, and index options — differences that can catch even experienced equity traders off guard. An index is a pool of financial instruments grouped into a single value. You cannot own an index directly, which means index options are cash-settled: when an ITM index option expires, you receive the difference between the index settlement value and your strike price, multiplied by 100, as a cash credit. The SPX is the most widely used by professionals, with SPY as its companion ETF that settles via actual share delivery. The DJIA tracks 30 blue chips (tradeable via DIA), the Nasdaq-100 is heavy tech via QQQ, and the Russell 2000 covers small-caps with bigger moves in both directions.

Index construction method matters for trading. The DJIA is price-weighted, meaning a $300 stock like Boeing moves the index more than a $40 stock like Pfizer regardless of market cap. The S&P 500 is market-cap-weighted, so Alphabet at roughly $585 billion in market cap dwarfs smaller components. Equal dollar- weighted indexes give every stock the same impact but require quarterly rebalancing. The settlement mechanics are where things get tricky. SPX options with third-Friday expiration use the first reported sales price for each component (AM settlement), while other expiration dates use the closing index value (PM settlement). The settlement ticker for SPX is SET, for RUT it is RLS, for NDX it is NDS.

The American versus European style distinction has real practical consequences. American-style options (all stock and ETF options, plus OEX and SOX among indexes) can be exercised at any time. European-style options (most index options including SPX) can only be exercised on the designated exercise date, and the last trading day is usually one day prior to settlement — many index options stop trading on a Thursday with expiration on Friday. Duarte’s warning is blunt: index option specs vary widely, and you should never assume the same specifications as SPX. Always check the product specification sheet before trading any index option for the first time.

The exercise-versus-sell decision gets a clear rule: if more than $0.20 of time value remains, selling the option and separately acting on the stock is more profitable than exercising. Exercise forfeits the remaining time value, which is money left on the table. This can create unexpected positions — an ITM put that auto-exercises forces you to buy stock, and an ITM call auto-exercises into a stock purchase that might exceed your margin capacity. The ex-dividend date introduces assignment risk for short calls: if the dividend exceeds the remaining time value, early exercise becomes rational for the call owner, and you get assigned before you expected to.

Duarte’s warning about margin is blunt: it is dangerous. Cut losses and sell an assigned position rather than holding on margin in most cases. The margin interest compounds, the position can deteriorate further, and what started as a small option trade becomes a large stock headache.

The expiration management rules create a systematic framework for the final days of every position. Long OTM options should be sold if any value remains above commission, not allowed to expire worthless. Short OTM options require monitoring after the close because unexpected exercise is possible when the stock hovers near the strike. Short ITM options should be assumed to result in assignment — exit on the last trading day if you want to avoid it. ATM options in either direction require the most active management because small moves can flip them from worthless to assigned or vice versa.

Chapter 10: Protecting Your Portfolio with Options

Portfolio protection is where options earn their keep. The protective put (or married put, when stock and put are purchased simultaneously) locks in a minimum sell price without requiring exercise. As the stock falls, gains in the put offset losses in the stock. One long put protects 100 shares. The formula for exercise net profit is straightforward: put strike minus put premium equals put breakeven, then subtract the stock purchase price and multiply by 100. For XYZ bought at $34 with an Oct 37.50 put at $1.05, the breakeven is $36.45, yielding a net profit of $245 per contract even if the stock drops to zero below that level.

Chapter 11: Increasing Profit Potential and Decreasing Risk

The core insight is simple: options reduce risk by reducing capital at stake. A long call caps your loss at the premium paid, while a long stock position can lose its full value. The tradeoff is a higher breakeven, slower profit accrual, and a time constraint. Three factors determine whether a directional option trade will succeed: direction (trade with the trend), magnitude (estimate price projections using technical tools and focus on higher-probability delta moves), and time (identify time horizons first, then check option chains, anchoring to earnings releases and economic reports). A stock can sit flat for months as dead money — annoying for stock investors but deadly for option holders, because theta punishes you for being right on direction but wrong on timing.

Chapter 12: Combination Strategies — Spreads and Other Wild Things

Covered calls are the most popular option strategy among individual investors. Sell a call against stock you own, receive the credit, and accept a cap on your upside at the short call strike. The best scenario is selling calls with 30 to 45 days to expiration when IV is relatively high, in a market where the stock is moving sideways — earning income while waiting for a directional move. The critical rules: the short call strike should be above your stock purchase price, never sell a covered call on a stock you absolutely must hold (the obligation to sell is real), and never sell the stock without first buying back the call, or you will be left holding a naked short call.

The collar adds genuine downside protection by pairing a long put with the covered call. Long stock plus long put plus short call — limited risk, limited reward, with the call premium offsetting part or all of the put cost. The best use case is protecting stock gains late in the calendar year without triggering a taxable sale. The collar example (100 shares at $37.86, buy 37.50 put at $1.20, sell 40 call at $0.70) shows a net debit of $50, max risk of $86, max reward of $164, and breakeven at $38.36. Once you sell the call, you are obligated to sell the stock if assigned, so collar only positions where you are genuinely willing to part with the shares.

Calendar spreads use the same type and strike but different expiration months, always created for a net debit. The idea is that the near- month short option decays faster than the longer- dated long option, creating a profit if the stock stays near the strike. The risk profile creates a distinctive tent or bow-tie shape — maximum profit when the stock closes at the strike at near- month expiration, losses if it moves sharply in either direction. Three scenarios at near-month expiration determine your next move: if the stock moved significantly higher, consider a bull call spread with the remaining long call; if significantly lower, evaluate rolling or exiting; if near the strike, sell another near-term call to create a new calendar.

Diagonal spreads combine different strikes and different expirations — a vertical plus calendar hybrid. They offer more upside profit potential than a straight calendar because the long option has a lower strike. The example: stock at $46.64, short the near-month $50 call at $1.80, long the three-month $47.50 call at $3.10, for a net debit of $130. The critical danger with both calendars and diagonals: if the long option (the later-month leg) expires or is exited while the short option remains, you have a naked short position with unlimited risk. Never let this happen.

The covered call formulas quantify what is at stake. Max risk equals the stock price times shares minus the call premium received. Max reward equals the difference between call strike and stock price times 100 plus the initial credit. Breakeven equals the stock purchase price minus the call price. For ABC at $37.72 with a near-month 40 call sold at $0.50: max risk $3,722, max reward $278, breakeven $37.22. The reward looks small, but compounded monthly in a sideways market, covered call income can add 6% to 12% annualized to a stock portfolio’s return.

Chapter 13: ETFs, Options, and Other Useful Tricks

ETFs are where the practical power of options comes alive. Unlike indexes, you can actually own an ETF, which means you can build combination strategies: protective puts, covered calls, and collars. ETF volatility is inherently lower than its component stocks because declines in one holding get offset by others. Three key differences from index options: ETF options are settled with shares (not cash), they are always American-style (no exercise-date surprises), and the underlying is something you actually own and can take delivery of.

Duarte lays out five IV principles that serve as a permanent decision framework. Technical analysis is core to options trading. The underlying asset price sets the tone for option price. Let the underlying guide your strategy. Buy options when IV is low. Sell options when IV is high. The 150 strike was chosen for its tightest bid-ask spread (highest open interest at 88,278) and sufficient delta coverage. The historical outcome: the market topped in late October/early November 2007, and the December puts provided excellent protection through the initial leg of what became a devastating bear market.

Chapter 14: Making Money Without Worrying About Direction

Straddles and strangles are the weapons for when you know something big is coming but cannot predict the direction. Both combine a long call and a long put to profit from increasing volatility rather than a directional bet. A straddle uses the same strike price (more expensive, tighter breakevens). A strangle uses two OTM strikes (cheaper, but requires a larger move). Three ideal setups: a sideways consolidation pattern where a breakout is expected, before earnings announcements, and before economic reports where surprises drive large swings. The critical requirements: buy when IV is relatively low (because time decay hits both legs, doubling your theta exposure) and exit at least 30 days before expiration.

The Goldman Sachs straddle example is one of the book’s best case studies. GS at $178, buy two Oct 180 calls plus two Oct 180 puts for a net debit of $20.10 ($4,020 max risk). Downside breakeven at $159.90, upside breakeven at $200.10. The exit plan: sell half of the ITM leg when price moves 80% to target, close the rest on earnings day. GS hit $200.50 on a Fed meeting day, one call was sold at $22.80, and the full exit on earnings day produced a $1,410 profit. The strangle on the same stock (two Oct 185 calls at $7.20, two Oct 170 puts at $6.70) cost less ($2,780 max risk) but required a larger move. It actually produced a higher profit of $1,710 because of the different strike mechanics.

Delta-neutral trading is the framework for removing directional bias entirely. Position delta equals the sum of all individual leg deltas — when it is near zero, you have no net directional exposure. The McKesson (MCK) example is dramatic: 100 shares at $180, hedged with three Nov 160 puts (delta approximately −0.32 each) when the stock was at $166. An earnings miss sent MCK from $159.89 to $129.80 at the open. Without the hedge: a 27.9% loss of $5,020. With the delta-neutral puts: a 22.1% gain of $4,229. The hedge did not just reduce the loss — it converted it into a substantial profit.

Adjusting a straddle position as delta drifts requires buying or selling calls, puts, or stock to restore neutrality. The GS position delta table shows how it evolved from +5.0 at entry (nearly neutral) to +159.6 by day 24 as the stock rallied. The practical rule: near the 30-day mark, take profits rather than adjust. And adjusting is not the same as avoiding losses — if the position is not working, exit it rather than throwing more capital at a broken thesis.

Chapter 15: Letting Volatility Show You the Way

Volatility is not just a Greek — it is a tradeable signal. When IV significantly exceeds HV, options are expensive and you should favor selling strategies. When IV is at or below HV, options are cheap and buying strategies have an edge. Duarte shows the Cisco example where IV spiked predictably eight times over two years, driven by the quarterly earnings cycle. These seasonal tendencies create repeating opportunities for traders who pay attention, but a single IV reading in isolation is meaningless — always view it in historical context.

Volatility skews reveal pricing anomalies that create specific strategy opportunities. The normal pattern is an IV smile, where ATM options have the lowest IV and it increases moderately as strikes move away from ATM. A forward price skew (higher strikes have higher IV) creates selling opportunities for ratio spreads. A reverse price skew (lower strikes have higher IV) creates opportunities for ratio backspreads. A time skew (later months have higher IV) favors calendar and diagonal spreads. The trading edge in all cases: sell the relatively high-IV option and buy the relatively low-IV option.

Ratio spreads have uneven numbers of long versus short options — more short than long — and are created for a net credit. The extra uncovered short options create unlimited risk on calls or limited-but-high risk on puts. The call ratio spread example: stock at $115.70, buy one $110 call at $7.70, sell two $115 calls at $4.20 each, for a $70 net credit. Max reward of $570 at the $115 expiration, but above $120.70 losses become unlimited. Duarte is notably candid about this strategy, calling the unlimited loss arrow on the risk graph something that “should really catch your attention” and saying he does not recommend it as a first trade or at all, in fact.

Chapter 16: Trading Profitably When Markets Move Sideways

A sideways market is an optionable trend, not the absence of opportunity. Long calls and long puts both fail in trendless conditions, but combination positions thrive. The governing rule: sell premium when IV is relatively high, buy it when relatively low. The Dell/XYZ covered call case study walks through a complete trade lifecycle. Stock purchased at $24.00 with a stop at $22.00. It rallied 10% to $26.50 with low IV, so Duarte waited for IV to strengthen before selling the Apr 27.50 call at $0.70. At April expiration, XYZ closed at $27.34 — shares retained, cost basis dropped to $23.30. He then sold the May 27.50 call at $0.55, rolled after earnings to the June 30.00 call, and ultimately sold the stock at $23.98. Net result: a $0.78/share gain including call premiums, versus a $0.52/share loss for buy-and-hold.

Butterfly spreads are the precision instrument for range-bound markets. Two vertical spreads combined with the same short strike create a body (two short ATM options) and wings (two long options at outer strikes), entered for a net debit. Maximum profit occurs when the stock closes exactly at the short strike at expiration. The DIA example (103-106-109 call butterfly) shows $140 debit, $160 max reward, breakeven range between $104.40 and $107.60. An important subtlety: narrowing the strike distance lowers the cost, increases the reward-to- risk ratio, but shrinks the breakeven range. The 1-point spread (105-106-107) costs only $30 with a 2.33 reward-to-risk ratio but a 1.3% breakeven window. The 3-point spread costs $140 with a 1.14 ratio but a 3.0% window. You are trading probability against magnitude.

The covered call rules for sideways markets deserve emphasis because violations are common and costly. Chapter 17: Ten Top Option Strategies

The unifying theme across all ten strategies is limited risk. Duarte builds a hierarchy from simple to complex, each strategy paired with the market conditions where it works best. The married put (long stock plus long put) converts high-risk stock ownership into a defined-risk position; best in low IV bullish markets. The collar adds a short call to further reduce cost; ideally entered when IV is low (buy the put cheap) and then the call is sold after IV increases. The long put replaces short stock entirely, eliminating unlimited upside risk; exit 30 to 45 days before expiry. The LEAPS call replaces long stock for a fraction of the capital but misses dividends; best entry when IV is low.

The intermediate strategies target specific conditions. Diagonal spreads generate rolling credits each near-month expiration, reducing cost basis over time in a neutral-to-trending environment with an IV time skew. The bear call credit spread replaces the dangerous naked short call with defined risk, best in high IV with less than 45 days to expiration. The straddle profits from big moves in either direction, set up before catalysts, exited 30 or more days before expiry.

The advanced strategies use ratio structures. The call ratio backspread converts the limited reward of a bear call credit spread into unlimited upside reward by adding extra long calls; best with reverse IV price skew. The put ratio backspread does the same on the downside; best with forward IV price skew. And the long put butterfly replaces the dangerous short straddle with defined risk, profiting from time decay in range-bound conditions with maximum profit when the stock closes at the body strike. All ten share the property that your worst-case loss is known before you enter.

Chapter 18: Ten Do’s and Don’ts in Options Trading

The final chapter distills the entire book into behavioral rules. Focus on managing risk , not on making money. Diversify both sectors and strategies — five trending trades in the same sector using the same strategy violates diversification even with proper position sizing. Before every trade, ask: what if I am wrong? Do not avoid losses — avoiding them makes them bigger. One catastrophic loss you refused to take wipes out months of gains. Reframe success: a rule-following losing trade is a success; an undisciplined winning trade is a failure. The lesson from Duarte’s own 30% biotech loss became an immeasurable advantage, teaching him to spot bad management and assess clinical trial risk.

Trade with discipline every single trade, not most of the time. Early luck is dangerous because it creates a false sense of being right that will eventually destroy the account. Manage emotions rather than trying to eliminate them — things that elevate trading emotions include discretionary approaches, trading during market hours, holding revenge positions, and personal problems. If you are waking up cranky or unable to sleep, emotions are managing you, not the other way around.

Have a written plan that includes risk management rules, IV guidelines, and separate sections for investments versus trades. Expect the plan to change — that is calibration, not failure. Revisit at three months, six months, then on a regular schedule. Be patient: sometimes the best trading action is nothing. Do not suffer from analysis paralysis — endless optimization is a trap, and at some point you must go live with limited-risk strategies and proper position sizes.

Passion for the craft — reading about trading, learning strategies, hearing other traders’ stories — is what sustains performance through the inevitable drawdowns. Trading requires intense, focused work, not long hours but concentrated ones.

Early luck is dangerous because it creates a false sense of being right that will eventually destroy the account. A new trader who profits on their first five trades develops confidence that is indistinguishable from overcon- fidence. Each trade requires reasonable position size, defined max risk, defined entry and exit signals, and execution when the plan requires it — not when emotions suggest it. The discipline to follow rules on every single trade, not most of the time, is what separates the traders who survive from the ones who blow up after their first lucky streak ends.

Analysis paralysis is the final trap. Endless data, backtesting, and optimization is a form of procrastination disguised as preparation. At some point you must go live. Paper trading will not fully prepare you because it cannot replicate the emotional response to real losses. Live trading with limited- risk strategies and proper position sizes is how real learning happens. The confidence that comes from following tested rules is what keeps you disciplined when the picture gets hazy — and in trading, the picture is always hazy.

Trading & Investing Lessons

Time Decay as Position Sizing

Theta is not just a pricing factor — it is a cost of carrying the trade. Options traders must think of time decay as a daily expense, just like a short seller pays borrow costs. The cost-per-day comparison (October put at $1.75/day versus January put at $1.43/day) shows that cheaper does not mean less expensive when measured against the protection period. Every trade has a carrying cost, and ignoring it means your position sizing is wrong.

IV as a Regime Indicator

Low IV combined with a technical setup is the options equivalent of buying a value stock with a catalyst. High IV with deteriorating breadth is like buying at the top of a hype cycle. The VIX and put-to-call ratios serve the same function as valuation multiples: they tell you whether the market is pricing in too much complacency or too much fear, and both extremes are actionable.

Defined Risk as Edge

Vertical spreads, butterflies, and backspreads all share the property that your worst case is known before entry. This is the options equivalent of using stop-losses religiously, except the stop is built into the structure of the position itself. You cannot be stopped out by a gap — the long option guarantees your maximum loss regardless of what the market does overnight.

Sector Rotation as Relative Value

Duarte’s ROC-based sector ranking system is pure relative momentum: overweight what is working, underweight what is not, and use the 50/200-day crossover to switch between aggressive and defensive postures. This is the same principle behind pair trading and relative value strategies in institutional finance.

The Collar as a Synthetic Bond

A collar (long stock, long put, short call) creates a bounded range with defined upside and downside. The risk profile resembles a fixed-income instrument: limited return with limited loss. For investors nearing a liquidity event or wanting to lock in gains without triggering a taxable sale, the collar is the closest thing to converting equity into a bond-like payoff.

Backtesting Degradation is Expected

Forward tests always show worse results than backtests because conditions change and inefficiencies close. Traders who abandon a system after the first disappointing forward test are making the same mistake as investors who sell a stock after one bad quarter. The system is not broken — it is reverting toward realistic expectations.

Key Takeaways

Risk Management Is the Product

Profits are the byproduct of managing risk well. Define your worst-case scenario before entering any position. A concrete exit for losses is fixed in stone; a potential exit for profits can be adjusted. The asymmetry is intentional: protect the downside rigidly, manage the upside flexibly.

Buy Low IV, Sell High IV

This single heuristic guides strategy selection more than any other factor. Low IV favors buying strategies (long calls, long puts, straddles, LEAPS). High IV favors selling strategies (covered calls, credit spreads, ratio spreads). Check IV relative to historical levels before every trade.

Time Decay Accelerates Exponentially

Options do not lose value at a constant rate. The last 30 days see dramatic acceleration, which is why Duarte repeats the rule: exit long options 30 days before expiration, and sell short options in the 30-to-45-day window to capture the steepest decay curve.

Vertical Spreads Are the Foundation

Every complex strategy — butterfly, condor, ratio spread, backspread — is built from vertical spread components. Master bull call, bear put, bear call, and bull put spreads before attempting anything more complex.

Position Sizing Must Account for Leverage

An option position provides leveraged exposure. Allocating the same dollar amount as a stock position dramatically amplifies your effective exposure. Start with single-contract sizes for new strategies and scale up only as you prove competence.

Paper Trading Builds Skills, Not Confidence

Paper trading develops mechanical skills and forces you to confront situations before risking money. But it cannot replicate the emotional response to real losses. The bridge is going live with limited-risk strategies and minimum contract sizes.

Breadth Divergences Precede Every Major Decline

Every significant market decline since 1987 has been preceded by a negative divergence in the NYSE A-D Line. Breadth is the market’s internal health check — when the index rises while more stocks are declining, the advance is fragile.

Contrarian Ideas

Options Are Safer Than Stocks

The standard perception is that options are risky derivatives used by speculators. Duarte argues the opposite: a properly structured option position (married put, vertical spread, collar) has objectively less risk than owning stock outright, because the maximum loss is defined and the capital at risk is lower.

Losses Are Operating Expenses

Most traders frame losses as failures. Duarte frames them as the cost of doing business. A losing trade executed according to the rules is a success because it kept the loss small. An undisciplined winning trade is a failure because it reinforced bad habits that will eventually produce a catastrophic loss.

Perfect Signals Come Too Late

Waiting for all indicators to align results in no trading activity or, worse, entering at the end of a move. Mixed signals are the normal state. The edge comes from acting when indicators mostly agree, not from waiting for unanimous confirmation.

Paying More for Longer Options Is Actually Cheaper

A January put at $2.15 seems expensive next to an October put at $1.05. But the January put costs $1.43 per day of protection versus $1.75 per day for the October. The more expensive option delivers cheaper daily insurance, the opposite of what intuition suggests.

You Can Profit Without Being Right

The call ratio backspread example shows a trade that made money when the stock barely moved — the exact opposite of the predicted explosive rally. Well-structured positions with credit entries can profit from wrong directional calls.

Proposals & Frameworks

Strategy-to-Conditions Matrix

Duarte provides a table matching market conditions to optimal strategies. Bullish low volatility: long calls, married puts, call debit spreads. Bullish high volatility: covered calls, credit spreads. Bearish low volatility: long puts, put calendar spreads. Bearish high volatility: call credit spreads. Range-bound high volatility: butterflies, condors. Range-bound low volatility: calendar spreads, diagonal spreads. The biggest strategy determinant is your financial goal: income, capital gains, or hedging.

Three-Part Profit Management Hierarchy

First, cut losses. Second, prevent profits from turning into losses. Third, let profits run. The order is non-negotiable. When you own multiple contracts, take profits on a portion at a 20% gain and let the remainder ride.

Nine-Step Backtesting Process

Identify strategy, define entry/exit rules, select market and period, set account assumptions, test, identify filters, re-test with filters, add risk management, final test and evaluation. Must cover bull, bear, and sideways periods. Stability is the key metric: average profit should exceed average loss, no handful of trades should drive all returns, and drawdowns must be manageable.

Four-Step Sector Rotation System

Weekly rank all ten Select Sector SPDRs by 3-month return. Invest in the top-ranked ETF. Maintain until it drops below rank 3 for two consecutive weeks. Switch to the new top-ranked ETF. Use 50-day versus 200-day SMA to determine whether to run the system (bullish) or retreat to core plus puts (bearish).

Probability Alignment Table

Before entering a trade, score every indicator (price, ATR, ROC, SV, volume, weekly trend, EMAs) as bullish, bearish, or neutral. Act when most agree. Mixed signals require market context judgment. Perfect alignment either does not happen or happens too late.

Ratio Backspread Implementation Rules

Seek 90 days to expiration. Focus on stocks in the $25 to $75 range for better credit availability. Keep ratios at 1:2 or 2:3 maximum. Calculate net delta to minimize directional bias. Exit with 30 days remaining when in the danger zone between strikes. Never hold a short ITM or ATM option into expiration weekend.

What Didn’t Fit

The McKesson Earnings Disaster — A Delta-Neutral Save

McKesson (MCK) was held at $180 per share. When the stock dropped to $166, Duarte bought three November 160 puts with delta approximately −0.32 each to create an approximately delta-neutral hedge. Then earnings missed badly and MCK opened at $129.80, down from $159.89 — a gap of over 18% overnight. The puts surged to between $29.70 and $45.24, a gain of 657%. After selling the stock at $129.80 and the puts at $35.53, the position netted $4,229, a 22.1% gain. Without the hedge, the portfolio would have absorbed a $5,020 loss, or 27.9%. The spread between the hedged and unhedged outcomes — roughly 50 percentage points — is the most compelling argument in the book for understanding and applying the Greeks to real positions.

The Goldman Sachs Straddle — Playing the Fed

Goldman Sachs at $178 with a major Fed meeting ahead and earnings shortly after. Two straddles (Oct 180 calls and puts) for $4,020 total risk. The stock hit $200.50 on the Fed meeting day, and Duarte sold one call at $22.80 to lock in partial profits. He held the rest through earnings and closed the full position for a $1,410 profit. The strangle on the same stock with different strikes actually produced a higher profit ($1,710) because the OTM structure amplified the per-contract gain. The lesson is not which structure is better in the abstract but that the same fundamental thesis can be expressed through different vehicles with different risk-reward tradeoffs, and the optimal choice depends on how much premium you are willing to spend versus how large a move you need.

The XLI Covered Call — Probability Scoring

The XLI trade used a ten-indicator probability table: price action, ATR, ROC, statistical volatility, volume, weekly trend, 20-day EMA, 50-day EMA, and 200-day EMA each scored as bullish, bearish, or neutral. Six of ten indicators tilted bullish, two bearish, two neutral. Duarte entered a covered call rather than a directional trade because the conflicting signals suggested range-bound conditions. The framework is not just about the XLI trade — it is a template for any position, forcing you to articulate your thesis in specific terms before committing capital.

The SPY December 2007 Puts — Insurance That Paid

With 150 shares of SPY at $151.97, Duarte selected four December 150 puts at $4.30 each, spending $1,720 for protection through the fall season. He chose the 150 strike specifically for its tightest bid-ask spread, backed by open interest of 88,278 contracts — far more liquid than the 152 or 154 strikes. The combined delta of −156 slightly exceeded the +150 stock delta, giving a mild downside bias. The market topped in late October 2007, beginning what would become the worst financial crisis since the Depression. Those December puts, purchased as routine insurance, turned out to be one of the best-timed hedges in the book.

The Butterfly Width Tradeoff

Narrowing the butterfly from 3-point strikes (103-106-109) to 1-point strikes (105-106-107) dramatically changes the risk profile. The narrow butterfly costs $30 with a 2.33 reward-to-risk ratio but only a 1.3% breakeven range. The wide butterfly costs $140 with a 1.14 ratio but a 3.0% range. This is the clearest illustration in the book of the probability-versus-magnitude tradeoff. You can have high probability or high payoff, but not both, and the choice depends entirely on your conviction about how tightly range-bound the stock will remain.
