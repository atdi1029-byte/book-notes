## Chapter 1: Options Trading and the Individual Investor

### The "Rewiring" Concept [MUST KNOW]
- Options can be added to investment strategies for **individual stocks, indexes, and ETFs** regardless of experience level
- Learning options requires **rewiring your brain** to see markets in a new, more profitable way

### Investing vs. Trading [MUST KNOW]
- **Investing**: using time and compounding to build wealth over long periods; buy-and-hold; does NOT always work for options due to **time limit in the life of an option**
- **Trading**: shorter-term, targeted, closed-end proposition; options used for short-term trading OR for protecting longer-term holdings

> "Options trading is a cautious and very precise exercise." — Joe Duarte

### Pre-Trading Checklist [MUST KNOW]
1. **Check your financial balance sheet** — ensure it's healthy before taking extraordinary risks
2. **Set realistic goals** — don't trade beyond your experience level; don't risk too much in one trade
3. **Know your willingness to take risks** — find strategies that match your comfort level
4. **Become a good analyst** — technical AND fundamental; understand the dominant trend and the underlying
5. **Paper trade first** — practice before real money; builds discipline and avoids costly mistakes

> **Never trade with money you aren't willing to lose.** Options are risk-management vehicles, but you can still lose money trading them.

### Understanding Options [MUST KNOW]
- **Calls** — profit from rising (bullish) markets
- **Puts** — profit from falling (bearish) markets
- Uses: limit portfolio risk, protect existing positions, generate income through spreads and writes
- The underlying security's **volatility** is a key component of option prices — must analyze carefully
- Four things to understand as a risk manager:
  1. Conditions (market and individual security) to consider when analyzing a trade
  2. Proper trade mechanics when creating a position
  3. Rules and requirements for the security
  4. Individual variables that make a position gain and lose value

### Two Primary Risks of Options [MUST KNOW]
1. **Time value decay** — options have a time limit; if the anticipated move happens too close to expiration, the option can expire worthless
2. **Leverage** — causes option prices to change in larger percentage moves than the underlying stock

### Call and Put Rights [MUST KNOW]
- **Call owner rights**: buy specific quantity of underlying stock (exercise) by expiration at the strike price
- **Put owner rights**: sell specific quantity of underlying stock by expiration at the strike price
- A call rises in value when the underlying goes up (rights are fixed, stock is increasing)
- A put gains value when the underlying moves **down** in price

### Selecting Expiration Dates [MUST KNOW]
- **Options with more time until expiration are more expensive**
- **Options with more attractive strike prices are more expensive**
- Plan your exit **before you trade** — cuts losses and avoids heartache
- Exit long options **at least 30 days before expiration** — time value decays rapidly within 30 days

### Leverage Example [MUST KNOW]
- A **$500 investment in an option strategy** may give as much profit potential as a **$5,000 investment in an individual stock**
- Spending less capital but potentially getting similar rate of return

### ETFs and Sector Strategies [SHOULD KNOW]
- **ETFs** trade like stocks intraday with full liquidity; every option strategy applies to entire sectors
- **Sideways markets**: option combination strategies let you profit whether a stock moves up OR down
- Chapters 14, 15 cover non-directional trades; Chapter 16 covers income strategies for flat markets

## Chapter 2: Introducing Options

### Option Contract Basics [MUST KNOW]
- A **financial option** = contractual agreement between two parties; this book covers **listed** (exchange-traded, standardized) options only
- **Call option**: owner has the *right* (not obligation) to buy a specific number of shares at strike price by expiration; seller is *obligated* to sell if buyer exercises
- **Put option**: owner has the *right* (not obligation) to sell a specific number of shares at strike price by expiration; seller is *obligated* to buy if buyer exercises

**Summary of rights and obligations:**
- **Call owner**: right to buy at predetermined price
- **Put owner**: right to sell at predetermined price
- **Call seller**: obligated to sell shares at predetermined price if assigned
- **Put seller**: obligated to buy shares at predetermined price if assigned

### Three Key Objectives for Using Stock Options [MUST KNOW]
1. Benefit from upside price moves for less capital than buying shares outright
2. Profit from downside price moves without the unlimited risk of short-selling
3. Protect an individual stock position or entire portfolio during falling markets

### Two Critical Risks [MUST KNOW]
1. **Limited life / expiration date** — if the anticipated move doesn't occur before expiration, you lose the entire premium paid
2. **Wrong strategy can compound losses** — risky strategies can expose you to losses that overwhelm winners

### Options vs. Other Securities [SHOULD KNOW]
- Options are **derivatives** — they derive their value from an underlying security
- **Indexes**: NOT securities — you can't buy an index directly; instead buy ETFs tracking the index (e.g., SPY for S&P 500)
- **ETFs**: mutual funds that trade like stocks; most track an index or market sector; **leveraged ETFs** use swaps to mimic index with leverage (2x, 3x)
- Two most popular ETFs: **SPY** (S&P 500) and **QQQ** (Nasdaq 100)

### Valuing Options [MUST KNOW]
To value an option you must know:
1. Type and strike price (put or call, what price it locks in)
2. Price of the underlying security
3. Characteristics of the underlying's past trading pattern (volatility drives pricing)
4. Time remaining until expiration (more time = more value; decay accelerates near expiration)

### Key Options Terminology [MUST KNOW]
- **Underlying security**: the stock that determines the option's value
- **Strike price**: the price you'd pay (call) or receive (put) per share if exercising
- **Expiration date**: the date your rights disappear
- **Option deliverable**: number of shares and name of underlying
- **Multiplier**: number used to determine total option value; most stock options = 100
- **Premium**: total value of the option = market quote × multiplier (usually × 100)
- **Open interest**: number of existing contracts for a given option; higher = more liquid

### Option Chain [MUST KNOW]
- Not all stocks have listed options — only those with enough trading interest
- **Option chain** = full list of call and put option strike prices for a stock in each expiration month
- Chain shows: open interest (1-day delay), market quotes (bid/ask), last traded price

### The Expiration Cycle [MUST KNOW]
Three expiration cycles exist; all options have at least 4 expiration dates available:
| Cycle | Months |
|-------|--------|
| Cycle I | January, April, July, October |
| Cycle II | February, May, August, November |
| Cycle III | March, June, September, December |

- **LEAPs** (Long-term Equity Anticipation Securities): long-term options, typically expire in January and June
- All options always have at least current month + following month available

### Options Expiration Is Decision Time [MUST KNOW]
- **Never hold an option you plan to sell when there are 30 days or less to expiration**
- Know your **last trading day** — write it down
- Know whether expiration is **a.m. or p.m. settlement**
  - **A.m. settlement**: last trading day is the day BEFORE expiration
  - **P.m. settlement**: last trading day is ON expiration day
- Almost always better to **close out an in-the-money option** prior to automatic exercise

### Opening and Closing Positions [MUST KNOW]
```
Buy to Open → new long position
Sell to Close → exit a long position
Sell to Open → new short position (naked)
Buy to Close → exit a short position
```

### Selling Options You Don't Own [MUST KNOW]
- **Covered call**: you own the shares when selling the call — limited risk
- **Naked call**: you do NOT own shares — same unlimited risk as a short stock position
- **Covered put** (short the stock + short put): if assigned, put automatically covers the short
- **Naked put** (no underlying position): if assigned, creates a new long stock position at the put strike price

### Keeping Tips in Mind [MUST KNOW]
1. Get broker approval first (SEC requires it)
2. Be disciplined — enter for a specific reason; exit when conditions change
3. Keep track of the expiration date
4. Practice first / paper trade before committing real money

## Chapter 3: Trading Places — Where the Action Happens

### The Greeks — Introduction [MUST KNOW]
Greeks are individual variables that combine to provide the value of expected changes in the option given changes in the underlying stock. Named because most are derived from Greek letters. Available from option calculators at most exchange websites.

**The five Greeks:**
- **Delta**: expected change in option value for each $1 change in underlying stock price
- **Gamma**: expected change in delta for each $1 change in underlying stock price
- **Theta**: the option's expected daily decline due to time
- **Vega**: expected change in option value due to changes in volatility expectations
- **Rho**: estimates changes in option value due to changes in the risk-free interest rate (less influential)

### Delta [MUST KNOW]
- Most important Greek to understand initially; connects underlying stock value changes directly to option value changes
- **Delta values range:**
  - Calls: 0 to +1.00 (or 0 to +100)
  - Puts: 0 to –1.00 (or 0 to –100)
- **ATM option delta ≈ +/–0.50** regardless of past volatility (50% chance stock moves up or down)

### Gamma [MUST KNOW]
- Provides expected change in delta for each $1 change in underlying
- Understanding gamma reduces the chance delta values will get away from you

**Delta/Gamma Example (ABC trading at $20, moves to $21; 20 strike call and put):**
| Type | Moneyness | Value | Delta | Gamma |
|------|-----------|-------|-------|-------|
| Stock at $20: Call | ATM | $1.10 | +0.50 | 0.1962 |
| Stock at $20: Put | ATM | $1.00 | –0.50 | 0.1931 |
| Stock at $21: Call | ITM | $1.60 | +0.70 | 0.1438 |
| Stock at $21: Put | OTM | $0.50 | –0.30 | 0.1467 |

Key: when stock goes from $20→$21, call delta increases +0.50→+0.70 (change of ~0.20 ≈ gamma of 0.196)

### Historical vs. Implied Volatility [MUST KNOW]
- **Historical volatility (HV)** = measure of past movement; annualized standard deviation; allows apples-to-apples comparison of a stock's past movement and comparison between stocks
- **Implied volatility (IV)** = component of an option's price related to time remaining; based on HV but also incorporates **supply and demand pricing pressures** for the individual option; reflects what market participants expect to see happen; more reactive to news and sentiment than HV

**Key distinction:** HV uses a specific formula with past data; IV is based on HV but is more abstract and reacts more immediately to news events

### Option Pricing Model [MUST KNOW]
- An **option pricing model** uses stock and option data to provide a **theoretical value** for the option
- **Expensive isn't always bad; cheap isn't always good** — you need context
- Difference between model value and actual market value = difference between HV and IV
- **Two ways to use an option calculator:**
  1. Input HV → get theoretical value for call and put at that strike price
  2. Input current market price → get IV

### Intrinsic Value vs. Extrinsic Value [MUST KNOW]
- **Intrinsic Value (Call)** = Market Price of Stock – Option Strike Price (cannot be less than zero)
- **Intrinsic Value (Put)** = Option Strike Price – Market Price of Stock (cannot be less than zero)
- **Extrinsic Value (Time Value)** = Option Price – Intrinsic Value

### Option Moneyness [MUST KNOW]
- **In-the-money (ITM)**: Intrinsic Value > 0
- **At-the-money (ATM)**: Intrinsic Value = 0
- **Out-of-the-money (OTM)**: Intrinsic Value < 0 (only extrinsic/time value)

### Costs Unique to Options [MUST KNOW]
1. **Liquidity**: ease with which you can enter/exit without impacting price; low liquidity = wider bid-ask spread = higher costs; **slippage** = money lost due to the spread
2. **Time**: more time until expiration = greater cost
3. **Volatility**: more volatile underlying = more expensive option

> **REMEMBER: Liquidity saves you money.** Lean toward **higher open interest contracts with higher volumes** to reduce slippage costs.

> **WARNING: Understand time-value decay and how it accelerates near expiration. Exit long options at least 30 days before they expire.**

### Leverage with Reduced Risk — Example [MUST KNOW]
**Scenario: ABC trading at $43, 40 strike call**
- Stock moves from $43 → $47; call moves from ~$4 → ~$7

| | Stock (50% margin) | Call Option |
|---|---|---|
| Initial Investment | $2,150 | $400 |
| Gain | $400 | $300 |
| Gain % | 18.6% | 75% |
| Catastrophic drop to $30: Loss | (60%) | (100%) |

**Key conclusion:** Although the loss percentage is higher for the option, **it is capped**. Stock losses can continue and generate margin calls.

## Chapter 4: Option Risks and Rewards

### Understanding Trading Risks [MUST KNOW]
- Before trading, know: (1) maximum amount of loss possible, (2) probability of sustaining a loss
- Professional traders spend significant time mapping out potential risks **before** worrying about reward
- **Gap down risk**: stocks may open significantly below the previous close — below your pre-decided exit level — creating larger losses than planned

### Stock Risk [MUST KNOW]
- **Long stock**: maximum risk = # shares × price (can only fall to zero); on margin: doubles risk, adds interest
- **Short stock**: unlimited risk — stock can rise indefinitely; particularly vulnerable to unexpected positive news; requires margin account; NOT allowed in retirement accounts

**Margin interest formula:** Annual rate × (days held ÷ 360)

### Option Risk [MUST KNOW]
- **Buying** calls or puts: risk is **limited to the initial investment (premium paid)**
- **Selling** options: can incur losses **greater than the initial investment**
- Chance that an option will go to zero is 100% because it eventually expires
- When stock is **above** call strike price (ITM): time decay can still result in losses but they're limited (intrinsic value remains)
- When stock is **below** call strike price (OTM): value is all time value → diminishes to zero → **total loss of initial investment**
- Same logic for puts in reverse (below strike = ITM = limited loss; above strike = OTM = total loss)

### Breakeven Formulas [MUST KNOW]
```
Call Breakeven = Call Strike Price + Call Purchase Premium
Put Breakeven = Put Strike Price – Put Purchase Premium
```
- Stock must rise **above** call breakeven for call profits
- Stock must fall **below** put breakeven for put profits

### Risk Graphs [MUST KNOW]
- **Long stock**: unlimited reward, losses limited to zero; breakeven = entry price
- **Short stock**: profit limited to zero; unlimited risk on upside
- **Long call**: flat max loss at premium paid below strike; unlimited reward above breakeven (strike + premium)
- **Long put**: flat max loss at premium paid above strike; high but limited reward below breakeven (strike − premium)

### Combined Positions [MUST KNOW]
- **Married put** (long stock + long put): creates a floor on losses; risk graph similar to a long call — capped downside, unlimited upside
- **Covered call** (long stock + short call): income generation; caps both downside (slightly) and upside
- **Collar** (long stock + long put + short call): limits both downside and upside

[CHART: chart_p081_01.png] — Married put risk graph: flat loss floor at put strike, unlimited upside; loss is capped regardless of how far stock falls

## Chapter 5: Analyzing Mood Swings in the Market

### Macro Factors to Monitor [SHOULD KNOW]
- **Interest rates**: low/falling rates are bullish for stocks; rising rates eventually negative; **Federal Reserve** remains most influential central bank globally
- **Global economic trends**: now includes **People's Bank of China** and **European Central Bank**
- **Brexit** (2016) and **Trump election** (2016) reversed decades-long trade doctrines — game-changers affecting all three economic areas

### Market Breadth [MUST KNOW]
- **Market breadth** = the market's heartbeat; gives in-depth look at internal components of market rises and declines
- Track **advancing vs. declining stocks** to gauge health of any move
- **Bearish divergence** = advancing market + declining stocks outnumbering advancing stocks
- **Bullish sign**: stocks in variety of sectors trading above their **200-day moving average** (line dividing bull and bear markets)

> "Keep the odds in your favor by using a variety of tools to confirm your market assessments and then use strategies consistent with such assessments."

### Advance-Decline (A-D) Line [MUST KNOW]
- **Formula**: # of Advancing Issues – # of Declining Issues (kept as daily cumulative total)
- Used with **NYSE Composite Index (NYA)**; NOT looking for a specific number — use it as a **confirming or diverging tool via visual inspection**
- **Bearish divergence**: NYA making new highs while A-D line is failing to confirm (making lower highs) → serious warning signal
- **Historical fact**: every significant decline since 1987 has been preceded by a negative divergence in the NYSE Advance-Decline line

[CHART: chart_p090_01.png] — Daily A-D Line with NYA overlay (2007 data): A-D line diverged bearishly from NYA from June–July 2007, correctly predicting serious declines

**Duarte's A-D line technical toolkit:**
- **20-day SMA**: A-D line above = trend UP; below = trend DOWN; chopping = seeking direction
- **RSI(14)**: very low readings = market bottom; very high readings = market top
- **ROC(12)**: gauges whether momentum in current direction is rising or falling; change in ROC warns of change in A-D trend

[CHART: chart_p093_02.png] — NYSE A-D line (SNYAD) with 20-day MA, RSI(14), and ROC(12): 5-year chart (2013–2017) showing bullish conditions post-2016 election

### Sentiment Analysis [MUST KNOW]
- **Sentiment** = overriding bias for the market — bullish or bearish
- **Greed** drives bullish markets; **Fear** drives declines; these patterns repeat decade after decade
- Sentiment analysis tries to identify when greed is unsustainable or fear is exhausted
- Markets act on whatever information is available at any time — whether the response is right or wrong

> "Option data primarily provides insight to fear. Historical volatility and volume measures give you a feel for how much emotion was involved with moves in the past. Implied volatility levels let you know what's anticipated for the road ahead."

### Put-to-Call (P:C) Ratios [MUST KNOW]
- Created by **Martin Zweig** (who predicted the **1987 crash** on national television the Friday before Black Monday)
- Formula: divide put contract volume by call contract volume
- P:C ratios are **contrarian measures** — implications are opposite to current sentiment
- **Extremely low readings** → bearish (everyone bullish/complacent → danger of decline)
- **Extremely high readings** → bullish (excessive bearishness → conditions right for a rally)

**Key P:C instruments:**
- **CBOE equity P:C ratio**: readings above **0.90** → increased fear / oversold; above **1.0** reflects rising fear
- **CBOE index-only P:C ratio** (SPX and OEX): NOT a contrarian signal — sophisticated index traders who buy puts tend to be right; readings above **2.4** (~3 standard deviations above mean) have occurred immediately before market tops
- **ISE Sentiment Index (ISEE)**: call/put ratio (inverse of P:C); focuses on new buys only; readings less than **85** (2SD below average) have coincided with short-term and intermediate-term bottoms

### SPY P:C Ratio Approach [MUST KNOW]
[CHART: chart_p102_03.png] — SPY price line with raw P:C ratio and 5-day SMA of P:C ratio. When 5-day SMA reached +1SD above mean, near-term bottom was signaled **six of eight times**.
- Use 5-day SMA to smooth the raw P:C ratio noise; use ±1SD to identify extremes

### Put Volume Indicator (PVI) [MUST KNOW]
- Created by **John Bollinger**; measures relative put activity levels
- **PVI calculation**: daily put volume ÷ 10-day SMA of that volume
- High PVI readings signal bears are gaining the upper hand and acceleration of downtrend may be near — but a reversal may also be close at hand

[CHART: chart_p104_02.png] — Daily SPY PVI with mean and ±2SD, ±1SD lines. Four of six extreme highs coincided with acceleration of selling and subsequent bottom reversal.

### Volatility Indicators [MUST KNOW]
- Fear is a **stronger emotion than greed** — markets fall faster than they rise
- **VIX** = **volatility index**: blended IV value calculated using specific S&P 500 Index option contracts
- **Climbing VIX** → bearish conditions in SPX; **Declining VIX** → neutral to bullish conditions
- **SPX and VIX are negatively correlated** — when SPX goes down, VIX goes up
- Other volatility indexes: **VXN** (Nasdaq Composite), **VXO** (S&P 100)

[CHART: chart_p107_03.png] — Weekly VIX chart with SPX overlay: dramatic VIX spikes coincide with SPX collapses; as SPX trended up over years, VIX trended down to low teens

### Volatility Strategy Rules [MUST KNOW]
- **Goal**: buy options when IV is relatively low; sell when IV is relatively high
- When IV is **relatively low and increases quickly** → adds value to both calls and puts
- When IV is **relatively high and decreases quickly** → decreases value for both calls and puts
- Pending news and unexpected events can **spike IV suddenly**; after the news, IV declines as quickly as it spiked

### Sentiment Tools Summary [MUST KNOW]
- Use **2 or 3 option sentiment tools at most** — more is not better; redundancy causes "indicator burnout"
- Use tools that provide **different or complementary information**
- Identify **atypical readings** by calculating average value and standard deviations
- Exception to contrarian logic: CBOE index-only P:C ratio (sophisticated traders who buy puts tend to be right)

## Chapter 6: Sector Analysis — Technical and Fundamental

### Chart Basics [MUST KNOW]
- **Line chart**: single price data point per period connected by a line; typically plots closing price; great big-picture view; lacks intra-period strength and gap information
- **OHLC bar chart**: vertical line (low to high), horizontal tab left (open), horizontal tab right (close); long bar = high volatility = important for option traders; closes near high = strength
- **Candlestick chart**: most used by professionals; same as OHLC but with thickened body (open-to-close); unique patterns describe bull/bear battles; best on daily charts
- **Best practice**: use both — line charts for big-picture trends, OHLC/candlestick for price action detail

### Multiple Timeframe Analysis [MUST KNOW]
- **Long-term major trends** — monthly charts on indexes and sectors
- **Intermediate-term** — weekly charts on broad market indexes and sectors
- **Short-term minor trends** — daily charts on sectors
- Longer-term trends are considered **stronger** than shorter-term ones

### Support, Resistance, and Trends [MUST KNOW]
- **Support**: chart area where buyers come into a falling trend; draw line after price touches level **twice**, confirmed on **third touch**
- **Resistance**: chart area where sellers come into a rising trend; same drawing rules
- Previous support **often serves as resistance** in the future and vice versa (support/resistance flip)
- **Upward trend**: rising line drawn **under pullbacks** showing **higher lows**; confirmed on third test
- **Downward trend**: declining line drawn **above retracement peaks** showing **lower highs**
- **CRITICAL**: Stop losses for a loss are **written in stone**; profit targets are flexible

### Moving Averages [MUST KNOW]
- **SMA** (Simple Moving Average): equally weights all closes for the period
- **EMA** (Exponential Moving Average): greater weight to more recent data; more responsive to price changes
- **Standard settings:**
  - **20-day MA** — short-term trends
  - **50-day MA** — intermediate-term trends
  - **200-day MA** — long-term trends (same as 40-week EMA)
- Break of **200-day MA** is significant and may confirm a **trend reversal**

### Relative Ratios and Sector Analysis [MUST KNOW]
- **Relative ratio line**: constructed by dividing one security's price by another; rises when primary security outperforms; falls when it underperforms
- **Moving average crossovers** = trend confirmation, NOT timing tools
- **Sector rotation**: cash flows from outperforming market/sector to another as economic conditions change
- **Ten Select Sector S&P Depository Receipts (SPDRs)** for sector comparison; SPY tracks entire S&P 500

[PAGE IMAGE: page_122.png] — XLF vs. SPY weekly relative ratio chart: SPY outperformed XLF through 2007, preceding the first down leg of the U.S. market related to the subprime mortgage crisis

**Trading rules from relative ratios:**
- Relative strength lines serve as **leading indicators**; moving averages confirm
- Once a **negative EMA cross** occurs, favor bearish strategies
- Apply same trend line rules to relative ratio lines

### Rate of Change (ROC) [MUST KNOW]
- **Formula**: (Today's price ÷ Price 10 Trading Days Ago) × 100
- **ROC trade signal**: ROC rises **above** its SMA → bullish alert; ROC crosses **below** SMA → bearish alert
- ROC ranks sectors; use **chart trend analysis** to confirm direction — never act on ROC alone

### Volatility Indicators [MUST KNOW]
- **Statistical Volatility (SV)**: uses closing values to plot annualized standard deviation line; lagging indicator
- **ATR (Average True Range)**: developed by **J. Welles Wilder**; uses True Range (TR) = greatest of: High-Low today, Yesterday's Close – Today's High, Yesterday's Close – Today's Low; ATR = EMA of TR; captures gaps that simple high-low range misses; lagging indicator; can lead SV at inflection points (Brexit example)
- **Key rule**: buy options during periods of **low IV**; sell options as volatility rises

[PAGE IMAGE: page_131.png] — SPY daily chart with 14-day ATR and HV(20,262): Brexit (June 2016) ATR bottomed two weeks BEFORE SV; 2016 election low ATR and HV predicted steady market with multiple new highs

### Bollinger Bands [MUST KNOW]
- Developed by **John Bollinger**; SMA (20-period default) surrounded by ±2 standard deviation bands
- Bands **contract** when volatility decreases; **expand** when volatility increases
- **Bandwidth (BW)** = (Upper BB – Lower BB) ÷ Moving Average
- **BW at 6-month low** → **squeeze candidate**: consolidating before a potentially strong breakout; use straddles when direction unclear
- **%b** = identifies where price is relative to bandwidth:
  - 0 to 100: price between the bands
  - < 0: below lower band (bearish)
  - > 100: above upper band (bullish)
- **Key insight**: price **touching a Bollinger band is strength, not exhaustion** — look for pullbacks to MA for entries

### Price Projections [MUST KNOW]
- **Support and resistance**: useful for entry and exit points; identifying stop-loss and profit-taking exits; calculating risk-reward ratios
- **Trend lines**: identify intact trends; dynamic stops increase over time as trend continues; break of trend line = original reason for trade no longer valid
- **Regression channels**: center line (expected value), upper/lower boundaries
  - Exit rules: exit on Monday after weekly close **outside lower channel line**; take profits if price moves above upper channel line and returns; take partial profits at middle regression line
- **Fibonacci ratios**: 0.382, 0.500, 0.618, 1.00, 1.618, 2.618; used to define potential retracement and extension areas
- **Volume as confirming indicator**: during an uptrend, if **volume increases as price declines toward trend line** → bearish alert; break with increasing volume = more bearish evidence

### Probability Framework [MUST KNOW]
**Table 6-2 — Lining Up Probabilities:** Indicators for XLI bullish reversal: Price (2-day higher close = bullish), ATR (declining after peak = bullish), ROC (crossing above SMA = bullish), SV (diminishing = neutral-bullish), 200-day EMA (flat = neutral), 20-day and 50-day EMA (downward = bearish)

> "No system is perfect, so at some point you have to take a chance on a trade... waiting for every tool to turn perfectly bullish will typically result in no trading activity at all. Worse, when everything is perfect, your signal will be created toward the end of a move."

> "If you lose a little money 60% of the time but you are able to make good money the rest of the time, you are more likely to come out ahead. The big problems in trading are the ones caused by frequent heavy losses."

**Strategy-market condition matching:**
- **Bullish, low volatility** → long call, married put, ATM call debit spread
- **Bullish, high volatility** → covered calls, credit spreads
- **Bearish, low volatility** → long put, put debit spread
- **Bearish, high volatility** → call credit spreads
- **Range-bound, high volatility** → butterfly, condor
- **Range-bound, low volatility** → calendar spreads, diagonal spreads

## Chapter 7: Practicing Before You Swing

### Paper Trading [MUST KNOW]
- Trading is like professional sports — repetition builds hand-eye coordination, muscle memory, and performance; **paper trade before risking real money**
- Paper trading is excellent for: working out analysis methods, building record-keeping habits, learning to respond to the market, training yourself to minimize losses
- **Honest limitation**: paper trading is not real trading; doesn't fully prepare you emotionally for the battle of greed and fear — but forces you to address situations mentally before real money is at risk

**Advantages vs. Disadvantages:**
| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-----------|---------------|
| Provides feedback via profits/losses | Does not prepare you emotionally |
| Allows incorporating all trade costs | No assignments |
| Identifies issues not considered | Doesn't address margin problems |
| Avoids account losses | Doesn't help with trade execution nuances |

### Tracking Greek Changes [MUST KNOW]
- Set up a spreadsheet to track **daily**: underlying price, ITM/ATM/OTM calls and puts at varying expirations, intrinsic value, time value, delta, gamma, theta
- **Option prices do not change by the exact amounts projected by the Greeks** — the Greeks are approximations

[PAGE IMAGE: page_155.png] — MSFT tracking spreadsheet (Aug 1-31, 2007): as MSFT fell from $29.30 to $27.93, call value fell from $1.10 to $0.35; put rose from $1.61 to $2.25

### Implied Volatility Rules [MUST KNOW]
- **HV (Historical/Statistical Volatility)** = measure of past price movement
- **IV (Implied Volatility)** = volatility implied by the current option price; measure of expected FUTURE movement
- **High IV** (52-week range) → correctly predicts large future price movements
- **Low IV** → correctly predicts small future price movements
- Buy options in **low IV environments**; high IV periods offer excellent **exit points**
- **IV at 30%** for a $100 stock means: stock may trade between $70–$130 over the next year (1 standard deviation = 68.2% probability)

### IV Crush [MUST KNOW]
- **IV crush**: when IV is relatively high and then drops significantly — usually event-driven (earnings, major leadership change, product launches)
- When the event becomes reality, uncertainty disappears → IV drops
- **Warning**: selling high IV options in hopes of IV crush is dangerous — the news itself may have caused a significant stock price move, overwhelming the IV decline

### Time Value Decay "Sweet Spot" [MUST KNOW]
- Selling options with **30 to 45 days to expiration** accelerates time-value decay for you
- The "sweet spot" combines: an accelerating rate of decay AND a sizeable amount of remaining time value
- Time-value decay is **NOT a linear constant** — drops like a waterfall, accelerating near expiration

### IV Varies By Strike Price [MUST KNOW]
- Usually **ATM IV is the lowest**
- **Skew charts** (Chapter 15) provide IV by strike price
- The deeper ITM the option, the **less impact IV has** on total premium
- IV is the primary determinant of **time value**

### Trading Systems [MUST KNOW]
- A **trading system** has specific rules for entry and exit; once designed and tested, **no decision-making during implementation**
- When using a trading system: establish a position for **ALL** buy signals; exit when exit signal generated
- Cherry-picking signals invalidates the backtest results
- **Backtesting**: use past data to determine if system generates stable profits; include bullish, bearish, AND sideways periods
- **Forward testing**: run rules on more recent period after backtesting; expect **diminishing returns**

**ROC Trending System Backtest (6-year period, 6 semiconductor stocks):**
| Run | Avg Return | Gain:Loss | Max Loss |
|-----|-----------|-----------|----------|
| Test1 (no stop) | 4.7% | 1.96:1 | 35% |
| Test2 (15% stop) | 4.6% | 1.91:1 | 16% |
| Forward Test | 1.6% | 1.56:1 | 10% |

Key: 15% stop reduced max loss from 35% to 16% with only minor profitability reduction

### Three Rules for Cutting Losses / Taking Profits [MUST KNOW]
1. **Cut losses** (primary focus)
2. **Prevent profits from turning into losses** (trailing stops)
3. **Let profits run**

### Strategy Mastery [MUST KNOW]
- Strategy mastery does NOT mean every trade is profitable — it means the appropriate conditions were in place, putting the odds in your favor
- Mastery can take **years** to evolve; best trades often take the longest to find
- **Determine appropriate trade sizes BEFORE analyzing a specific trade** to minimize account risk
- When trying a new strategy (after paper trading), further **reduce trade sizes**
- Focus on three things in order: cutting losses, preventing profits from turning into losses, letting profits run

## Chapter 8: Designing a Killer Trading Plan

### Developing a Reliable Plan [MUST KNOW]
- No matter what you trade, you are **running a business** — develop as a trader = evolve as a business manager
- Write the plan down; put it somewhere visible
- **Set realistic goals** with quantifiable measures of success (e.g., making a monthly car payment)
- Keep excellent records of trades, results, and expenses

### Managing Costs [MUST KNOW]
- **Education costs**: decline over time as you learn the craft; largest early expense is learning curve losses
- **Analysis costs**: may increase as you add charting platforms and data services; limit subscriptions
- **Trading costs**: commission + **slippage** (cost associated with the bid-ask spread)
- **Taxes**: trading in retirement accounts defers taxes (limited strategies allowed); establishing options when holding the underlying may trigger a tax event
- **Losses**: part of operating expenses — minimize by managing risk; will likely be higher at first

### Order Execution [MUST KNOW]
**Four option order types:**
| Order | Guarantees |
|-------|------------|
| Market order | Execution |
| Limit order | Price |
| Stop order | Execution |
| Stop-limit order | Price |

- **Buy to Open, Sell to Open, Sell to Close, Buy to Close** — must specify open/close for OCC reconciliation
- **Stops are superior to stop-limit orders** for managing risk because they guarantee execution
- **Order duration**: Day or Good 'Til Canceled (GTC); market orders are day-only

### Option Stop Order Challenges [MUST KNOW]
- Option stop can be triggered in two ways: if a trade executes at the stop price, OR if the bid/ask **moves** to the stop price
- Due to low volume, the **quote is frequently the trigger** (not an actual trade)
- When placing a stop on an option, you use a **maximum risk amount** to target an exit price — it's an **estimate**
- **Sell-stop**: triggered when option trades at or below your stop OR if the ask reaches your stop
- **Buy-stop**: triggered when option trades at or above your stop OR if the bid reaches your stop
- **Account for the bid-ask spread when determining an option stop level**
- Some option contracts may only allow **day-stop orders** — enter a new stop order each evening

### OCO (One Cancels Other) Orders [MUST KNOW]
- Allows two different active orders for the same position; when one executes, the other automatically cancels
- Use OCO to have both a stop-loss (risk management) AND a limit order (profit-taking) active simultaneously
- **Without OCO**: a strong swing could execute BOTH orders, potentially leaving an unlimited-risk position

### Combination Orders [MUST KNOW]
- Combination positions can be entered as a **single combined order** OR as **individual orders (legging in)**
- Single combined order: better chance of execution between bid-ask spread; ALL legs execute or none
- **Debit spread**: moderately reduce the limit **below** the market
- **Credit spread**: moderately increase the limit **above** the market
- Spread orders have higher execution risk — takes more time to receive confirmation

### Rolling an Option Position [MUST KNOW]
- **Rolling out**: pushing back the expiration date; pays extra commission, gains time value
- **Rolling up**: higher strike price — to avoid assignment or capitalize on high IV at higher strike
- **Rolling down**: lower strike price — to avoid assignment or capitalize on high IV at lower strike
- Can combine: roll out AND up, or roll out AND down

### Contingent vs. Stop Orders [MUST KNOW]
- **Stop order**: active on the **exchange** — visible to the market
- **Contingent order**: active on your **broker's system** — market doesn't see it (advantage)
- Best reason to use contingent orders: allows you to identify **technical exit points** for the underlying stock
- Use OCO when you have triggers set both above AND below the market simultaneously

## Chapter 9: Getting to Know Different Option Styles

### Index Basics [MUST KNOW]
- An **index** = pool of a singular type of financial instrument grouped into a single value
- You **cannot buy an index** — buy ETFs or mutual funds tracking it; index options are **cash-settled**
- Three index construction methods:
  1. **Price-weighted** (Dow Jones): higher-priced stocks have more impact
  2. **Market cap-weighted** (S&P 500): higher market-cap stocks have more impact
  3. **Equal dollar-weighted**: each stock has same impact; requires quarterly rebalancing

**Key indexes:**
- **S&P 500 (SPX)**: diverse U.S. large-cap stocks; companion ETF: **SPY** (stock-settled)
- **Dow Jones**: 30 blue-chip companies; companion ETF: **DIA**
- **Nasdaq-100 (NDX)**: 100 largest nonfinancial Nasdaq companies; primarily tech; companion ETF: **QQQ**
- **PHLX Semiconductor Sector Index (SOX)**: 19 semiconductor companies
- **Russell 2000**: small-cap, growth-oriented; expect big moves in both directions

### Index Option Value Determinants [MUST KNOW]
1. Type (call or put): calls rise with index; puts rise when index falls
2. Value of index relative to strike price (same moneyness rules as stock options)
3. Time to expiration (time decay applies identically)
4. Historical volatility of the index
5. Expected future volatility

**Cash settlement example** (SPX closes at 1,523; you own June 1,520 call):
```
(Index Settlement Value – Call Strike Price) × Multiplier
(1,523 – 1,520) × 100 = $300 credit to you
```

### American vs. European Style [MUST KNOW]
- **American style**: exercise your rights **at any point up to** exercise cutoff time
  - All stock and ETF options that use stock as deliverable = American style
  - Trades until last trading day prior to expiration
- **European style**: can **only exercise on a designated date** — the exercise date
  - Options based on indexes are **often (but not always)** European style
  - Exercise is **automatic** if option closes ITM (no action required)
  - Last trading date may be a **Thursday** rather than Friday — check specs!
  - Completely avoid assignment by buying back before expiration
  - **No early assignment risk** for short contracts (advantage)

**Exception — American-style index options:**
- **S&P 100 Index (OEX)**: American style but cash-settled — can exercise at any time
- Also: **Gold/Silver Index (XAU)** and **Semiconductor Index (SOX)**

### SPX Option Specifications (CBOE) [MUST KNOW]
- **Exercise style**: European-style — automatic exercise date
- **Settlement**: for third-Friday options, uses **opening prices** (SET ticker) on last business day before expiration; for other Fridays and Mon/Wed expirations, uses **afternoon closing index value**
- **Last trading day**: for third-Friday options, typically the Thursday before expiration (not Friday!)
- **Trading hours**: 8:30 a.m. to 3:15 p.m. Central Time
- **Multiplier**: 100

> **WARNING**: Before trading any index option, **first check the specs**. Settlement methods vary significantly.

### Exercise Decision [MUST KNOW]
- **Early exercise is rarely desirable** — it forfeits the option's time value
- Before exercising, calculate and compare: (1) exercising rights, (2) selling the option in the market
- **Never exercise an option if it still has time value** — sell the option instead to capture time value
- **General rule**: when option has more than $0.20 time value remaining, selling the option will result in a credit exceeding the extra transaction cost

**Example**: own ABC stock at $23, bought $21 put for $2.00; stock plummets to $20, put moves to $5.00
- Recommendation: sell BOTH the stock and the put — put has nice gain it would lose if stock rallied; selling captures time value

### European-Style Cash Settlement [MUST KNOW]
```
Index – Option Strike Price = Call Moneyness
Option Strike Price – Index = Put Moneyness
Option Moneyness × Option Multiplier = Cash Settlement Amount
```
- Settlement tickers: SPX = **SET**, RUT = **RLS**, NDX = **NDS**
- Find at: www.cboe.com/data/historical-options-data/index-settlement-values

### Assignment Mechanics [MUST KNOW]
- **Short put assigned**: option removed + Buy transaction occurs for underlying stock
- **Short call assigned**: option removed + Sell transaction occurs for underlying stock (or short position if uncovered)
- **Early assignment** most likely when option is **deep in-the-money** with little time value
- **Puts**: generally better chance of early assignment (exerciser gets cash immediately)
- **Calls**: early assignment risk jumps when **ex-dividend date** is 1-2 days away AND dividend > option's remaining time value
- All options ITM by **$0.01 or more** at expiration get **automatically assigned/exercised** (OCC rule)

### Expiration Management [MUST KNOW]
| Option Type | Action | Consideration |
|-------------|--------|---------------|
| Long: OTM | Expires worthless | If it has value, sell for credit > commission |
| Short: OTM | Expires worthless | Monitor news; anyone can still exercise, causing assignment |
| Short: ITM | Assigned | Close on last trading day to avoid; auto-exercise occurs at $0.01 ITM |

**European-style at expiration**: know the option's last trading day and how settlement value is determined; account will be **debited the difference between settlement value and strike price** if ITM at expiration

**Warning for SPX short OTM puts**: bad news after Thursday close could cause a strong morning drop when SET is determined, turning OTM puts into ITM

## Chapter 10: Protecting Your Portfolio with Options

### Protective Put Basics [MUST KNOW]
- **Married put**: stock and put purchased simultaneously
- **Protective put**: stock and put purchased separately (different timing)
- Functionally identical — buy book uses "protective put" throughout
- Ratio: **one long put for every 100 shares of stock held**

**Two key decisions:**
1. **Term for the protection** (expiration month) — for temporary 30–60 day protection, use options with 60–90 days to expiration
2. **Level of protection** (strike price and option price)

### Option Chain Data for XYZ (Stock at $37.50) [MUST KNOW]
| Month | Strike Price | Bid | Ask | OI |
|-------|-------------|-----|-----|-----|
| Oct | 35.00 | 0.60 | 0.70 | 24,641 |
| Oct | 37.50 | 1.25 | 1.05 | 1,338 |
| Jan | 35.00 | 1.25 | 1.35 | 52,734 |
| Jan | 37.50 | 2.00 | 2.15 | 24,225 |

**Cost per day formula:**
```
Option Premium × 100 ÷ Days to Expiration = Daily Cost of Protection
Oct 37.50 Put @ $1.05: $105 ÷ 60 days = $1.75/day
Jan 37.50 Put @ $2.15: $215 ÷ 150 days = $1.43/day
```
Jan put is cheaper per day ($1.43 vs $1.75) AND covers 2.5× longer → better value

### Theta Decay Table [MUST KNOW]
| Days to Expire | Ask | Theta |
|---------------|-----|-------|
| 60 | $1.05 | −0.0078 |
| 30 | $0.75 | −0.0117 |
| 10 | $0.45 | −0.0216 |
| 5 | $0.30 | −0.0314 |

Theta nearly doubles from 60→30 days; doubles again from 30→10 days; accelerating waterfall decay

> **WARNING**: Impact of time decay accelerates in the last 30 days. Exit long option positions **30 days prior to expiration** to avoid accelerating losses to extrinsic value.

### Bearish Position Comparison [MUST KNOW]
**Table 10-4: Long Put vs. Short Stock (XYZ at $37.50)**
| Position | Entry Cost | Max Risk | Max Reward |
|----------|------------|----------|------------|
| Long 1 Oct 37.50 Put | $105 | $105 | $3,645 |
| Short 100 Shares XYZ | $3,750 | Unlimited | $3,750 |

**Key conclusion**: Buying puts is less risky than shorting stock — maximum loss is capped at premium ($105 vs. unlimited). Main cost: time decay erodes the put's value.

### Hedging [MUST KNOW]
- **Hedge**: a position used to offset losses in a security resulting from adverse market moves
- **Perfect hedge**: option gains exactly offset stock losses ($1 down in XYZ = $1 up in hedge)
- ATM put options have delta near –0.50; **two ATM puts** have combined delta close to –1.0 (mirrors stock's +1.0 delta)
- Perfect hedges are expensive; most practical hedges are **partial**

**Delta approach for portfolio hedging:**
- 100 shares of stock = +100 delta
- To perfectly hedge 100 shares: position delta should be as close to 0 as possible
- Example: Jan 35 put with delta of –29.1 → need **4 puts**: 4 × (–29.1) = –116.4 delta; combined with 116 shares ≈ 0 net delta
- Hedge is impermanent — delta changes by gamma for every $1 move in underlying

### Index Hedging (OEX Example) [SHOULD KNOW]
- Scenario: $225,000 portfolio correlated to OEX (trading at ~1040)
- Using 2 Mar 1040 puts: 2 × 1040 × 100 = $208,000 protection = 92.44% of portfolio
- As OEX drops below 1040, puts gain intrinsic value at rate of put's delta; once ITM, gain 1-for-1
- Delta approach with Mar 1050 puts (delta –0.549): 2 puts × –0.549 = –1.10 combined delta → $210,000 × 1.1 = **$231,000** portfolio protected

### Adjusted Options [MUST KNOW]
- **Adjusted options**: options that existed when corporate actions took place; contract terms adjusted to reflect those actions
- Corporate actions triggering adjustments: stock splits, large cash dividends, mergers/acquisitions, spinoffs
- **2:1 stock split**: number of contracts doubled; strike price cut in half
- **3:2 split**: multiplier changes to 150; strike price changes to two-thirds of original
- **Special dividends**: strike prices adjusted down by the amount of the special dividend
- **Never create a new position using an adjusted option** you don't completely understand
- Check CBOE (www.cboe.com) for adjusted option specifications

## Chapter 11: Increasing Profit Potential and Decreasing Risk

### Leveraging Assets to Reduce Risk [MUST KNOW]
- Options leverage assets while decreasing the amount of investment at risk
- Your initial investment = maximum potential loss (for long options)
- With options: less capital at risk, less money spent, same upside potential — tradeoff is slower profit accrual

### Long Stock vs. Long Call Risk Graph [MUST KNOW]
[PAGE IMAGE: page_259.png] — Long stock (dashed line, breakeven at $36) vs. long call (solid line, breakeven at $40): stock has much steeper loss profile as price falls (to –$3,900); call caps loss at –$300; profits accrue faster with stock (higher delta) but the tradeoff (slower gains in exchange for dramatically reduced downside) is worth taking

### Three Factors That Change Option Value [MUST KNOW]
1. **Stock price moves** (up or down with underlying)
2. **Implied volatility (IV)** increases or decreases — higher IV = higher contract value
3. **Time passes** — always decreases option value (theta decay)

> "Establish long option positions when implied volatility (IV) is relatively low to increase the probability for profits and minimize losses due to decreases in IV."

### LEAPS [MUST KNOW]
- **LEAPS** = Long-term Equity AntiCiPation Security — just an option with long time to expiration
- Time to expiration: **9 months to 3 years**; expiration month almost always **January**
- Not all stocks with options have LEAPS available
- Created in May, June, or July; new contracts expire in January ~2½ years from creation date
- More time = more money you pay, but greater chance of holding a contract ITM at expiration

### Three Requirements for Basic Option Trading [MUST KNOW]
1. Correctly predict the **direction** of the underlying move
2. Correctly predict the **magnitude** of the move
3. Correctly predict the **maximum time** it will take for the move to occur

> "There are times when a stock breaks out of a limited-range, sideways channel, only to return back into the channel. As a trader, if you've created a directional position based on the breakout, you **must exit the position** if the stock returns to the channel."

### Covered vs. Naked Short Options [MUST KNOW]
- **Covered position**: short option obligation can be satisfied using stock or by exercising a long option of the same type — significantly reduced risk
- **Naked call**: unlimited risk, limited reward — the riskiest position you can create; months of small credits can be wiped out by one trade
- **Naked put**: very risky even if willing to buy stock at strike price; assignment occurs when stock is declining

### Vertical Spreads — Introduction [MUST KNOW]
- A **vertical spread** combines two options — one long and one short of the same type — with the same expiration month and **different strike prices**
- "Vertical" = how strike prices line up in an option chain
- Each option in a spread = a **leg**
- Vertical spreads: **limited risk and limited reward**

### Bull Call Spread (Debit) [MUST KNOW]
- Buy lower-strike call + sell higher-strike call (same month) for a net debit
- Bearish short call reduces the long call cost AND caps upside reward
- **Example (ABC at $37.65):**
  - Buy 1 Jan 35 Call @ $4.20; Sell 1 Jan 40 Call @ $1.50
  - Debit = Max Risk = ($4.20 – $1.50) × 100 = **$270**
  - Max Reward = [($40 – $35) × 100] – $270 = **$230**
  - Breakeven = $35 + $2.70 = **$37.70**
  - Max loss when ABC closes at $35 or less; max reward when ABC closes at $40 or higher

[PAGE IMAGE: page_270.png] — Bull call spread risk graph: max risk $270 (lower horizontal line); max reward $230 (upper horizontal line); diagonal ramp between $35 and $40 strikes; breakeven at $37.70

### Bear Put Spread (Debit) [MUST KNOW]
- Buy higher-strike put + sell lower-strike put (same month) for a net debit
- **Example (XYZ at $50.85):**
  - Buy 1 Jan 50 Put @ $2.75; Sell 1 Jan 45 Put @ $1.30
  - Debit = Max Risk = ($2.75 – $1.30) × 100 = **$145.00**
  - Max Reward = [($50 – $45) × 100] – $145.00 = **$355.00**
  - Breakeven = $50 – $1.45 = **$48.55**

[PAGE IMAGE: page_272.png] — Bear put spread risk graph: max risk $145 (lower line); max reward $355 (upper line); diagonal ramp between $45 and $50 strikes; breakeven at $48.55

### Bear Call Spread (Credit) [MUST KNOW]
- Buy higher-strike call + sell lower-strike call (same month) for a net credit
- The long call covers the short call — bearish outlook
- **Example (XYZ at $50.85):**
  - Buy 1 Jan 55 Call @ $0.95; Sell 1 Jan 50 Call @ $3.20
  - Credit = Max Reward = ($3.20 – $0.95) × 100 = **$225.00**
  - Max Risk = [($55 – $50) × 100] – $225.00 = **$275.00**
  - Breakeven = $50 + $2.25 = **$52.25**

[PAGE IMAGE: page_276.png] — Bear call spread risk graph: max reward $225 (upper line); max risk $275 (lower line); breakeven at $52.25; max reward occurs when stock below $50 (both expire worthless)

### Bull Put Spread (Credit) [MUST KNOW]
- Buy lower-strike put + sell higher-strike put (same month) for a net credit
- The long put covers the short put — bullish outlook
- **Example (ABC at $37.65):**
  - Buy 1 Jan 35 Put @ $1.70; Sell 1 Jan 40 Put @ $4.10
  - Credit = Max Reward = ($4.10 – $1.70) × 100 = **$240.00**
  - Max Risk = [($40 – $35) × 100] – $240.00 = **$260.00**
  - Breakeven = $40 – $2.40 = **$37.60**

[PAGE IMAGE: page_278.png] — Bull put spread risk graph: max reward $240 (upper line); max risk $260 (lower line); breakeven at $37.60; max reward when stock above $40 (both expire worthless)

### Spread Summary Tables [MUST KNOW]
**Debit spreads (you pay to enter):**
| | Bull Call Spread | Bear Put Spread |
|---|---|---|
| Risk | Initial debit | Initial debit |
| Reward | [(Higher – lower strike) × 100] – debit | [(Higher – lower strike) × 100] – debit |
| Breakeven | Long strike + net premium | Long strike – net premium |

**Credit spreads (you receive premium to enter):**
| | Bear Call Spread | Bull Put Spread |
|---|---|---|
| Risk | [(Higher – lower strike) × 100] – credit | [(Higher – lower strike) × 100] – credit |
| Reward | Credit received | Credit received |
| Breakeven | Short strike + net premium | Short strike – net premium |

### Universal Spread Rules [MUST KNOW]
- **Debit spreads**: bullish → bull call or bull put; bearish → bear put or bear call
- **Credit spreads**: receive premium; max reward is the credit if both options expire worthless
- **Always exit both legs together** — never exit just the long leg
- Enter debit spreads with at least **60 days to expiration**
- Close credit spreads for a debit on **last trading day if near the short strike price**
- Use limit orders; shave a little off the market price for spread orders (appealing to floor traders who are naturally hedged)

> **WARNING**: Close a vertical credit spread on the last trading day before expiration if the underlying is near the short strike price — risk of assignment over the weekend without ability to exercise your long contract rights.

## Chapter 12: Combination Strategies — Spreads and Other Wild Things

### Creating Covered Positions [MUST KNOW]
- A **covered position** includes a short option whose obligation can be satisfied with stock or a long option of the same type
- Rather than unlimited or high risk (naked), covered positions significantly reduce risk
- Strategy: sell options against the underlying to bring in income AND reduce position risk by changing the cost basis

### Covered Calls [MUST KNOW]
- Own stock + sell call against the shares → brings in income + reduces risk of long stock position
- **Major downside**: caps potential gains — if stock rises above strike, obligated to sell at strike price
- Best scenario: stock moving **sideways** — advance payment for any further gains up to the strike price
- Best outcome: stock rises above strike → gets called away → buy stock again and sell another covered call
- Short call strike price should be **above the long stock purchase price**
- **Optimal timing**: sell calls within **30–45 days to expiration** (accelerated time decay works for you)

> TIP: If you are extremely bullish in the short term, a long call is a better strategy than a covered call because it allows unlimited gains.

**Covered call formulas:**
```
Max Risk = (Stock Purchase Price × # shares) – Call Premium
Max Reward = [(Call Strike – Stock Purchase Price) × 100] + Credit
Breakeven = Stock Purchase Price – Call Price
```

**Example (ABC at $37.72, 40 strike call at $0.50):**
- Max Risk = $3,772 – $50 = **$3,722**
- Max Reward = ($2.28 × 100) + $50 = **$278**
- Breakeven = $37.72 – $0.50 = **$37.22**

[PAGE IMAGE: page_287.png] — Covered call risk graph: profit line descends steeply to the left (stock decline = full stock loss), crosses zero at $37.22 breakeven, flattens above $40 at ~$278 maximum profit; multiple time-decay lines show short call time value decaying favorably

> **WARNING**: Never allow a short call that is part of a covered position to become uncovered by selling the stock. If you want to exit, buy the call back first, or exit both at the same time.

### Collars [MUST KNOW]
- **Collar** = long stock + long put + short call; **limited-risk, limited-reward** position
- Risk limited to downside by the **put strike price**; reward limited to upside by the **call strike price**
- Goal of short call in a collar: **reduce the cost of protection**
- Best use case: protecting gains late in the calendar year without triggering a taxable event

**Collar formulas:**
```
Net Debit = (Put Price – Call Price) × 100
Max Risk = [(Stock Purchase – Put Strike) × 100] + Net Debit
Max Reward = [(Call Strike – Stock Purchase) × 100] – Net Debit
Breakeven = Stock Purchase Price + Net Debit
```

**Example (ABC at $37.86; 37.50 put @ $1.20; 40 call @ $0.70):**
- Net Debit = (1.20 – 0.70) × 100 = **$50**
- Max Risk = ($0.36 × 100) + $50 = **$86**
- Max Reward = ($2.14 × 100) – $50 = **$164**
- Breakeven = $37.86 + $0.50 = **$38.36**

[PAGE IMAGE: page_292.png] — Collar risk graph: "flat-bottom, rising middle, flat-top" shape; flat at –$86 below put strike, rises to cross zero at $38.36, flattens at +$164 above call strike

> WARNING: Once you sell a call short in a collar, you are obligated to sell the underlying stock when assigned. Do not create a collar unless you're willing and able to part with the stock.

### Calendar Spreads [MUST KNOW]
- Combines long option (later month) + short option (earlier month) with the **same strike price** and **different expiration months**
- Created for a **net debit** (longer-term option costs more)
- **When to use**: short-term outlook is neutral to bearish/bullish, long-term outlook is the opposite
- **Risk is limited to the initial debit**; maximum profit occurs when stock is **AT the strike price** at expiration of the short option
- If stock moves significantly away from the strike: short option may be assigned → need decision tree

**Three scenarios at short-option expiration:**
1. Stock significantly above strike: consider creating a bull call spread with remaining long call, or exercise long call to satisfy assignment
2. Stock significantly below strike: short call expires worthless; sell the long call, create another spread, or exit if bearish
3. Stock near strike: sell long call, create another calendar, or create a bull call spread

[PAGE IMAGE: page_298.png] — Calendar spread risk graph (tent/bell-curve shape): maximum profit when stock is at strike at short-option expiration; profits taper symmetrically away from strike; after short option expires, remaining long call provides potentially unlimited upside

### Diagonal Spreads [MUST KNOW]
- **Diagonal spreads** = combination of vertical AND calendar spreads — have BOTH different strike prices AND different expiration months
- More flexibility than calendar spreads; both **debit and credit** structures are possible
- When to use:
  - Short-term slightly more bullish than neutral + long-term bullish → **call diagonal spread for debit**
  - Short-term slightly more bearish + long-term bullish → **call diagonal spread for credit**
  - Short-term slightly more bearish than neutral + long-term bearish → **put diagonal spread for debit**
  - Short-term slightly more bullish + long-term bearish → **put diagonal spread for credit**

**Example — call diagonal spread (stock at $46.64):**
- Buy 3-months-later $47.50 call @ $3.10 (IV 32.4)
- Sell next-month $50 call @ $1.80 (IV 34.6)
- Initial Debit (Max Risk) = $3.10 – $1.80 = $1.30 × 100 = **$130**
- Note: modest **time skew** (IV 34.6 vs 32.4) makes sell side relatively attractive

### Volatility Skews [SHOULD KNOW]
- **Volatility skew**: same underlying options have IVs that deviate significantly from each other due to demand factors
- **Price skew**: options expiring same month have different IVs (e.g., ITM options with higher IV than OTM)
- **Time skew**: options expiring different months have different IVs (e.g., near-term options with higher IV than later-term)
- Strategy: **sell the option with atypically high IV** and/or **buy the option with atypically low IV** as part of the spread

> **WARNING**: A calendar or diagonal spread with **short option in the later month and long option in the earlier month** is equivalent to holding a naked position — not consistent with good risk management.
