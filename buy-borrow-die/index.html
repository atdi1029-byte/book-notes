The Buy Borrow Die Guide

The Family-Office Playbook Taught by BigBankTheory: Why You Never Sell, How You Borrow Instead, What You Measure Against, and Every Way It Can Go Wrong

This guide is built from twenty videos by the YouTube creator BigBankTheory, a former UK mortgage professional who now teaches what he calls the family-office way of handling money. His ideas are the backbone. Around each one, this guide adds the machinery he skips: the history, the tax code, the arithmetic, and the research that supports or contradicts him. Every chapter follows the same rhythm. First comes his framework, stated as strongly as he would state it. Then comes the mechanism underneath it, so you can reason about it yourself instead of taking it on faith. Then comes a box labeled The Other Side, which lists the fact-checks, the counterarguments, and the places where his numbers are more optimistic than the record.

The goal is not to sell you the strategy or to talk you out of it. The goal is that by the end you understand it well enough to argue both sides at a dinner table, run the numbers on your own portfolio, and know exactly which assumptions you are betting on. Where he promised a topic but never covered it in the videos (how to track liquidity, trust structuring, the UK version), the guide fills the gap from research and says so plainly. Nothing here is financial advice, a disclaimer he repeats in nearly every video, and one worth repeating here too.

Glossary

These are the terms that come up again and again. Skim them now and come back when a chapter uses one you have forgotten. Each definition ends with a concrete example, because a definition without an example rarely sticks.

The Strategy Itself

Buy Borrow Die (BBD): Buy appreciating assets, never sell them, borrow against them to fund spending, and hold until death so heirs inherit them with a stepped-up tax basis. Example: instead of selling $50,000 of stock for a wedding, you borrow $50,000 against the portfolio and the stock keeps compounding.

Wealth extraction vs wealth creation: His distinction. Creation is picking assets that grow; extraction is getting spending money out without selling them. BBD is only the extraction half. Example: a portfolio returning 4% cannot be rescued by clever borrowing, because there is nothing to extract.

Escape velocity: The portfolio size (he says roughly $1–2 million) at which growth outpaces what you could ever contribute from salary, and lifestyle borrowing becomes safe. Example: a $2M portfolio growing 15% adds $300,000 a year, far more than most people save.

Family office: A private firm that manages one wealthy family’s money: investments, taxes, trusts, borrowing. He uses the phrase to mean “how the rich actually structure things.” Example: the Walton or Rockefeller family offices.

Operator: His word for an investor running BBD deliberately, as opposed to a passive saver. Example: “the operator who opted for the Nasdaq paid $1,500 in tax; the dividend investor paid $55,000.”

Architecture / structure: How assets, accounts and loans are arranged. His claim is that structure matters more than income. Example: Renee (Chapter 29) earns more retired than working, purely by rearranging what she already owns.

Borrowing

Leverage: Using borrowed money so that your exposure is bigger than your own capital. Example: $100,000 of stock plus a $30,000 loan used to buy more stock gives you $130,000 of exposure.

Margin loan: A loan from your broker secured by the securities in your account. No credit check, no monthly payment; interest is added to the balance. Example: clicking “withdraw” at Interactive Brokers and sending $2 million to your bank.

Lombard loan: The European and private-banking name for a loan secured by a portfolio of liquid securities. Same idea as a margin loan. Example: a Swiss private bank lending 60% against a bond-and-equity portfolio.

SBLOC (securities-based line of credit): The US wealth-management version: a revolving credit line secured by a portfolio, usually at a bank affiliate. Example: Schwab Pledged Asset Line, Morgan Stanley Liquidity Access Line.

Loan-to-value (LTV): Loan divided by the gross value of the collateral. His rule: 20% for lifestyle, up to 30% when borrowing to buy more stock. Example: a $400,000 loan against a $2 million portfolio is a 20% LTV.

Capitalized interest: Interest that is added to the loan balance instead of being paid. Example: a $200,000 margin loan at 5% becomes $210,000 after a year if you pay nothing.

Margin call: A demand to add cash or sell assets because your equity has fallen below the broker’s minimum. At many brokers it is not a call at all; the system simply sells. Example: a $100,000 account with an $80,000 loan is wiped out by a modest drop.

Maintenance margin: The minimum equity, as a share of the account, you must keep. FINRA’s floor is 25%; brokers can require more. Example: at 25%, a $1 million portfolio with a $400,000 loan is called if it falls to about $533,000.

Reg T: The Federal Reserve rule that sets initial margin at 50% for stock purchases. Example: with $100,000 you can hold at most $200,000 of stock in a Reg T account.

Portfolio margin: A risk-based margin system that looks at the whole portfolio’s simulated losses, usually allowing much more leverage. Requires a larger account (around $100,000+). Example: a hedged, diversified account may need only 15% equity instead of 25%.

SOFR: The Secured Overnight Financing Rate, the benchmark most US floating loans now use. Broker margin rates are SOFR or the Fed funds rate plus a spread. Example: SOFR 3.66% + 1% spread = 4.66% margin rate.

Box spread: An options combination that behaves like a loan at close to the Treasury rate. Example: he mentions ~3.75% via box spreads when margin was 6.5%.

Real interest rate: Nominal rate minus inflation. Example: borrowing at 4% while inflation runs at 4% costs you 0% in real terms.

Money and Inflation

M2: The broad US money supply: cash, checking, savings, money-market deposits, small time deposits. Example: about $286 billion in 1959 and about $22 trillion today.

CPI: The Consumer Price Index, the official inflation measure based on a basket of consumer goods. He calls it “the cost of survival.” Example: bread, milk, eggs, rent, iPads.

Monetary debasement: The loss of a currency’s purchasing power as more of it is created. Example: his claim that the dollar loses about half its value against assets every ten years.

Hurdle rate: The minimum return an investment must clear to be worth doing. His hurdle: M2 growth (~7%) plus the loan rate. Example: 7% + 5% loan = 12%, which is why he targets 15%.

Financial repression: Policies that keep interest rates below inflation so government debt shrinks in real terms, quietly taxing savers. Example: 1946–1980 in the US, and December 2021 when the Fed funds rate was near 0% with 7% inflation.

Cantillon effect: New money does not reach everyone at once; those who receive it first (banks, asset holders) benefit before prices rise for everyone else. Example: asset prices rose in 2020 long before wages did.

Liquidity: In his usage, the amount of money flowing through the financial system and looking for assets to buy. Example: 2020–21 (expanding, stocks soared) vs 2022 (contracting, stocks fell).

Reverse repo facility (RRP): A Fed facility where money-market funds park cash overnight. Rising balances mean cash is sitting idle at the Fed rather than chasing assets. Example: it went from near zero in early 2021 to over $2 trillion by late 2022.

Valuation and Quality

P/E ratio: Share price divided by earnings per share. Example: a $100 stock earning $5 a share trades at 20 times earnings.

Forward P/E: Price divided by next year’s expected earnings. Example: Microsoft at 19 times forward earnings in his margin-of-safety video.

PEG ratio: P/E divided by the earnings growth rate. Around 1 is “fair”; 2–3 is expensive. Example: P/E 25 with 25% growth = PEG 1.

Margin of safety: Benjamin Graham’s term for the gap between what you pay and what something is worth. Example: if you bought Google at $50, it would need to trade at about 2 times earnings to get back to your cost.

Free cash flow (FCF): Operating cash flow minus capital spending: the cash a business can hand to owners. Example: the money that funds buybacks and dividends.

Owner earnings: Buffett’s version: net income plus depreciation minus maintenance capital spending only. Example: Waste Management’s owner earnings grew 15–20% while its stock rose 4.5%.

ROIC / CROIC: Return on invested capital; the cash version divides free cash flow by the capital invested. Example: invest $6.5 million, receive $2.4 million a year = ~37% CROIC.

Pricing power: The ability to raise prices without losing customers. His filter: “Can they raise prices without losing a single customer?” Example: Visa, ASML, Microsoft Office.

Moat: A durable competitive advantage. Example: ASML is the only maker of EUV lithography machines.

Beta / weighted beta: How much a stock (or a whole portfolio) moves relative to the market. Example: Walmart’s beta of 0.59 means it moves about 59% as much as the market.

Coiled spring: His term for a stock whose price is flat while its cash flow grows, building up pressure for a catch-up move. Example: Microsoft 2000–2013.

Accounts, Tax and Estate

Taxable brokerage account: A normal investment account with no tax wrapper. Paradoxically, the key BBD account. Example: an Interactive Brokers individual account.

Step-up in basis: At death, the cost basis of inherited assets resets to market value (IRC §1014), erasing the unrealized gain for income-tax purposes. Example: stock bought for $100,000 worth $5 million at death; heirs can sell for $5 million and owe no capital gains tax.

Capital gains tax (CGT): Tax on the profit when you sell. Long-term federal rates are 0/15/20%, plus 3.8% net investment income tax at higher incomes, plus state. Example: selling $50,000 of stock with a $10,000 basis at 23.8% costs $9,520.

Estate tax: Federal tax on estates above the exemption, $15 million per person from 2026 under the 2025 tax law, at a 40% top rate. Example: a married couple can pass $30 million with no federal estate tax.

1031 exchange: Selling investment real estate and rolling the proceeds into “like-kind” real estate to defer capital gains tax. Example: rentals sold into a Delaware Statutory Trust.

Delaware Statutory Trust (DST): A trust that owns institutional real estate in fractional interests, eligible for 1031 exchanges. Example: a share of an Amazon distribution center.

Roth conversion ladder: Moving money from a traditional 401(k)/IRA into a Roth in low-income years, paying tax at low brackets. Example: Renee converting over 15 years after she stops working.

72(t): The tax-code section imposing a 10% penalty on retirement-account withdrawals before age 59½ (with exceptions). Example: a 45-year-old withdrawing $100,000 pays the penalty plus income tax.

If You Only Remember 10 Things

  1. Never sell an appreciating asset to buy a liability. Borrow against it instead, and let the asset keep compounding.
  2. BBD is wealth extraction, not wealth creation. It only works if the assets grow faster than the loan and inflation combined; clever borrowing cannot rescue a weak portfolio.
  3. Measure against the money supply, not CPI. CPI is the cost of survival; M2 (about 6–7% a year) is the cost of the assets you actually want to own.
  4. The S&P 500’s 10% can be an illusion. After money-supply growth and tax, his math leaves 0.65%, which is why he borrows against growth assets like the Nasdaq-100 instead.
  5. A margin call is a management failure, not a market failure. At 20% loan-to-value, a portfolio must fall about 73% before it is called.
  6. Volatility is the price of admission. Risk is permanent loss of purchasing power, and “risk is a behavior, not a tool.”
  7. Build a barbell. Roughly 70% growth engine, 30% defensive ballast plus T-bills, so the portfolio can both outrun the debt and survive a crash.
  8. Cheap debt shrinks on its own. Inflation erodes a loan the same way it erodes cash, so rushing to repay debt cheaper than inflation is, in his words, “mathematical suicide.”
  9. Liquidity drives markets. Borrow in expansion; pay down with outside cash (never by selling) when the Fed starts hiking.
  10. The step-up in basis is the finish line. Assets held until death pass to heirs with the gain erased, so the family can repay the loan tax-free or keep running the strategy.
Part I
The Worldview: Why the Strategy Exists

Before any loan or portfolio, he builds a picture of how money works. Everything else depends on it.

Chapter 1: The Goal — Protect Purchasing Power

Videos: The Compound Interest Lie; CPAs Give Terrible Advice; Repaying the Loan, Part 2

Almost every personal finance book starts with a goal like “retire comfortably” or “reach a number.” BigBankTheory starts somewhere else. His goal is to protect purchasing power: to make sure that the share of the world’s scarce assets you can command goes up over time, not merely the number of dollars in your account. The distinction sounds academic until you notice what it rules out. A savings account that pays 4% while the money supply grows 7% has, by his definition, made you poorer. A house that doubled in price over a decade in which the money supply doubled has done nothing. Only assets that outrun the growth of money itself count as wealth building.

His reasoning runs in a straight line. Governments with large debts cannot raise taxes enough or cut spending enough to repay them, so they print money and let it quietly erode the debt. That erosion is inflation, and it falls on anyone who holds currency: wage earners whose raises lag, savers whose cash sits in a bank, and bondholders whose coupons are fixed. The people it does not fall on are owners of assets that can reprice: companies with pricing power, scarce real estate, gold. So the first decision in his framework is not which stock to buy. It is choosing which side of the debasement you want to be on.

“The rules of the game are: get cheap debt, let inflation erode it, and buy assets that go up with inflation.” — BigBankTheory

That sentence is the whole strategy compressed. Notice that it has three parts, and each one becomes a section of this guide. Buy assets that go up with inflation is the asset-selection problem, and it is where he spends most of his energy. Get cheap debt is the borrowing problem: margin loans, Lombard loans, securities-backed lines, and the discipline to keep them small. Let inflation erode it is the patience problem: holding for decades without selling, so the debt shrinks relative to the assets instead of the other way around. Most people who hear about Buy Borrow Die fixate on the middle part, the borrowing. He insists the first part does almost all the work.

Survivor vs Capitalist

He frames the choice with a pair of labels he uses in nearly every video. A survivor measures wealth in bread, milk, eggs and iPads, and so tracks the Consumer Price Index. A capitalist measures wealth in land, prime property, gold and ownership of great businesses, and so tracks the money supply. “If your goal is to be rich in bread, milk and eggs, track CPI,” he says. “We’re capitalists.” The practical point underneath the rhetoric is that the things wealthy people compete for (a house in a good school district, a stake in a monopoly, a waterfront lot) have risen much faster than the grocery basket. If you benchmark yourself against the grocery basket, you can feel rich while falling behind in the auctions that matter.

This is why he dismisses the familiar comfort that the stock market “beats inflation.” It beats CPI, certainly. Whether it beats the growth of money itself is a much harder question, and Chapter 5 shows that on his math the broad index barely does. The answer to that question determines everything downstream: whether borrowing against a portfolio makes you richer or slowly poorer, whether an index fund is enough, and whether a paid-off mortgage is prudence or a mistake.

Extraction, Not Creation

One of his most important clarifications comes almost in passing: “Buy Borrow Die is for wealth extraction, not wealth creation.” The strategy does not make a portfolio grow. It is a method for getting spending money out of a portfolio without selling it, and therefore without interrupting its compounding or triggering tax. The growth has to come from somewhere else: from good asset selection and from time. That is why he spends so many videos on valuation, pricing power and the Nasdaq-100, and comparatively few on loan mechanics. If the asset underneath returns 4%, borrowing at 5% against it is not a strategy. It is a slow leak.

He is blunt about who therefore cannot use it. Someone with $50,000 is not extracting anything; the job at that stage is to “build the engine.” Someone with a $2 million portfolio of dividend stocks and REITs has wealth but not growth, and borrowing against it leads to what he calls a silent margin call, the slow rise in loan-to-value that Chapter 19 describes. Only when a sizable portfolio is compounding faster than the loan rate plus money-supply growth does the strategy turn from a trap into a tool.

His framework in one box

Goal: grow purchasing power, measured against the money supply.

Engine: assets with pricing power that compound above ~15% (growth ETFs such as the Nasdaq-100, or monopoly businesses), balanced with defensive ballast.

Extraction: borrow roughly 20% of the portfolio’s value instead of selling.

Time: hold until death, when the step-up in basis erases the embedded gain.

Why the Framing Matters

It would be easy to treat this chapter as scene-setting and move on to the loan mechanics. That would be a mistake, because the purchasing-power framing is where his strategy departs most sharply from mainstream advice, and where you should decide early whether you agree with him. If you believe CPI is the right yardstick, the S&P 500 returns roughly 7% a year in real terms, borrowing against it at 5% leaves a thin positive spread, and much of his argument against index funds collapses. If you believe M2 is the right yardstick, the same index returns something closer to 3–4% real, borrowing at 5% against it destroys wealth, and his insistence on higher-growth assets becomes logically necessary.

The honest answer, developed in Chapter 4, is that the truth sits between the two. Money supply growth overstates debasement, because part of it simply funds a larger real economy. CPI understates the rise in the price of scarce assets, because it was built to measure living costs, not the cost of joining the owning class. A careful reader can take his core insight (the benchmark for an asset owner should be higher than CPI) without accepting that it must be the full M2 growth rate. Keep that in mind as the chapters progress, because it determines how much of his framework you actually need to adopt.

The Other Side

Purchasing power is relative. Beating M2 means gaining ground on other asset holders, which is a positional goal. Most people’s real needs (housing, food, health care, education) track something between CPI and asset prices.

The goal raises the stakes. Demanding 15% a year pushes you toward concentrated growth assets, which have had multi-year drawdowns of 50–80%. A higher target is also a riskier target.

What survives the critique: for someone who owns assets and intends to borrow against them, the hurdle is clearly the loan rate plus some measure of inflation, and CPI is the most generous possible choice of that measure.

Chapter 2: The US Debt Machine

Videos: Why the USA Debt “Problem” Doesn’t Worry Me; Repaying the Loan, Part 2; What a BBD Portfolio Looks Like

If the goal is to protect purchasing power, the next question is what threatens it. His answer is the sovereign debt crisis, which he treats not as a looming catastrophe but as a permanent condition to be exploited. At the time of his US debt video the federal debt was about $34 trillion; in later videos he rounds it to $40 trillion. He puts it in human terms: $34 trillion counted in seconds would take more than a million years. His point is not that the number is frightening. It is that a number this large will never be repaid, and once you accept that, the government’s behavior becomes predictable.

The Rollover

He explains the mechanics with himself as the example. Suppose he owns a two-year Treasury note that is about to mature. The government owes him his money back, but it has no spare cash, because it runs a deficit every year. So it issues a new two-year note, sells it to you, and uses your money to repay him. Two years later your note matures, and the government repeats the trick with someone else. “It’s like a giant Ponzi scheme,” he says, “in an informal way.” The debt is never paid down; it is rolled over, and because the deficit adds to it every year, it grows.

The numbers he cites make the deficit concrete. Federal revenue runs a little under $5 trillion a year; spending runs close to $7 trillion. The gap of roughly $2 trillion has to be borrowed, on top of rolling over every maturing bond. None of the conventional exits is realistic in his view. Austerity fails because the big items (Social Security, Medicare, defense, interest) are politically untouchable. Tax increases large enough to close the gap are “political suicide.” That leaves two paths, and he argues the government will use both.

The Only Two Exits: Inflation and Growth

The first exit is inflation. If prices and incomes double while the debt stays fixed in nominal terms, the debt’s real burden halves. Inflation is “bad for people with savings but good for people with debt,” and the government is the largest debtor on earth. The second exit is growth. If the economy grows fast enough, a fixed debt shrinks as a share of GDP even without inflation. This is why he calls Nvidia “a national treasure” and frames the AI race as the answer to the debt problem: a productivity boom from AI and robotics would make $34 trillion look small next to a much larger economy.

Here is the part that matters for an investor. Both exits push stock prices up. Inflation lifts the nominal value of companies that can raise prices. Growth lifts their real earnings. So the debt, which frightens most people, is in his framing the tailwind behind the whole strategy. You cannot vote your way out of it or save your way out of it. You can only choose to be on the asset side of the ledger when it resolves. “You cannot outwork a sovereign debt crisis,” he says in another video. “You have to structure an architecture that does it for you.”

Why It Doesn’t Break: Confidence

Why can the US do this when other countries cannot? Because it borrows in its own currency, and that currency is the world’s reserve. A country that owes dollars it cannot print (Argentina, Greece) can be forced into default. The US can always create the dollars to repay, so an involuntary default is impossible. The only way it defaults is by choice, if Congress refuses to raise the debt ceiling, which he calls “political theater”: a performance of fiscal responsibility that always ends with the ceiling being raised.

What the system truly runs on, he says, is confidence. The world must keep wanting to buy Treasuries. This explains a subtlety in how officials talk. They must always say they will fix the debt, through spending discipline, taxes or tariffs, because if investors believed the real plan was to inflate it away, nobody would buy a 10- or 30-year bond. “The narrative control is so strong,” he says, because the narrative is load-bearing. And there is no alternative for global capital to flee to: China has capital controls and a trust deficit, Europe is fragmented, and the euro will not replace the dollar short of a catastrophe.

The Historical Proof: Weimar Germany

To show what happens at the extreme, he tells the story of Weimar Germany. Germany financed the First World War largely with debt, planning to repay it from the spoils of victory. It lost. Saddled with war debt and reparations, it printed money. In January 1923 a loaf of bread in Berlin cost about 250 marks. By November 1923 it cost about 200 billion marks. Workers were paid twice a day so they could spend their wages at lunch before prices rose again in the afternoon. People pushed wheelbarrows of banknotes to buy groceries; children built towers out of bundles of cash. A lifetime of savings, held in marks, became worthless in a matter of months.

The hero of his version of the story is Hugo Stinnes, the industrialist who understood what was happening and did the opposite of the savers. He borrowed enormous sums in marks and bought real things: coal mines, steel works, shipping lines, newspapers, hotels. By the end of the inflation he controlled well over a thousand companies and was called the “Inflation King.” His debts, denominated in marks, evaporated. His assets, being real, kept their value. The same inflation that ruined a pensioner let a homeowner pay off a mortgage with less than a day’s wages.

“The person that survives hyperinflation is someone who owns assets.” — BigBankTheory, on Hugo Stinnes

He is careful to say he does not expect hyperinflation in the United States. The lesson is about direction, not magnitude. What happened to Germany in eighteen months happens to dollar savers over decades, “so slowly that people don’t notice it.” People only ask why everything feels so expensive and why they always feel broke, without connecting it to the debt. He calls that feeling “a treadmill you cannot get off unless you understand how to defeat the enemy,” and the enemy is the money supply.

The Other Side

“Can’t default” does not mean “no risk.” The risk moves from default to inflation, currency weakness and rising interest costs. Net interest on the federal debt passed defense spending in 2024, and Moody’s removed the last AAA rating from the US in May 2025.

Confidence can crack. The UK gilt crisis of 2022, when long-bond yields spiked in days after an unfunded budget, shows how fast a reserve-quality market can wobble. Ray Dalio’s Big Debt Crises argues that debt cycles end in a “beautiful deleveraging” only when printing is balanced against austerity and restructuring. Too much printing produces the inflationary version.

The Weimar framing is selective. Stinnes was an insider with access to credit that ordinary Germans lacked, and his empire collapsed within two years of his death in 1924, when stabilization came and his debts could no longer be inflated away. Leverage that works during inflation can be ruinous during a sudden return to tight money.

Chapter 3: The Bond Market

Videos: Why the USA Debt “Problem” Doesn’t Worry Me; When Rates Rise (supplementary)

Most retail investors ignore bonds as boring. He thinks that is a mistake, not because bonds belong at the center of a BBD portfolio, but because the bond market sets the price of everything else. By his figures the US bond market is about $55 trillion against a US stock market of about $49 trillion. “The bond market is where the real money is made,” he says. The Treasury yield is the risk-free rate against which every stock, loan and property is priced. When it moves, your margin rate moves, your stocks reprice, and the case for borrowing changes.

Price and Yield Move Opposite

The core mechanic is simple once seen. A bond pays a fixed coupon. Suppose you buy a 10-year Treasury yielding 4.5%. A year later new 10-year Treasuries yield only 3%. Anyone who wants a 4.5% coupon now has to buy yours, and they will pay more than you did to get it. Your bond has risen in price. If instead new Treasuries yield 6%, nobody wants your 4.5% bond at the original price, and it falls. When rates fall, bond prices rise; when rates rise, bond prices fall. The longer the bond, the bigger the move, because the fixed coupon is locked in for longer.

This is why, uniquely, he says bonds are something you should buy low and sell high. He never sells stocks, but a bond is a trade on the path of interest rates. If you can see rate cuts coming, and he argues they are often visible 18 to 24 months in advance, you can buy long bonds before the cuts and sell them after. With stocks, you must study earnings and moats. With Treasuries, the question reduces to one thing: are yields about to go up or down?

How Institutions Make Money: Repo Leverage

He describes the institutional version of this trade. A Treasury is the only asset that can be financed at extreme leverage in the repo market, where institutions borrow overnight cash against Treasury collateral. In his example, an institution with $10 million of capital controls $100 million of 10-year Treasuries, borrowing the other $90 million at around 2% while the bonds yield 4.5%. That spread is a carry trade: they earn the difference between what the bonds pay and what the borrowing costs. Then, if the Fed cuts, the bonds rise in price, and the gain on $100 million of bonds lands on only $10 million of equity. He calculates about 165% on the capital. “You and I can’t do this,” he adds.

The carry trade, simplified Equity: $10M Borrowed (repo): $90M at 2% Treasuries held: $100M at 4.5% Carry per year: $4.5M − $1.8M = $2.7M = 27% on equity, before any price move If yields fall 1%: bond price +~8% (10-yr duration) = +$8M on $10M equity

Leverage magnifies both the carry and the price change. It also magnifies losses: a 1% rise in yields would erase most of the equity.

The Fed, Tariffs and the Last Man Standing

In mid-2025 he made a specific macro argument. With the Fed at 4.33% while the European Central Bank had cut to around 2% and Japan stayed low, the US was “the last man standing” with high rates. Higher US yields pull global capital into Treasuries, strengthening the dollar and funding the deficit. Europe, he argued, cannot hold rates high because its economies are too weak. The US can. He paired this with a warning about tariffs, which cause cost-push inflation: if a shop imports its T-shirts from China and the tariff raises its cost, it raises the price to the customer. As long as tariffs threaten inflation, the Fed cannot cut, whatever the President demands.

When Rates Rise: Nothing Is Safe

The supplementary video on rising rates adds the uncomfortable part. During an actual hiking cycle there is no safe asset except cash. Bonds lose value as yields rise. Stocks fall because the risk-free rate is rising: why own a volatile stock when a money-market fund pays 5 to 6% for doing nothing? Pension funds and institutions park their liquidity in T-bills and wait. Meanwhile the margin loan gets more expensive. “Two horrible things happening at the same time,” he says, and it is the moment most borrowers give up on the strategy. Chapter 23 returns to what he does about it.

The practical lesson for a BBD investor is that bonds have two jobs in his portfolios. Short-term T-bills are a cash buffer: they barely move in price, pay the going rate, and in a high-tax state they are exempt from state income tax. Longer Treasuries, like the 30-year bond in his FIRE comparison, provide guaranteed income and ballast, but they carry price risk and are eroded by inflation if held to maturity. “You can’t hold a 10-year Treasury at 4.5% for the full ten years,” he warns, if inflation is running hot, “because you probably have less money than when you invested.”

The Other Side

The 165% example is a best case. Real repo haircuts on Treasuries are often around 2%, allowing far more than 10× leverage, which is exactly why the hedge-fund “basis trade” blew up in March 2020 and required Fed intervention. Leverage that turns a 1% rate move into a 165% gain turns the opposite move into a wipeout.

Rate cuts are not reliably predictable. In early 2023 markets priced several cuts that did not arrive until September 2024. Long bonds bought on the expectation lost money in the meantime. 2022 was the worst year for US bonds in modern history, with long Treasuries falling more than 30%.

What holds up: the price-yield relationship, the role of T-bills as a near-cash buffer, and the insight that the Treasury rate drives the cost of every other asset and loan.

Chapter 4: The M2 Hurdle Rate

Videos: When Can You Borrow for Lifestyle; The Compound Interest Lie; CPAs Give Terrible Advice; Stop Using the S&P 500

This is the single most important idea in his framework, and the one he repeats more than any other: measure your returns against the growth of the money supply, not against consumer prices. He calls the M2 growth rate the hurdle rate, the line an asset must clear before it counts as creating wealth. By his figures M2 has grown about 6–7% a year on average. In 1959 there were about $286 billion in the US money supply. Today there are about $22 trillion, a roughly 78-fold increase. Consumer prices rose only about 11-fold over the same period.

What M2 Actually Is

The Federal Reserve publishes several measures of money. M1 is the narrowest: physical currency and checking deposits. M2 adds savings accounts, money-market deposit accounts, retail money-market funds and small time deposits. It is roughly “money people can spend or move quickly.” It grows when banks make loans (a new mortgage creates a new deposit), when the government runs deficits that get financed through the banking system, and when the Fed buys assets through quantitative easing. It is freely available from the Federal Reserve’s FRED database, which he points viewers to so they can verify his numbers.

Why use this instead of CPI? His argument has three parts. First, CPI is a lagging indicator: prices react after money is created, while M2 is the source. Second, CPI measures the wrong basket for an investor: it tracks survival costs, not the cost of scarce assets. Third, new money lands on assets first: when money is created, it tends to flow into financial assets and real estate before it reaches wages and grocery prices, so asset prices track money growth more closely than consumer prices do. “When there’s more money created, assets go up,” he says. “This is the cost capitalists track.”

The Dollar Halves Every Ten Years

At 7% a year, money doubles roughly every ten years (the rule of 72: 72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10). He turns that around: measured against the assets that absorb the new money, the dollar loses about half its value every decade. So to keep $1 million of purchasing power over ten years, you need about $1.97 million at the end of it. In his Part 2 repayment video he uses a gentler version and says $1 million needs to become about $1.7 million. Either way, the point is that a portfolio that merely doubles in a decade has stood still, and a portfolio that grows 50% has gone backward.

1959 vs today: his favorite comparison

In 1959, $100,000 could buy about 45 new cars, or about 26 years of a worker’s wages, or about 5.2 single-family homes.

M2 1959: ~$286 billion M2 today: ~$22 trillion (×78) CPI: ×~11 over the same period

His conclusion: “Inflation is simpler than ‘too much money chasing too few goods.’ It is just too much money.” If the money supply had stayed at $286 billion, asset prices would be roughly fixed, because there would be no new money to bid them up.

The Three Questions to Ask Any Advisor

In his video attacking CPAs and tax lawyers who teach BBD, he proposes three questions. If an advisor gets them wrong, “fire them immediately.” The first is what is inflation? The wrong answer is “the prices of eggs, bread and iPhones going up.” The right answer, in his framework, is expansion of the money supply. The second and third questions (why does a stock go up, and why do assets go up at all) are covered in Chapter 7, but they rest on this first answer. If you think inflation is 3%, you will think a 7% return is excellent. If you think it is 7%, you will think 7% is standing still.

It Applies to Everything

He applies the hurdle to every asset. Real estate: a house that rose 10% in a year of 10% money growth “just kept up with the money supply.” Only scarce prime locations beat it. Bonds: a 4.5% Treasury loses to 7% money growth by definition. Cash: guaranteed to lose. Dividend stocks: most fail once taxes are included. Businesses: a company whose free cash flow per share grows faster than M2 is “a winning asset”; one that grows slower is slowly liquidating its owners. The same measuring stick applied everywhere is what gives his framework its internal consistency.

Why this matters for borrowing

If you borrow at 5% against an asset, the asset must beat both the loan rate and the erosion of the dollar. He sets the combined hurdle at roughly 7% money growth plus the loan cost, and concludes the portfolio must compound around 15%. If you use CPI instead, the hurdle drops to about 8%, and a plain index fund appears to clear it. The choice of benchmark decides whether BBD works on an S&P 500 portfolio.

The Other Side: What Economists Say

M2 growth is not all debasement. The money supply has to grow to support a bigger economy. US real GDP grew roughly 7-fold since 1959. Divide M2’s 78-fold increase by 7 and you get about 11, almost exactly the rise in CPI. That is the economist’s answer: most “extra” money growth financed more output, not higher prices.

Velocity changes. The equation of exchange (MV = PQ) says money times its velocity equals prices times output. Velocity of M2 fell sharply after 2008 and again in 2020, so money growth did not translate one-for-one into anything.

M2 can shrink. In 2022–23 M2 fell by about $1 trillion, the first sustained decline since the 1930s. A benchmark that sometimes goes negative is awkward as a fixed hurdle.

What survives: asset prices have clearly outpaced CPI for decades, partly because falling interest rates and money growth inflated valuations. A reasonable compromise hurdle for an asset owner is somewhere between CPI (~3%) and M2 (~7%), for example M2 growth minus real GDP growth plus an allowance for asset scarcity, roughly 4–5%.

Chapter 5: The Illusion of 10.4% and the 0.65% Math

Videos: The Compound Interest Lie; Stop Using the S&P 500 for BBD

Every financial planner quotes some version of the same number: the S&P 500 has returned about 10% a year. He uses 10.4%, the figure often cited since the index’s modern launch in 1957. Then the planner subtracts inflation of about 3% and tells you your real return is about 7%, so $1,000 a month for thirty years makes you a multimillionaire. He calls this the illusion of 10.4%, and he devotes two full videos to it. The illusion is not that the 10.4% is false. It is that it has been deflated by the wrong number, and that the taxes have been left out.

Step One: 10.4% Minus M2 = 3.66%

Replace CPI with the money supply and the picture changes. M2 has grown about 6.74% a year since 1959. Subtract that from 10.4% and the true yield, what he calls the real return after money printing, is about 3.66%. He adds that this is probably generous, because it ignores Eurodollars, the vast pool of dollars held in banks outside the US that does not show up in M2. If those were counted, the debasement rate would be higher still and the true yield lower.

Step Two: Add Tax and It Falls to 0.65%

In the companion video he runs a forward-looking version: 9% nominal for the index over the next ten years, 7% money-supply growth. That leaves 2% before tax. Then he says the net after tax is “only a measly 0.65%.” He does not show the working on screen, and at first glance it looks wrong: 2% minus tax cannot be 0.65% unless the tax rate is enormous. The math works once you see the trick, and the trick is the most important thing in the chapter. The tax is charged on the nominal gain, not on the real gain.

Reconstructing the 0.65% Nominal return: 9.00% Long-term CGT (15%) on 9%: −1.35% After-tax nominal: 7.65% Money supply growth: −7.00% True after-tax return: 0.65%

The government taxes the whole 9%, including the 7% that merely kept pace with money printing. So the effective tax on your real gain of 2% is 1.35 ÷ 2 = 67.5%, not 15%.

This is a real and underappreciated feature of every tax system that taxes nominal gains. Inflation raises your effective tax rate on real returns, sometimes above 100%, meaning you can pay tax on a gain that did not happen in purchasing-power terms. It is also a large part of why he prizes the step-up in basis and the strategy of never selling. An unrealized gain is never taxed on its inflationary component, because it is never taxed at all. Deferring the sale is valuable; eliminating it at death is more valuable still.

“You’re a Saver, Not an Investor”

His conclusion is provocative on purpose. “If you are investing into the S&P 500, the reality is you are a saver.” An investor, in his vocabulary, understands the businesses they own, reads cash flows and runs a concentrated portfolio. Someone who buys the whole market and never looks is storing value, which is fine, but should not expect it to create real wealth. He does not tell index holders to sell; in the rate-rise video he explicitly says an existing S&P 500 holding is “still pristine collateral for a lender” and should be kept while new money tilts toward growth. What he rejects is borrowing against it, because borrowing at 5% against a 3.66% true yield is a guaranteed loss. “Hope is not a strategy,” he says.

The Market as Pacifier

He adds a psychological twist. If money is being created at 7% a year and much of it flows into financial assets, then index funds will rise in nominal terms almost regardless of whether the underlying economy is creating real wealth. People watch their 401(k) balances go up and feel wealthy. He calls the stock market “a pacifier”: it soothes people who are, in real terms, running in place. Argentina is his extreme illustration, where the stock market rose more than 400,000% in nominal terms between 1980 and 1991 while the currency was destroyed. Nobody in Argentina thought they were rich. In the US the same effect is slower and so invisible.

The Other Side

The 9% assumption is his, not the record. Using 10.4% nominal and a more defensible debasement rate of ~4–5% (see Chapter 4), the after-tax real return is roughly 3–4%, not 0.65%.

Dividends are taxed differently. Roughly 2–4 points of the historical S&P return came from dividends, taxed each year (unless in a tax-advantaged account), while the price gain is taxed only when sold, and never if held to death. A buy-and-hold index investor pays far less than 15% of the nominal return in tax each year.

In tax-advantaged accounts there is no tax. For money in a Roth IRA, the 0.65% becomes 2%. That is a different, milder argument than “the S&P is liquidating you.”

What survives: the insight that tax on nominal gains eats a much larger share of real gains than the headline rate suggests, and that borrowing at 5% against an asset with a thin real return is a poor trade.

Chapter 6: Asset Inflation, Gold and the Elastic Band

Videos: The Compound Interest Lie; Stop Using the S&P 500; Inside a Live BBD Portfolio

If money growth is the hurdle, then asset prices should roughly track money growth over long periods. He says they do. “Look at the money supply chart compared to the S&P 500 chart,” he says. “What a coincidence. They both go up and to the right.” This is his answer to a question financial advisors usually cannot answer: why does the S&P 500 have a 100% recovery rate? Advisors say companies keep making money or the economy always recovers. He says the index recovers because the money supply keeps growing, and new money has to go somewhere.

The Evidence He Offers

His evidence is partly data and partly the lived experience of the last few years. During COVID the money supply grew about 25% in a single year, the fastest since the Second World War. The result was a boom in almost every asset: houses selling for 50% over asking, used cars appreciating, stocks at record highs, crypto and meme stocks exploding. “The civilian mind thinks it’s just supply and demand,” he says. “They don’t think it’s because the money supply has been increased by 25%.” For him, 2020–21 was a natural experiment in what new money does to asset prices, compressed into eighteen months.

His strongest data point is gold. In the S&P video he argues with an AI chatbot that gave the standard economist’s reply: M2 rose about 78-fold since 1959 while CPI rose about 11-fold, and the difference was absorbed by real growth, faster turnover and dollars held abroad. His rebuttal is to ask where the rest went, and to point at gold. His on-camera version (“gold went up exactly 67%”) is garbled, but the underlying numbers make his case better than he did. Gold was $35 an ounce in 1959. It has since traded well above $3,000, roughly a 100-fold rise, which tracks M2’s 78-fold rise far more closely than it tracks CPI’s 11-fold rise.

Three yardsticks since 1959 (approximate) Consumer prices (CPI): ×11 Money supply (M2): ×78 Gold ($35 → $3,000+): ×90–110 S&P 500 price (~57 → ~6,500): ×~115

The S&P 500 price index roughly matched gold and modestly beat M2; with dividends reinvested it did much better. Consumer prices lagged all of them by a wide margin. This is the gap he is pointing at: the cost of scarce things has risen about seven to ten times faster than the cost of living.

The Elastic Band

If asset prices track money over long periods, what happens when they get ahead of it? His image is an elastic band. The money supply is one end; the S&P 500 is the other. In a boom, valuations stretch away from the money supply. The band cannot stretch forever; eventually it snaps back and pulls prices toward reality. At the time of the video the S&P 500 was up about 17–18% over twelve months while M2 grew about 3.4%. “This looks like the S&P 500 is vastly outperforming inflation,” he says, “but whenever the S&P outperforms the money supply, it normally means the market is overvalued.”

When the line is high, stocks are expensive relative to the money in the system; when it is low, they are cheap. You can see the band stretch in the late 1990s and snap in 2000–2002, compress to its lowest point in the early 1980s and again in 2009, and stretch once more in 2021 and today.

When Outperformance Is Legitimate

He does allow one exception. “There will be years during productivity booms where M2 can grow at 7% and the market rallies at 30%.” A genuine surge in productivity, like electrification, the internet or, he argues, AI, can justify prices growing faster than money. But he warns that when the rally is driven by leverage and multiple expansion rather than earnings, it is “growing into bubble territory.” The practical rule he draws is the one from the margin of safety video: watch the valuation of what you buy, and if the index’s growth-adjusted valuation gets extreme, slow your buying, without ever selling.

The Other Side

The ratio drifts. Falling interest rates from 1981 to 2021 justified higher valuations relative to money, so part of the recent “stretch” may be a new level rather than a band waiting to snap.

Correlation is not mechanism. Both M2 and stock prices rise with nominal GDP, so they would go “up and to the right” together even if money did not directly flow into stocks.

Gold is volatile. From 1980 to 2001 gold fell about 70% in nominal terms while M2 doubled. Its long-run tracking of M2 hides decades-long divergences.

What survives: money growth and asset prices are linked over long horizons, 2020–21 is a vivid case study, and extreme gaps between asset prices and underlying money or earnings have usually closed painfully.

Chapter 7: Why Stocks Actually Go Up

Videos: CPAs Give Terrible Advice; When Can You Borrow for Lifestyle; Stop Using the S&P 500

The second and third of his “fire your advisor” questions are why does a stock go up? and why do assets go up at all? The common answers (good earnings, a great CEO, good news, supply and demand) he dismisses as noise. His answer has two engines, and understanding both explains why he is willing to borrow against some assets and not others.

Engine One: Free Cash Flow per Share

The first engine is free cash flow per share. A company that generates more cash every year, after paying for the investment needed to keep running, has more money to hand back to owners through buybacks and dividends. That is the fundamental value to a shareholder. “If free cash flow per share is going up year over year faster than the money supply, that is a winning asset.” Note the “per share”: a company can grow total cash flow while diluting shareholders by issuing stock. Buybacks work the other way, shrinking the share count so each remaining share owns more of the cash. He likes buybacks precisely because they raise the per-share figure without triggering a tax bill for holders.

Engine Two: Liquidity and Multiple Expansion

The second engine is liquidity. When excess money floods the system, investors buy stocks, and the price paid for each dollar of earnings rises. “If the P/E is 20, suddenly it goes to 60 because everyone’s buying the stock. The company’s doing the same thing it did yesterday.” This is multiple expansion, and it can dominate returns for years. But it is borrowed from the future: a stock bought at 60 times earnings has to grow into that price, or fall back toward it. “If we’re buying at such a high valuation, eventually reality will pull it back down.” This is why entry valuation matters to him even though he never sells.

Decomposing a stock’s return Total return ≈ earnings growth + change in P/E multiple + dividend yield − net share dilution

Example: earnings grow 15%, P/E is unchanged, yield 0.7%, buybacks reduce the share count 2%: return ≈ 15 + 0 + 0.7 + 2 = 17.7%. If the P/E also expands from 20 to 25 over the year, add about 25%. If it contracts from 25 to 20, subtract 20%. Over short periods, the multiple term dominates; over decades, earnings growth does.

Who Gets the Money First

His answer to the third question (why do assets go up at all) is simply that more dollars are created. But he adds a refinement that economists call the Cantillon effect, after the 18th-century Irish-French economist Richard Cantillon, who noticed that new money benefits whoever receives it first, before it spreads and raises prices. In his words: “We want the assets that grab that liquidity first. That’s the companies with pricing power. They soak up the liquidity first because they can raise prices.” A monopoly that can pass on price increases immediately captures new money at the front of the queue. A business with no pricing power sees its costs rise before its revenue.

This also explains why he says “elephants can gallop”, rejecting the old saw that large companies cannot grow fast. Liquidity flows to the biggest, most liquid, most trusted assets first. Index funds buy by market capitalization, so every new dollar into an index buys more of the largest names. Institutional money needs size. In his telling, the Mag 7 are not just good businesses; they are the reservoirs into which new money pools.

Where Compounding Comes From

Put the two engines together and you get his answer to where compound growth actually comes from. “It comes from the money supply for a start. New money is always entering the system. The more that grows, the more these companies grow, because they soak it up like a sponge. Combine that with buybacks.” Nominal economic growth (real growth plus inflation) flows into revenue; pricing power converts it into profit; buybacks concentrate it per share. A company doing all three compounds at the rate of the money supply plus its own real growth plus its buyback yield, which is how the best companies clear a 15% hurdle.

The Other Side

Earnings still matter most over decades. Studies decomposing US stock returns since 1926 find that dividends and real earnings growth explain most of the long-run return; multiple expansion contributes only about 1 point a year, much of it since 1980 as interest rates fell.

Buybacks are not free value. They add value only when the shares are bought below intrinsic value. Companies that bought back heavily in 2007 at high prices and then issued stock in 2009 at low ones destroyed value. Since 2023 buybacks also carry a 1% federal excise tax.

What survives: the two-engine model (cash flow plus valuation) is exactly how professional analysts decompose returns, and his emphasis on per-share cash flow and pricing power is sound.

Chapter 8: The 97% Problem — Bessembinder and the Gold Guillotine

Video: 97% of the Stock Market Is a Mathematical Waste of Time

This is his most ambitious argument. It begins with a famous 2018 paper by the finance professor Hendrik Bessembinder, titled “Do Stocks Outperform Treasury Bills?” Bessembinder studied every US common stock from 1926 to 2016, nearly 26,000 companies. His finding stunned the profession: the best-performing 4% of stocks accounted for all of the net wealth the US stock market created above Treasury bills. The other 96%, taken together, merely matched T-bills. More than half of all individual stocks delivered lifetime returns below T-bills.

Two Readings of the Same Study

Mainstream finance draws the obvious conclusion: since you cannot know in advance which 4% will be the winners, you should own all of them through an index fund. Picking stocks is looking for a needle in a haystack. He draws the opposite conclusion. “If 4% of companies generate all the returns, they’re highly visible and very obvious.” The winners are the monopolies with inelastic pricing power: the biggest, most profitable, most dominant companies on earth. They are not hidden; they are the Mag 7, TSMC, ASML. Capital, he argues, follows a power law, “like a law of physics,” flowing to the handful of companies that form the backbone of global infrastructure.

A Harsher Test: Money Supply and Gold

Then he raises the bar. Bessembinder measured stocks against T-bills. He measures entire national stock markets over roughly twenty years against two tougher benchmarks: each country’s own money supply, and gold, which he calls “the true yardstick for measuring the return of an investment.” If a market cannot beat gold or its own money supply, “it’s not worth an investment, and it certainly does not qualify for Buy Borrow Die.”

Why Most Markets Fail

His explanation of why most national markets fail the test is one of his more original points. A stock market can only create real wealth for a country if its public companies sell something the rest of the world wants. A country that mostly passes its own currency around among its own citizens (domestic banks, retailers, builders, telecoms) is running “a circular loop.” New money inflates its prices, but nothing new comes in. Tourism, he adds, brings money into a country’s economy but not into its public markets. The markets that pass are the ones with a company that pulls in global demand: Taiwan has TSMC, which makes most of the world’s advanced chips; Denmark had Novo Nordisk and its GLP-1 drugs; the Netherlands has ASML; the US has its technology giants.

The Gold Guillotine

Converting returns into gold acts as what he calls “a guillotine.” It cuts India, Switzerland, New Zealand, Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands and Japan. What survives, on his figures, is four markets: the Nasdaq-100, the S&P 500, Taiwan and Denmark. “The entire engine of human economic progress over the last two decades was restricted to just four indexes. Every other bank, retail store, construction firm and telecom provider was a net wealth destroyer when you measure it in gold.” Even the S&P 500, he adds, only made the list because of the last four or five years of the AI buildout. Before that it would not have.

What He Concludes

Several conclusions follow in his telling. Diversification is dilution. A global all-world fund gets its real growth from TSMC, ASML, Novo Nordisk and the Mag 7; everything else is “complete dead weight” held for the feeling of safety. Concentration is not the risk. National pension systems that invest in their home markets are destroying their citizens’ wealth every day, invisibly. Avoiding bad investments matters more than finding good ones: the gold and money-supply filter cuts through the noise. And, for business owners, a paradox: large public companies earn lower returns on capital as they grow, while a small boutique business can earn very high returns, so owners must compare their own return on capital with the market’s before deciding where to put the next dollar (Chapter 31).

“Wealth is about the weight. The weight of gold has always been the same. Nominal numbers can be faked and inflated.” — BigBankTheory

For growth ETFs, he names alternatives to his default Nasdaq-100: VGT (Vanguard Information Technology), semiconductor funds that “outperformed violently,” and SPMO, the S&P 500 Momentum ETF, which he likes for holding up in 2022 because its momentum rules rotated into energy during the Fed’s hikes. The broader point is that whatever vehicle you use, it should hold the small set of companies that actually generate real returns.

The Other Side: Hindsight Is Doing a Lot of Work

Look-back bias. The four survivors were selected using the same twenty-year window that ends in a US tech and AI boom. Over 1980–2000, gold fell about 60% and almost every stock market on earth beat it. The yardstick flatters itself when measured over its own best period.

The winners were not always obvious. Bessembinder’s own conclusion favors diversification because past giants (Nortel, General Electric, Intel, Nokia, Cisco in 2000) looked like obvious permanent winners. Novo Nordisk fell more than 50% from its 2024 peak, and Denmark’s market with it.

Country comparisons are messy. Using local money supply ignores exchange rates, population growth and the fact that many multinationals list at home but earn abroad. Long-run global data (Dimson, Marsh and Staunton) show most developed markets beat local inflation by 3–6% a year over a century.

What survives: returns really are concentrated in a few companies; home-market bias really has cost investors in many countries; and measuring against a harder benchmark than CPI is a useful discipline.

Part II
Debt as a Tool

Most people are taught that debt is the enemy. His central claim is that debt is an amplifier, and whether it helps or hurts depends entirely on who holds it and what it is secured against.

Chapter 9: Understanding Debt — Amplifier, Real Rates, Good vs Bad

Videos: Margin Loan vs Credit Card; Why I Never Sell Stocks; Repaying the Loan; Stop Using the S&P 500

He opens his credit-card video with the sentence that frames his entire view of borrowing. “Debt really is just an economic amplifier of whoever is using it.” Give debt to someone with strong investing knowledge, a portfolio and financial discipline, and it amplifies their competence. Give it to someone with no investments, no literacy and no discipline, and it amplifies their mistakes; in his words it becomes “a financial death trap.” The same credit card, the same margin account, the same mortgage, produces opposite outcomes in different hands. That is why he refuses to call any form of debt good or bad in itself.

“Risk is a behavior. It’s not a tool.” — BigBankTheory

The Real Interest Rate

The first concept he wants viewers to grasp is the real interest rate: the nominal rate on a loan minus the rate of inflation. He admits he worked in the UK mortgage industry for more than ten years without ever framing it this way. “I just thought, whatever the Bank of England base rate is or the Fed funds rate is, that’s what interest rates are. But it’s not. Inflation is what decides.” If you borrow at 4% and inflation runs at 4%, your true cost of borrowing is zero. The dollars you repay are worth less than the dollars you borrowed, by exactly enough to cancel the interest.

His favorite illustration is December 2021. The Fed funds rate was effectively zero, about 0.08%, while consumer inflation was running around 7%. The real rate was therefore roughly minus 7%. “You’re practically getting paid to borrow money,” he says. It was precisely because real rates were so deeply negative that the Fed began raising rates aggressively in 2022. Negative real rates are wonderful for borrowers and brutal for savers, and they are exactly the condition under which governments shrink their debts.

Real rate: three examples Margin loan 5%, CPI 3%: real cost +2% Margin loan 5%, M2 7%: real cost −2% Dec 2021: rate 0.08%, CPI ~7%: real ≈ −7%

Which inflation measure you subtract changes the answer. Against CPI, most loans have a positive real cost; against M2 growth, a 5% loan is shrinking in real terms by 2% a year. This is how he concludes that a $20,000 loan is “worth only $16,000” in real terms after ten years.

Inflation Works on Debt Just as It Works on Cash

The second concept is the mirror image of something everyone already believes. “People know about inflation, but they don’t apply it to debt.” Everyone accepts that $10,000 fifty years ago could buy far more than $10,000 today. Few people notice that the same logic applies to a loan: $10,000 owed fifty years ago would be trivial to repay today. Inflation transfers wealth from lenders to borrowers. Savers are lenders (to their bank); homeowners with fixed-rate mortgages are borrowers. This is why he says inflation is bad for savers and good for people with debt, and why he wants to be on the debt side of that transfer.

Good Debt and Bad Debt

If debt is an amplifier, the obvious question is what it is amplifying. He draws the line by what the debt is attached to and what it costs. Bad debt: a credit card balance carried at 20–40% APR; car finance at 10–20% on a depreciating car; consumer loans for things that lose value. The rate is far above inflation and the thing bought shrinks. Good debt: a loan at or below the rate of inflation, used to hold or buy assets that appreciate faster than the rate. “If you’re borrowing against the most productive assets in the world, like Microsoft and Google, that is not bad debt. It’s the complete opposite.”

No Credit Check

One practical feature of borrowing against securities surprises most people. “It’s completely separate to your credit rating.” A margin loan or securities-backed line is secured by the portfolio, so the lender cares about one thing only: the quality and value of the collateral. There is no income verification, no payslips, no employment check, no credit score. “They won’t even ask how much your income is.” And the better the collateral, the more they will lend: “If you have bonds, we’ll give you more, because a US Treasury is pristine collateral.” This is also why he argues the strategy is not only for the rich. The lender does not care whether you are rich; it cares what you have pledged.

How He Actually Uses Leverage

It helps to be precise about what kind of leverage he means, because “leverage” covers everything from a mortgage to a 3× ETF. He uses exactly one kind: a loan against a diversified, liquid portfolio of quality assets, kept small relative to the portfolio. He uses it for two different purposes, with two different limits.

The first purpose is extraction leverage: borrowing to spend, for lifestyle, weddings, cars, travel, a house deposit, or a retiree’s living costs. The money leaves the portfolio, so the collateral does not grow when you borrow. Here his limit is about 20% loan-to-value, 25% at most. The second purpose is accumulation leverage: borrowing to buy more of the same kind of assets. The collateral grows as you borrow, because the loan buys more stock. Here he allows up to about 30% debt against the original equity. In his example, $100,000 of stock plus a $30,000 loan used to buy more stock gives $130,000 of assets and a loan-to-value of 23%. At a 5.1% margin rate that costs about $80 a month, partly offset by dividends, to control $30,000 of extra assets.

Why he uses leverage at all

Because selling is expensive. Selling triggers capital gains tax, stops the sold shares compounding forever, and forces a re-entry decision. Borrowing avoids all three.

Because cheap debt shrinks. If the loan costs less than the rate at which money is debased, its real value falls every year you hold it.

Because the assets grow faster than the loan. A portfolio compounding at 15% against a loan at 5% widens the gap every year; loan-to-value falls without any repayment.

Because the step-up erases the tax. If you never sell, the embedded gain disappears at death, so the loan is the only way to spend from the portfolio without ever paying capital gains tax.

The Other Side

Real rates can turn positive fast. In 2023 the Fed funds rate was above 5% while inflation fell toward 3%, so real rates were about +2% and margin loans cost a real 3–4% a year. A strategy that relies on negative real rates suffers in those years.

Inflation helps fixed-rate borrowers, not floating-rate ones. A 30-year fixed mortgage is genuinely eroded by inflation. A margin loan’s rate floats; when inflation rises, central banks raise rates, and your loan cost rises with them. The inflation hedge is weaker than it first sounds.

What survives: the real-rate lens, the amplifier framing, and the distinction between debt attached to appreciating assets and debt attached to consumption are all sound and widely taught in corporate finance.

Chapter 10: The Debt Fallacy — When Paying Off Debt Is the Mistake

Videos: When Can You Borrow for Lifestyle; Repaying the Loan

If cheap debt shrinks on its own, then racing to repay it has a cost. He puts it at its most extreme: “People are in such an aggressive rush to pay off cheap debt, and it’s actually mathematical suicide.” The example he uses is the home mortgage. If your mortgage rate is below the true rate of inflation, then every extra dollar you send the bank is a dollar that could have been in an asset growing faster than the mortgage costs. In his words, “the bank is actually paying them to live in the property.”

The Arithmetic

Consider a household with a $400,000 mortgage at 3.5% and an extra $2,000 a month. Option one: prepay the mortgage. Each dollar earns a guaranteed 3.5% (the interest avoided), tax-free, but it is locked into the house and cannot be accessed without a new loan. Option two: invest the $2,000 in a growth portfolio. If it compounds at 10%, the gap between 10% and 3.5% is the reward for keeping the mortgage. Over twenty years, $2,000 a month at 3.5% grows to about $690,000 of mortgage savings; at 10% it grows to about $1.5 million. And if inflation runs at 4%, the mortgage is shrinking in real terms the whole time.

$2,000/month for 20 years Prepaying a 3.5% mortgage: ~$690,000 of value Investing at 7%: ~$1.04 million Investing at 10%: ~$1.52 million Investing at 15%: ~$3.0 million

The gap is the price of the certainty and peace of mind that come from having no mortgage. He argues most people pay that price without knowing its size.

His Critique of Dave Ramsey

His target throughout is Dave Ramsey, the radio host whose “Baby Steps” program tells listeners to pay off all debt, including the mortgage. He is more respectful than you might expect: Ramsey is “correct about 99.9% of finance.” But “his program is literally called Baby Steps. What does that tell you?” Ramsey’s audience, he argues, are people who need to have their hands held: people with high consumer debt, no savings and no discipline, for whom any debt is dangerous. For them, Ramsey’s rules are right. “He’s actually teaching a behavior. It’s not math. It’s behavioral psychology.”

The mistake, in his view, is when people who have outgrown that stage keep following rules designed for it. He cites Ramsey callers who earn $300,000 a year but spend $350,000; for them, Ramsey is right. He also cites callers with millions in assets who ask Ramsey for permission to buy a car, or who work in jobs they hate because they are afraid to stop. The same rules that rescue the first group imprison the second. Chapter 29 is a full case study of exactly that: a doctor with a $7.5 million net worth who called Ramsey asking how to stay motivated for thirteen more years.

When He Would Repay

None of this means he never repays debt. “The only time you’re in a rush to pay off debt is when the rate is higher than inflation.” High-rate consumer debt should go first, always. And for margin loans specifically, he has one situation where he pays down the balance: when the Fed starts hiking rates and liquidity contracts, as in 2022. Even then, he pays down with new cash from outside the portfolio, never by selling. That rule is the subject of Chapter 23.

The Other Side

The comparison is not like for like. Prepaying a mortgage earns a guaranteed after-tax return. Investing earns an uncertain one. The fair comparison is the mortgage rate against a risk-free investment, such as a Treasury. When Treasuries yield more than your mortgage, investing in them wins with no extra risk; when they yield less, prepaying wins on a risk-adjusted basis.

Sequence risk is real. Someone who chose to invest instead of prepay in 2007 and then lost their job in 2009 could face a 50% portfolio loss and a mortgage at the same time. Paid-off homes are not efficient, but they are resilient.

Behavior is part of the math. Money not sent to the mortgage is often spent, not invested. Ramsey’s rules win for most people precisely because they account for that.

What survives: for a disciplined investor with a low fixed-rate mortgage and a long horizon, keeping the mortgage and investing the difference has historically produced much more wealth. Low-rate fixed mortgages from 2020–21 are among the best financial assets many households own.

Chapter 11: Institutional vs Civilian Debt

Videos: Why I Never Sell Stocks; Stop Using the S&P 500; Repaying the Loan

He frames the difference between how the wealthy and everyone else use debt as a difference in training. “We humans have been trained that debt is like a predator.” Most people learn about debt through credit cards, car loans and student loans: high rates, attached to things that lose value, with fixed monthly payments. Their experience of debt is overwhelmingly of the bad kind, so they generalize. Institutions and the wealthy experience debt differently: low rates, attached to appreciating assets, with no required payments. “The wealthy view debt as a tool. That’s what they use to buy the Lamborghinis and the yachts in Monaco.”

What a Civilian Does

He is harsh about what he sees as the civilian pattern. A civilian sells stock “to buy cars, watches, clothes, milk, bread, eggs and iPads.” They sell a productive asset to buy a liability, pay tax on the sale, and permanently shrink the pool of capital compounding for them. In his margin-of-safety video he calls selling an asset to buy a liability “financial malpractice.” The same person is terrified of a margin loan but perfectly comfortable with a mortgage, which he finds inconsistent: “People are quite comfortable to take a mortgage out on a property. But when it’s the same strategy for stocks, it causes a lot of fear and uncertainty.”

What an Institution Does

The institutional pattern is the inverse. Hold the productive assets. Borrow at institutional rates, usually a benchmark plus a small spread, against them. Use the loan for spending or for buying other assets. Let the assets outgrow the debt. “Why would you use cash if cash can be used to buy assets?” Family offices, sovereign wealth funds and private-bank clients, he says, “don’t sell. They let it grow and grow and grow.” The debt is not something to be eliminated; it is a permanent part of the balance sheet, managed as a ratio rather than a burden.

The Google Example

He makes it concrete with a single stock. Put $100,000 into Google and assume it grows 20% a year for ten years; it becomes about $620,000. Borrow 20% ($20,000) at the start. The broker never asks for a monthly payment. “They just add a small amount of interest on top of the loan. It’s capitalized.” At 5%, the loan grows to about $32,600 over ten years. Meanwhile the stock has grown to $620,000. Loan-to-value falls from 20% to about 5.3%. “It’s not about repaying the debt. It’s about assets that just grow faster. It’s a race.”

“But Buffett Sells”

The objection he hears most is that Warren Buffett sells stocks. His answer is that Buffett sells for different reasons. Berkshire sells when a business breaks, when the fundamentals deteriorate, or to move into a better investment or into T-bills. A retail investor sells to take profits or fund lifestyle. “You can’t compare yourself to a professional investor who’s been doing it for seventy years.” And Buffett himself calls selling some of his greatest mistakes: he sold Disney in the 1960s and Costco too early, while holding Coca-Cola and American Express for decades and letting compounding do the work.

The Other Side

Institutions also fail with debt. Long-Term Capital Management, Lehman Brothers, Archegos and countless leveraged real estate funds were “institutional” borrowers. Institutional rates do not make institutional leverage safe; the size of the loan does.

Buffett has sold a lot. Berkshire exited airlines in 2020, sold most of its Wells Fargo stake, and trimmed Apple heavily in 2023–24, while accumulating over $300 billion in cash. He sells more than the “never sell” framing implies, and Berkshire itself uses very little debt.

The Google example assumes 20% a year for a decade. Google did roughly that from 2015 to 2025, but single stocks can stall for years (Microsoft 2000–2013, Intel 2000–today). The LTV math only works if the growth arrives.

What survives: the civilian habit of selling appreciating assets to fund consumption really is costly, and the institutional habit of treating debt as a managed ratio is more rational than treating all debt as an emergency.

Chapter 12: Buy, Never Sell, Borrow, Die

Videos: Why I Never Sell Stocks; Repaying the Loan; CPAs Give Terrible Advice

The name describes four steps, and he explains them in plain language. “The rich buy assets. They never sell the assets. They borrow against the assets, take a loan out at a low interest rate, and allow the asset to just continue to grow.” The fourth step, dying, is where the tax code finishes the job. Under the US step-up in basis, inherited assets are revalued at market price on the date of death, and the entire unrealized gain disappears for income-tax purposes. The heirs can sell with no capital gains tax, repay the loan, and keep the rest, or keep running the strategy themselves. “That’s how you’ve got these dynasties,” he says, naming the Waltons, Rockefellers and Rothschilds.

Every Investor Runs the Same Strategy

His first insight in the “never sell” video is that almost every style of investing is secretly the same. The day trader tries to buy low and sell high within 24 hours. The swing trader does it over weeks. The buy-and-hold investor does it over ten years. The retirement saver does it over forty years, then starts selling to live. “It’s all the same strategy, just packaged in different ways.” All of them end with a sale. He proposes the one strategy that does not: buy, keep buying, and never sell, including at the end. “I plan on keeping my portfolio long after I’m gone.”

Seven Reasons Not to Sell

He lists the costs of selling, and they are worth setting out one by one because each is a separate argument.

1. Tax. Every sale outside a Roth IRA or UK ISA triggers capital gains tax, and you lose not only the tax but everything that money would have compounded into. Even inside tax wrappers, he notes, you still have to sell to access the money. 2. Timing. “There is zero evidence that anyone knows when a stock is at its bottom or its top,” not even the largest hedge funds. 3. Compounding. Selling interrupts the curve. He cites the rule of thumb that most of the gains arrive late: $10,000 that becomes $100,000 over fifteen years makes most of its gain in the last five. Selling resets you to the flat part of the curve.

4. Re-entry. If you sell a winner, you face a new decision: when to buy back in. You only sold because it went up, so buying back means paying more. 5. Behavior. He cites the Dalbar studies (the Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior), which found that the average fund investor earns far less than the funds they own, because of badly timed buying and selling. “It wasn’t the asset class that caused the portfolio to underperform. It was the investor’s behavior.” The typical result is selling winners and keeping losers.

6. Winners keep winning. Great companies can look expensive for years while continuing to compound: Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Apple, Nvidia. He cites the finding that about 4% of stocks produce most of the market’s gains (Chapter 8), and argues that the winners are often already known. 7. You must be right three times. Buy-low-sell-high requires being right when you buy, right when you sell, and right again when you re-enter or pick the next stock. Buying and holding requires one decision. “Do I buy Nvidia? Yes or no? One decision, and I let it go.”

“Why would you sell something that goes up in value?” — BigBankTheory

When He Would Sell

“Never sell” has exceptions, and he states them clearly. You sell if the business has genuinely lost its pricing power or moat, and you are completely certain of it; not because of scary news or a 20% drop, which is “just emotional selling.” You sell to buy another asset, never a liability. And if a single stock has done spectacularly well, say up 1,000% on Nvidia, selling it and paying the tax to rotate into an ETF is fine: “For a strong investor, capital gains tax is a rounding error.” The one absolute rule is: never sell assets to fund lifestyle.

A Mindset First, a Strategy Second

The most revealing line comes from his video on CPAs: “Buy Borrow Die is a mindset first, an investment strategy second.” Removing the sell button changes how you invest. If you know you will never sell, you only buy what you would be happy to own forever, so you research harder, pay attention to valuation, and ignore short-term noise. “The sell button is the enemy for most investors. If we eliminate that because it’s part of our framework, that can only benefit us. Otherwise we become traders.” He admits he cannot know if Google will exist in twenty years. “That’s not the point.” The point is the behavior the commitment produces.

Capital Gains Tax Is the Cost of Being Right

The same video contains the most mature statement in his whole body of work. “The IRS is not your enemy. Capital gains tax is truly the cost of being right.” He criticizes people who hold failing assets “to spite the IRS,” and says that if the returns are strong enough, paying capital gains tax is still better than being a passive investor borrowing against a mediocre portfolio. “Your enemy is M2 inflation.” This matters because it shows that for him, tax avoidance is a bonus, not the engine. The engine is compounding high-quality assets without interruption.

His loop

Buy quality assets → keep buying → never sell to fund lifestyle → borrow ~20% against them → let assets outgrow the loan → borrow more as the portfolio grows → die → heirs inherit with a stepped-up basis → heirs repay the loan tax-free or continue the loop.

The Other Side

The Dalbar study is contested. Critics, including the financial researcher Michael Edesess and others, argue its methodology exaggerates the “behavior gap”; independent estimates from Morningstar put it at roughly 1–1.5 points a year, real but much smaller.

The “80% of gains in the last 20%” line is a loose description of exponential growth rather than a law; the exact share depends on the rate and the time period.

Never selling concentrates risk over time. Winners grow into ever-larger shares of a portfolio. Without rebalancing, a portfolio can end up 40% in one stock. That is how many Enron, Lucent and GE employees lost their retirements.

What survives: selling has real costs that most investors underestimate, and the discipline of buying only what you would hold forever is one of the few behaviors consistently associated with better results.

Chapter 13: Hardening the Collateral — Margin of Safety Through Time

Video: The First Law of Buy Borrow Die: Hardening the Collateral

He calls this video “the most important rule, the first law of Buy Borrow Die,” and admits he was surprised he had not made it earlier. The idea comes from Benjamin Graham, Warren Buffett’s teacher, born in 1894, who coined the phrase margin of safety: the gap between the price you pay and the value you get. His twist is to show that the margin of safety is not fixed at the moment of purchase. It grows the longer you hold, because the business keeps earning while your purchase price stays the same. “The best protection to your portfolio is time. You can buy defensive stocks, but you cannot buy time in the market.”

The Earnings Floor

The mechanism is simple. Suppose you buy a stock at $100 when it earns $5 a share (20 times earnings). Five years later it earns $10 a share. For the stock to fall back to your $100 purchase price, the market would have to value it at 10 times earnings. Ten years later, earning $20, falling to $100 would mean 5 times earnings. At some point the price at which you would lose money implies a valuation so low that it becomes, in his word, “impossible.” Every hedge fund on earth would buy. “The company continues to make money, and it creates a floor that the stock physically cannot break through, because the earnings protect it.”

Selling Surrenders the Margin

The corollary is the reason he gets so animated about selling. When you sell a position held for a long time, you give up a margin of safety you can never get back. If you buy back in later, your new purchase price is today’s price, with today’s thin margin. “You’ve given up your margin of safety. You’ll never get that back.” He describes seeing someone who had held Meta since 2022 sell it, and being unable to believe it. Worse still is selling to buy a liability, a watch, a car, a house, which he calls “financial malpractice.” This is exactly the problem borrowing solves: you get the cash without surrendering the position.

Valuation: PEG, Not P/E

Because the margin of safety depends on the price you pay, he insists that entry valuation is “rule one.” But he warns against the most common valuation mistake: looking at the P/E ratio alone. “A P/E ratio is just a number. It tells you nothing.” A P/E of 25 is cheap for a company growing earnings 25% a year and expensive for one growing 5%. So he uses the PEG ratio: P/E divided by the earnings growth rate. A PEG of 1 is fair value. “If a company had a P/E of 1,000 but is growing at 1,000% a year, technically it’s fairly valued.”

PEG in practice (his examples) Dot-com Nasdaq, 1999: fwd P/E ~75, PEG ~3.7 → ~270% overvalued Apple (his video): PEG 2.1–2.9 → no margin of safety Walmart: beta 0.59, growth ~10% → ~315% overvalued Nasdaq-100 (his video): PEG 1.3–1.4 → cheap

A stock can become cheaper without falling, if forward earnings estimates rise faster than its price.

Defensive Does Not Mean Cheap

The Walmart example produces one of his more useful distinctions. Walmart is a classic defensive stock: its beta of 0.59 means it moves about 60% as much as the market, which dampens a portfolio’s swings. But at the time of his video it traded at a valuation he calculated as far above fair value for a company growing about 10%. “If Walmart crashed 50%, it’s not really crashing. It’s just going back to fair value.” Low volatility controls the swings; it does not protect you from overpaying. He notes the oddity that, in the AI-fear market he was filming in, defensive stocks were more expensive than the Mag 7.

Dollar-Cost Averaging at Fair Value

How does this square with never selling and regular investing? If you are dollar-cost averaging into an index, you do not need to check valuation every month. But “keep an eye on it.” If the index’s PEG climbs to 2, 2.5 or 3, “you can still invest, but maybe not as much.” He insists this is not market timing. “Timing the market is saying what’s the stock price going to do next Wednesday. This is paying fair value.” You never sell; you only adjust how fast you buy.

Mr. Market

He closes with Graham’s most famous parable. Imagine you own a private business, and every day a stranger called Mr. Market knocks on your door and offers to buy it or sell you more. Some days he is euphoric and offers a fortune; some days he is terrified and offers a pittance. You are never obliged to accept. His version: a stranger offers you $50,000 for a house you know is worth far more. You do not sell your house because of the offer. “When you completely separate the stock from the actual company, you’ll become a better investor than 95% of the world.”

The Other Side

The floor assumes the earnings arrive. The table uses analyst projections five years out. If earnings disappoint, the floor is lower. In 2022 Meta fell about 76% from its peak as earnings fell and the multiple compressed at the same time, the “double hit” that the margin-of-safety argument assumes away.

“Impossible” valuations have happened. Apple traded near 10 times earnings in 2013 and 2016. Microsoft traded near 9 times earnings in 2011–12. Great companies do sometimes trade at “dying telecom” multiples.

PEG has limits. It ignores balance sheets, the durability of growth and interest rates. A PEG of 1 on a 50% grower that will slow to 10% is not fair value.

What survives: the principle is correct and powerful: a business whose earnings keep compounding builds a rising floor under a long-held position, and that makes old positions genuinely safer collateral than new ones.

Chapter 14: Lombard Loans, Margin and SBLOCs — One Tool, Many Names

Videos: How to Make a Margin Call Mathematically Impossible; What Net Worth Is Required; Inside a Live BBD Portfolio; Margin Loan vs Credit Card

Lombard loans are the tool at the center of the strategy, so this chapter goes deep. The first thing to know is his clarification: “A margin loan, a Lombard loan, or a securities-backed line of credit are all the same thing.” Each is a loan secured by a portfolio of liquid securities. The names differ mainly by who offers them and where. Understanding the differences in how they are priced, how they are called, and what they really cost is what separates a sensible borrower from someone who pays private-bank fees for the illusion of safety.

The Three Names

A Lombard loan takes its name from the Lombard bankers of medieval northern Italy, who lent against pledged goods; London’s Lombard Street still carries the name. Today it is the standard term in Swiss and European private banking for a loan against a portfolio of securities. A margin loan is the brokerage version: your broker lends against the securities in your account, under Federal Reserve Regulation T and FINRA rules. A securities-based line of credit (SBLOC) is the US wealth-management version, typically offered by a bank affiliated with a large brokerage (Schwab, Morgan Stanley, Merrill, UBS), with the money usually barred from being used to buy more securities.

How the Rate Is Set

In his margin-call video he shows the pricing page of the broker he uses, which is Interactive Brokers. The rate is a benchmark plus a spread. The benchmark is essentially the overnight rate (SOFR or the Fed funds rate; about 3.66% when he recorded). The spread falls as the loan gets bigger. At Interactive Brokers Pro, the spread has historically been around 1.5 points on small balances, 1 point from $100,000, and 0.5–0.75 points on balances in the millions. “The more a client borrows, the cheaper the debt,” he says, and a cheaper loan lowers the hurdle the portfolio has to clear.

Two things follow. First, the rate floats. When the Fed hikes, your margin rate rises within days. There is no fixed-rate margin loan. Second, the difference between brokers is enormous. Retail brokers that advertise zero commissions often charge 8–12% on margin, several times the institutional rate. “The customer is the product,” he says of Robinhood-style apps. “If they don’t charge anything, there’s a fee in there somewhere.” For BBD, the loan rate is the hurdle, so the broker choice is a strategic decision, not a detail.

The Private-Bank Myth

Many people assume that a private bank is safer: a relationship manager will call you instead of an algorithm selling your stock, and you will get a few days to post more collateral. He agrees this is partly true. At private-bank scale, it is no longer called a margin call; it is a securities-backed loan, and there is “more leeway than a broker.” But he argues you pay heavily for that courtesy. The private bank may match the broker’s loan rate or even lose money on the loan, because it makes the money back through assets-under-management fees, typically around 1% a year. Over a lifetime, compounded, he calls that “donating 30% of your lifetime wealth for the perceived safety of a bank.”

What a 1% AUM fee costs over 30 years $5M at 8% for 30 years: $50.3M $5M at 7% (after 1% fee): $38.1M Cost of the fee: $12.2M (~24%)

At 40 years the fee consumes about 31% of the terminal value, which is the source of his “30% of lifetime wealth” figure.

Banks vs Brokers

He adds a structural argument. “A bank and a broker are two completely different business models.” A bank takes deposits (which are loans from you to the bank, callable at any moment) and lends them out long. It is highly leveraged and fragile. His example is Silicon Valley Bank, where customers tried to withdraw about $42 billion in a single day on March 9, 2023, and the bank was seized the next morning. A broker does not lend out customer securities in the same way and does not depend on depositors not running. “The banks are just financial transit,” he says in the live-portfolio video. Family offices hold everything at a prime brokerage and use the bank only to move money to spend.

Reg T vs Portfolio Margin

A broker margin account comes in two flavors. Reg T is the standard: 50% initial margin (you can borrow up to half the value of stock you buy) and at least 25% maintenance margin (your equity must stay above a quarter of the account). Portfolio margin is risk-based: the broker stress-tests the whole portfolio and sets the requirement from the simulated worst loss, often allowing 15% or less for a diversified index portfolio. It usually requires an account of at least $100,000–$110,000. He notes that portfolio margin gives “a lot more breathing room and buying power,” but that since BBD never borrows anywhere near 50%, a Reg T account is perfectly adequate.

Why He Uses Interactive Brokers

He is open that he uses Interactive Brokers (IBKR Pro), and gives three reasons. The margin rates are among the lowest available to individuals. The broker is “very strict” and runs on an algorithm that liquidates reckless speculators quickly, which he sees as a feature: it protects the platform’s balance sheet and keeps its costs low. And the fee structure is transparent: one free withdrawal a month, low FX spreads for someone like him who buys in multiple currencies. “If you’re building your house on sand,” he says of expensive platforms, “the foundation is so important.”

The Cheaper Alternative: Box Spreads

In the FIRE comparison video he mentions in passing that a sophisticated borrower could use a box spread at about 3.75% when margin cost 6.5%. A box spread combines four options on an index (a long call and short put at one strike, a short call and long put at another) so that the payoff at expiry is fixed. Selling a box is economically a loan at close to the Treasury rate plus a small spread, because the counterparty is the options clearinghouse. On index options it can also be taxed favorably under Section 1256. It requires options approval and care with execution, but for large balances it can cut borrowing costs meaningfully.

The Other Side

Automatic liquidation cuts both ways. Interactive Brokers does not usually call you; it sells, in real time, sometimes at the worst intraday price. The strictness he praises is exactly what makes a thinly-margined account dangerous there.

Brokers are not risk-free either. Customer securities are protected by SIPC (up to $500,000, including $250,000 cash) and by segregation rules, but broker failures (MF Global in 2011) have frozen customer assets for months.

Private banks earn their fees for some clients. Estate planning, trusts, tax coordination and multi-currency lending have real value for very large families. For most people borrowing against a simple ETF portfolio, his point holds: the fee is large and the lending is cheaper elsewhere.

Chapter 15: The Mechanics — 20% LTV, Capitalized Interest, Re-levering

Here is the strategy run as he actually runs it, step by step. The mechanics are simpler than most people expect, which is part of his point: the hard part is the portfolio and the discipline, not the loan. In the live-portfolio video, taking out the loan is literally clicking “withdraw” in a brokerage account. “We would click withdraw $2 million and we press a button. The money goes into the bank. That’s really how easy it is.”

The 20% Rule

His core number is a loan-to-value of about 20% of the gross portfolio value, 25% at most, when borrowing for lifestyle. He calls 20% “very conservative” by investing-world standards, and chooses it because it makes a margin call nearly impossible (Chapter 22). “The true defensive part of this entire strategy, the protection and risk management, is how much you’re actually borrowing. Above all else, that is the defense.” He sometimes goes to 30% when the loan buys more stock, because then the collateral grows with the loan.

No Monthly Payment: Capitalized Interest

The feature that confuses people most is that a margin loan has no required payments. “It’s not like a car payment,” he says. “They don’t ask you for a monthly payment. They just add a small amount of interest on top of the loan.” The interest is charged monthly and added to the balance: it is capitalized. The broker is comfortable with this because it holds the collateral. The consequence is that the loan compounds at the interest rate, just as the portfolio compounds at its growth rate. Whether you get richer or poorer depends entirely on which compounds faster.

His $1M Walkthrough

In the second “repaying the loan” video he builds the model from scratch. A $1 million portfolio. A 20% loan: $200,000. He says the portfolio needs about 15% a year and the loan must cost under 7%; the figures on his spreadsheet work out to roughly 13% growth and a loan rate near 6%. After ten years the portfolio has grown to about $3.5 million. The loan has also grown, but because the assets grew much faster, loan-to-value has fallen to about 10%. Meanwhile the purchasing-power hurdle says $1 million needed to become about $1.7–2 million just to stand still, so the portfolio has genuinely grown in real terms.

Re-levering: Drawing More as It Grows

Here is the part many explanations of BBD leave out. Because loan-to-value falls as the portfolio grows, there is room to borrow more without increasing risk. In his example, by year six the portfolio is worth about $2 million and the loan about $280,000, a loan-to-value of 14%. “If you want to be really conservative, you can bring that back up to 20%.” Borrowing another 6% of $2 million releases about $120,000 for spending. This is how the strategy funds a lifestyle for decades: not by one big loan, but by periodically topping the loan back up to the target ratio as the portfolio grows.

The Live Example: $10 Million at Interactive Brokers

The live-portfolio video makes the scale concrete. He builds a $10 million portfolio in Interactive Brokers’ risk terminal, a 70/30 barbell of the Nasdaq-100 plus Waste Management, O’Reilly Automotive, Walmart and Visa, with about 5% in T-bills, carrying $5.4 million of unrealized gains. He withdraws $2 million, 20%. Net liquidation value drops to $8 million, but the gross portfolio is still $10 million, and all of it keeps compounding. The interest on $2 million at current rates is about $87,000 a year. That day, the Nasdaq sleeve alone had moved $104,000. “A year’s worth of interest paid in one day’s trading. This is where labor is detached from your body.”

Selling vs Borrowing: The $12.6 Million Gap

In the net-worth video he compares two people with the same starting portfolio over thirty years at 10% growth. Both need money for life, a wedding, holidays. One sells shares each time and pays capital gains tax. The other borrows. “The person that used Buy Borrow Die had an additional $12.6 million after 30 years.” Their net worth started identical, and at the moment of each $50,000 withdrawal it was identical again: one had $50,000 less stock, the other $50,000 more debt. The difference is what happens afterward. The sold shares never compound again. The borrowed-against shares do.

Sell vs Borrow Simulator

Play with the simulator and you will see the shape of his argument and its limits at the same time. With 10% growth, a 5% loan and modest spending, the borrower ends far ahead and loan-to-value stays low. Push spending up, or push growth down toward the loan rate, and loan-to-value climbs relentlessly, the “silent margin call” of Chapter 19. The simulator assumes no crashes; Chapter 22 handles those. Its message is his message: the strategy is a race between two compounding rates, and the growth rate must win by a clear margin.

The Other Side

Smooth returns are an illusion. Real portfolios arrive at 10% or 15% averages through years like −33% and +50%. A loan that compounds steadily against an asset that lurches can hit dangerous LTVs in bad years even when the average works out.

Rates move. His $2 million withdrawal cost $87,000 a year at ~4.35%. At 2023’s peak rates, around 6–6.5% at Interactive Brokers, the same loan would cost about $130,000.

The $12.6M figure’s assumptions were not shown. The simulator above lets you check the claim with your own numbers.

Chapter 16: Capital Location — Accounts, Taxes and the Paper-Millionaire Trap

Videos: What Net Worth Is Required (USA Only); Inside a Live BBD Portfolio; When Can You Retire

He repeats a phrase that is easy to overlook: “Capital location is just as important as capital allocation.” Allocation is what you own. Location is which account you own it in: a 401(k), a Roth IRA, a taxable brokerage account, a trust. The same stock behaves completely differently for BBD depending on where it sits. “Someone could spend their entire life funding a 401(k) and a Roth, and it’s in the wrong location, on the wrong platform, with the wrong broker, and they don’t know until ten years later.”

The Paper-Millionaire Trap

His net-worth video describes what he calls the paper millionaire. They follow the standard advice: max out the 401(k), max out the Roth IRA, buy a house, build an emergency fund. All good things, he says, and he uses them too. But they reach their fifties worth a million or more, almost all of it illiquid. “All of these strategies have great tax benefits at the start, but they have a fragile finishing line.” When they want money for life, the only options are cash, expensive or awkward debt, or selling taxable assets, which he calls “amputating” their wealth.

The 401(k): Survival, Not Wealth

He is clear that you should always take an employer match: “If your employer matches it, it’s 100% ROI.” But he calls the 401(k) “survival in retirement,” not a wealth-building structure, because of the friction around getting money out. Withdraw before age 59½ and IRC section 72(t) imposes a 10% penalty on top of ordinary income tax, easily 35% or more combined. Borrow against it and the loan is capped at $50,000 or 50% of the vested balance, whichever is less. Leave your job and the loan typically becomes due quickly; if you cannot repay, it is treated as a taxable distribution. “It is a leash held by your employer.”

The Roth, the Home and the Emergency Fund

The Roth IRA is excellent in retirement, because withdrawals are tax-free, but it offers no liquidity before then and cannot be borrowed against at all. The home can be borrowed against, but only with friction: a HELOC or refinance requires payslips, credit checks and weeks of processing, and usually requires repaying principal. And outside prime locations, he argues, a primary home “withdraws more money and requires more upkeep than it generates in wealth.” The emergency fund is for emergencies. None of these gives cheap, instant, tax-free liquidity.

The Taxable Brokerage: The Real Shelter

The account people overlook is the ordinary taxable brokerage account. It has no upfront tax deduction, which is why advice ranks it last. But it has two properties no retirement account has. You can borrow against it instantly at institutional rates. And its assets receive a step-up in basis at death. “It’s called a taxable brokerage, but it turns out to be one of the most efficient tax shelters available.” A traditional 401(k) or IRA never gets a step-up; heirs pay ordinary income tax on everything they withdraw, and under the SECURE Act most non-spouse heirs must empty an inherited IRA within ten years.

Other Tax Details He Uses

Several smaller tax facts appear across the videos. Loans are not income, so money borrowed is not taxed. Treasury interest is exempt from state and local income tax, which matters enormously in California or New York, and is why Renee’s plan (Chapter 29) uses T-bills. Margin interest can be deductible as investment interest expense, which he says can bring an after-tax borrowing cost down to about 2.6%, though there is a major catch (see below). And the IRS taxes US citizens wherever they live. “Americans can’t just pick up and leave and escape the tax,” unlike UK residents who can sever ties by moving. “There is no escape. You have to play the game.”

The Other Side: The Fine Print

Margin interest is only deductible if the loan bought investments. Under IRC §163(d) and Form 4952, investment interest is deductible only against net investment income, only if the borrowed money was used to buy taxable investments, and only if you itemize. Interest on a loan used for lifestyle spending is personal interest and is not deductible. This is a big caveat for lifestyle BBD.

Retirement accounts still usually win for most savers. The upfront deduction plus decades of tax-free compounding often beats a taxable account even after accounting for the step-up, especially for people who will be in a lower bracket in retirement. The strongest version of his argument is “fund the taxable account too,” not “instead of.”

What survives: the taxable account is badly underrated by standard advice for anyone who expects to hold appreciated assets until death, and the liquidity problem of the paper millionaire is real.

Chapter 17: How the Loan Ends

Videos: Repaying the Loan, Parts 1 and 2; What Net Worth Is Required

“How do they repay the loan?” is the first question almost everyone asks about Buy Borrow Die, and he has two videos on it. He thinks the question itself reveals a scarcity mindset: people imagine the loan as a car loan, a fixed debt that must be ground down with payments from their salary. “The assets pay the loan back, not you.” There are four ways the loan ends, and a serious BBD investor usually relies on a combination.

1. The Portfolio Outgrows It

The first answer is that you may never repay it at all. He starts with a simple example: $100,000 in the S&P 500, borrow $10,000, a 10% loan-to-value. If the portfolio doubles to $200,000, loan-to-value falls to about 5%. “The portfolio grows, the debt gets smaller.” If the asset keeps performing, “you might just not pay the loan. You might just carry that all the way to death.” He points out that this is exactly how the US government treats its own debt: it never pays down principal, it only tries to grow GDP faster than the debt. “You’re copying the same strategy the government uses.”

Dividends Chip Away

Even growth portfolios pay small dividends. Microsoft yields under 1%; the Nasdaq-100 about 0.5%. He lets those dividends flow into the account, where they reduce the loan balance. “It’s not going to pay it off, but it will chip away at it. You can look at it as reducing the cost of the loan.” On a portfolio five times the size of the loan, a 0.7% yield offsets about 3.5 points of interest, most of the cost. His title for one video makes the point with Treasury income: “Why a 0.7% dividend yield covers your entire Lombard loan.”

Why a small yield matters at 20% LTV Portfolio: $2,000,000 Loan (20%): $400,000 at 4.5% = $18,000/yr Dividends at 0.7%: $14,000/yr Net interest drag: $4,000/yr (0.2% of portfolio)

At a 10% LTV, the same yield covers the entire interest bill.

2. De-lever Before Liquidity Contracts

The second answer is to pay down the loan at the right time. “Do they never repay the debt in their entire life? There is a time where they should delever.” Assets rise during liquidity expansion and fall during liquidity contraction, typically when the Fed raises rates. The wise move is to be in a position to pay down debt before that happens, using cash, not asset sales. Chapter 23 covers how he thinks about spotting it.

3. Life Insurance

His simplest answer: life insurance. “Easiest, simplest solution. Life insurance pays it off. That’s why it’s called Buy Borrow Die.” A policy sized to the loan pays out at death, the loan is cleared, and the heirs keep the whole portfolio intact. Life insurance death benefits are generally received free of income tax. For large estates, the policy is often held in an irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT) so that the payout is also outside the taxable estate.

4. The Step-Up in Basis

The fourth answer is the one that gives the strategy its name. At death, IRC section 1014 resets the cost basis of inherited assets to their market value. Stock bought for $200,000 and worth $5 million at death has a new basis of $5 million. The heirs can sell $600,000 of it to repay a $600,000 loan and owe no capital gains tax. “They can sell some assets and pay off all the debt if they want. Or, if they’re well educated in the strategy, they might want to just carry the strategy on.” He mentions that the rules around estate size changed at the start of the year; under the 2025 tax law the federal estate-tax exemption became $15 million per person from 2026, indexed for inflation.

5. When Selling Is the Right Answer

He adds a fifth, less comfortable answer. If an asset has genuinely lost its pricing power, sell it, pay the capital gains tax, and repay the loan. “Sometimes people do this strategy where they’re losing money to spite the IRS. When in actual fact if they just sold the asset and paid capital gains tax, they would have been better off.” The strategy exists to protect good assets from being sold; it is not a reason to cling to bad ones.

The Other Side

The step-up could change. Proposals to tax unrealized gains at death, or to cap the step-up, have appeared repeatedly (the 2021 Build Back Better drafts included one). None has passed, but a strategy that depends on a single tax provision for its final step carries legislative risk.

Life insurance is not free. Permanent life insurance large enough to cover a seven-figure loan at age 60+ is expensive, and term insurance can expire before you do.

Estate tax still applies above the exemption. The step-up removes income tax on the gain; it does not remove the 40% estate tax on estates above $15 million per person.

What survives: for estates under the exemption, the combination of borrowing in life and the step-up at death is one of the most powerful tax features in the US code, which is exactly why the wealthy use it.

Part IV
Building the Portfolio

Since BBD only extracts wealth, the portfolio has to create it. These chapters cover what to own, what to avoid, and how to balance growth against the need to survive crashes.

Chapter 18: Asset Selection — Why the S&P 500 Is Not Enough

Videos: Repaying the Loan, Part 2; When Can You Retire; Stop Using the S&P 500; When Rates Rise (supplementary)

Every chapter so far leads to one practical question: what should the portfolio actually hold? His answer begins with what it should not rely on. “You cannot be a passive investor and copy Buy Borrow Die.” He is scathing about accountants and YouTubers who present BBD as a hack (“Jeff Bezos does this, so let’s copy him”) and then tell viewers to borrow against an S&P 500 index fund. “They think they’ve discovered some secret, when really they look foolish, because they’re liquidating themselves slowly.” Bezos borrows against Amazon. The strategy only works if you own something that grows like Amazon.

The 15% Hurdle

His required return is built from two pieces. The portfolio must beat money-supply growth, which he puts at about 7%, and it must beat the loan rate, around 4–6%. Combine them and the portfolio needs to compound at something like 12–15%. “I like to aim for 15%,” he says, “depending on how cheap the interest rates are at the time.” The loan itself must cost less than 7%. This is the number that disqualifies the broad index in his eyes: 9–10% nominal does not clear a 12–15% hurdle.

The Wrong Way, in Numbers

In his repayment video he runs the S&P 500 version alongside the growth version. Same $1 million, same 20% loan, same ten years. The S&P investor earns roughly 7–10%, pays the loan interest, and measures the result against 7% money growth. “They lose over 10 years. They lose $310,000 of purchasing power.” The problem is not the index; it is borrowing against it. Without the loan, an index fund merely keeps pace. With the loan, the interest pushes it below the line.

Weighted Beta: Flying Blind

The supplementary video on rising rates adds a concept he says most BBD investors ignore: weighted beta, the average beta of every holding, weighted by size. It tells you how much the whole portfolio moves when the market moves. “If you don’t know what your weighted beta is, you’re flying blind.” A beta of exactly 1 means the portfolio moves with the index and so earns roughly the index return, which is not enough to outrun the debt and inflation. Too high a beta means large swings that could threaten a margin call. He targets something like 1.15 to 1.3: enough extra growth to beat the hurdle, not so much volatility that the loan becomes dangerous.

Why the Nasdaq-100

For people who do not want to pick stocks, he keeps returning to one vehicle: the Nasdaq-100, most commonly held through QQQ or its cheaper sibling QQQM. “The only ETF that I looked at that qualifies is the Nasdaq-100.” His reasons: it holds the largest non-financial companies on the Nasdaq, it is about 56% technology but also includes consumer and healthcare names, it rebalances itself, and it has historically compounded around 15% a year, with roughly 20% over the five years before his video and about 40% in the year before. “You’re getting access to the strongest technology companies in the world.”

He is clear that it is a choice for people who will not do the research. In Renee’s case study: “I cannot expect her to be a full-time investor, reading 10-K reports, checking pricing power, checking whether a company’s moat is intact. So the QQQ is the only real answer to getting growth in a passive manner.” For people who will do the work, he prefers individual monopolies, and he mentions other growth vehicles too: VGT, semiconductor ETFs, and SPMO. And because the ETF is the one decision you will never reverse, “your ETF choice is probably the most important decision to make.”

What to Do With an Existing S&P Holding

His views on the S&P 500 need reconciling, because he says different things in different videos. In one he calls borrowing against it slow liquidation. In another he uses it as his example asset and says an index fund “always picks the winners.” In the rate-rise video he resolves it: if someone already holds the S&P 500, “I would still not tell you to sell it. It’s still pristine collateral for a lender.” Instead, direct new money toward growth until the portfolio’s weighted beta rises to around 1.15 or 1.3. The S&P is fine to own and fine to borrow against in small amounts; it is just not enough on its own to fund lifestyle borrowing for decades.

The Other Side

15% a year is a very high bar. The Nasdaq-100 compounded around 15–18% from 2010 to 2025, one of the best stretches for any major index in history. From March 2000 it took until 2015 to regain its peak. An investor who began BBD on the Nasdaq in 2000 would have faced fifteen years of negative returns against a growing loan.

Higher return means higher drawdown. The Nasdaq-100 fell about 83% in 2000–02, 42% in 2008 and 33% in 2022. The S&P’s worst drawdowns were smaller in the first and last of those.

Concentration risk. The top ten Nasdaq holdings are often more than half the index, so it is closer to a bet on a handful of mega-caps than its name suggests.

What survives: borrowing against an asset whose expected return barely exceeds the loan rate is a weak trade, and the case for pairing BBD with higher-growth assets follows directly from the arithmetic, as long as the investor accepts the larger swings.

Chapter 19: Growth vs Dividends and the Silent Margin Call

Videos: Why High-Yield Dividends Destroy BBD; The Silent Margin Call

The most common way people try to do BBD, he says, is also the most common way to get it wrong: a high-dividend portfolio. It feels safe. Money arrives every quarter. The portfolio moves less than the market. Wealth managers build it for clients because it keeps them calm. He argues it is exactly backwards. “The whole point of Buy Borrow Die is to avoid a taxable event. Dividends are a taxable event.” A loan against the portfolio is not.

He Is Not Against Dividends

He is careful to say dividends have a place. They are good for people in the lowest tax brackets (qualified dividends are taxed at 0% for lower incomes) and for retirees who need cash flow and are not borrowing. He owns defensive stocks that happen to pay dividends. What he objects to is buying stocks because of the dividend, and trying to run a borrowing strategy on a portfolio built for income. “I honestly don’t even look at the dividend yield when I’m looking at a stock. I’m looking at the company, its moat, pricing power, is it a monopoly, what’s the free cash flow like.”

The $100 Wallet

His core argument against dividends is an old one in finance, and his image for it is memorable. “If you have $100 in a wallet and you take $4 out, you don’t have $104. You still have $100.” A company’s share price includes the cash it holds. When it pays a dividend, the cash leaves the company and the share price drops by roughly the same amount on the ex-dividend date. You have not received a gain; you have been handed part of your own investment, and taxed on it. “Investors seem to think the money just comes out of thin air.” This is the dividend irrelevance theorem published by Merton Miller and Franco Modigliani in 1961, which he restates without naming.

A High Yield Is a Bribe

He goes further about very high yields. “When you see a 6% or 8% dividend yield, that to me is like a bribe. You should be asking, why are you bribing me?” A company paying out most of its cash is, in effect, saying it has no better use for the money: “I have no idea how to grow this company, so I’ll just give you the money back.” The best companies reinvest (data centers, research, infrastructure) or buy back shares. He quotes a line he attributes to the Buffett school: “Never ask a genius for a dividend.” If a company can compound capital at 30%, you want it to keep the money.

An 8% Dividend vs a Treasury

To make the point sharper he compares a stock yielding 8% against a US Treasury. A company paying 8% out of its cash probably cannot also grow; he assumes the share price erodes about 4% a year as it slowly cannibalizes itself. After a 15% dividend tax and the price erosion, the net return is about 2.8%. The Treasury, taxed at the investor’s 24% federal rate, nets about 3.4%, with no default risk. “A company throwing you an 8% yield can’t even beat a US Treasury.” The Treasury coupon is a legal obligation; the dividend is a board decision that can be cut.

Case Study: $2 Million, 15 Years

His first full comparison gives two investors $2 million each and a need for $100,000 a year. The dividend investor holds stocks yielding 6% with 4% annual price growth. That produces $120,000 of dividends, minus $24,000 of tax at 20%, leaving $96,000, not enough, so they sell shares for the rest. The growth investor holds the Nasdaq-100 at a conservative 10%, receives about $8,000 of dividends, and borrows the remaining $100,000. After fifteen years the dividend investor’s net worth has roughly doubled to about $4.1 million. The growth investor’s net worth is about $6.5 million, with a gross portfolio of $8.3 million.

Case Study: SCHD vs the Nasdaq, 5 Years

The second comparison uses a real dividend ETF, which he calls “the Charles Schwab ETF”: almost certainly the SCHD Schwab US Dividend Equity ETF. Over the five years he looked at, its price rose about 23% against about 120% for the Nasdaq. With $2 million at a 3.5% yield, the dividend investor receives $70,000, pays $10,500 in tax, sells shares to reach $100,000 of spending, and ends with about $2.28 million after paying $55,000 to the IRS over the period. The Nasdaq investor receives about $10,000 of dividends, pays $1,500 in tax, borrows the rest at 5%, never sells, and ends with a gross portfolio of $4.3 million and a net worth of $3.8 million after $500,000 of debt.

The Silent Margin Call

His second dividend video gives the failure a name. He builds a typical wealth-manager portfolio in the Interactive Brokers risk terminal: an all-world fund, AT&T, a high-dividend real estate fund, and a JPMorgan covered-call ETF. Weighted beta 0.53. About $92,000 a year of taxable income on $2 million. A $400,000 loan, 20%. Then he projects it forward ten years. The loan compounds at the interest rate. The portfolio barely grows. Loan-to-value creeps up instead of down. There is no sudden call, just a slow structural decay. “You’ve borrowed a $400,000 loan once and you can’t borrow ever again, because the portfolio hasn’t grown.”

Two further problems compound it. The blended yield, about 4.6%, is roughly the same as a risk-free Treasury, so the investor is taking equity risk for a bond’s return. And the low beta, which was the whole point, turns out to be an “emotional pacifier.” In COVID, the all-world fund fell about 34% while the Nasdaq fell about 28%. “In a true black swan, that beta figure goes out the window.” You get the volatility anyway, without the growth.

“Volatility is the price of admission. You can’t escape it if you want to do the strategy.” — BigBankTheory
The Other Side

The comparisons pick favorable windows. Five years that include the 2020–24 tech run is the best possible period for the Nasdaq against dividend stocks. In 2022, SCHD fell about 3% while the Nasdaq-100 fell 33%. In 2000–02, dividend and value stocks rose while the Nasdaq collapsed.

Buybacks are not tax-free forever. They defer tax (you pay when you sell) and, with the step-up, can avoid it entirely, which is his point. But corporations now pay a 1% excise tax on buybacks, and buybacks at inflated prices destroy value.

Covered-call funds are his strongest target. They cap upside by design while keeping most downside, which really does make them poor collateral for a growth-dependent loan.

What survives: for a borrowing strategy that depends on assets outgrowing a loan, high-yield, low-growth portfolios are structurally unsuitable, and the tax drag of dividends in a taxable account is real.

Chapter 20: The Barbell — Growth Engine Plus Defensive Ballast

Videos: When Can You Borrow for Lifestyle; What a BBD Portfolio Looks Like; Inside a Live BBD Portfolio; What Net Worth Is Required

Here is the problem he sets up in his lifestyle-borrowing video. To beat the hurdle, the portfolio needs volatile growth assets. But volatile assets can crash, and a crash is what triggers a margin call. “How can we beat inflation but also not get margin called?” His answer is a barbell: most of the portfolio in a high-growth engine, the rest in defensive ballast that holds up better in crashes, plus a cash buffer. The word comes from Nassim Taleb, who used it for a portfolio of very safe and very risky assets with nothing in the middle. His version is gentler: growth on one side, steady quality on the other.

The 70/30 Split

His standard allocation is about 70% growth, 30% defensive, with a few percent of that defensive side often in T-bills. The growth side is the Nasdaq-100 or big-tech monopolies. The defensive side is a handful of quality businesses that fall less in crashes. He adjusts the split by stage (Chapter 26): young investors with little wealth hold 100% growth, because “there’s no point in defensive stocks when there’s no wealth to defend yet”; people approaching the borrowing threshold start “pouring the concrete”; and more risk-averse investors can hold more defense.

His Defensive List

In his portfolio video he names the companies he considers genuinely defensive. Walmart, which he says was one of the few large stocks to rise in 2008. Costco. Visa and Mastercard, the payment toll roads. Moody’s and S&P Global, the ratings duopoly. Berkshire Hathaway, which “has practically got its own ecosystem,” with hundreds of billions in cash and its own railroad. Waste Management: “The government can’t just take away the moat of Waste Management. They can’t just make trash disappear.” In the live portfolio he also uses O’Reilly Automotive.

Defensive-Label Traps

Not everything labeled defensive is, he warns. UnitedHealth was classed as an absolute defensive until it collapsed amid regulatory and cost problems. FICO was considered untouchable, but “their moat was given to them by the government, so the government can take it away,” which is what happened when regulators opened mortgage credit scoring to competitors. The lesson: ask where the moat comes from. A moat granted by regulation is fragile. A moat built from physics, habit or network effects is not.

The reverse also holds. ASML is labeled risky tech because its stock is volatile, but it is the only company on earth that makes the extreme-ultraviolet lithography machines every advanced chip requires. “The entire semiconductor industry depends on ASML.” Volatility makes it look risky; its moat makes it one of the most defensible businesses in the world. “Just make a decision on the company.”

Judge Defensives by Owner Earnings

A common objection is that defensive stocks do not keep up with inflation. If a defensive stock rises 4.5% in a year when money grows 7%, surely it has lost ground? He says no, and uses Waste Management to explain. Its stock rose about 4.5% over the year before his video. But its owner earnings, Buffett’s measure of cash available to owners after the spending needed to maintain the business, grew about 15–20%. “The company’s grown; the stock price just hasn’t changed.” “Wall Street decides the price. You decide the value.”

The Coiled Spring

He calls this situation a coiled spring, or “a beach ball held underwater.” When the business grows and the price does not, pressure builds, and eventually the market corrects it. His historical example is Microsoft from 2000 to 2013: the stock went essentially nowhere for thirteen years while revenue roughly tripled. Eventually the price caught up and then some. He adds that Wall Street tends to punish capital spending, because it temporarily reduces free cash flow. But reinvestment is how a company stays competitive. That is why Buffett and Munger preferred owner earnings, which subtract only maintenance capital spending, not growth investment.

The Crash Test

The payoff of the barbell comes in his stress test, covered fully in Chapter 22. With $1.4 million in the Nasdaq and $600,000 in defensives, and a $400,000 loan, he models a Nasdaq crash of 85% alongside a defensive crash of 24%. The portfolio survives with about 40% equity. “I was shocked how much the defensive stocks protect the portfolio, even just having three or four solid defense companies.” The ballast is what lets the growth engine run at full speed without the loan becoming lethal.

They Protect Each Other

In the live-portfolio video he adds a subtler point. “The defensive stocks protect the Nasdaq, and the Nasdaq protects them.” In a true doomsday, everything falls together, including Walmart and Berkshire, and brokers raise margin requirements on individual stocks. But brokers are reluctant to raise requirements on the big index ETFs, because doing so would force selling across the whole market. So in a panic, the ETF sleeve’s margin stays stable while the single-stock sleeve’s may not. Each side covers a different failure mode.

The Other Side

Defensive stocks are not guaranteed to hold up. In 2008 the consumer-staples sector fell about 17% and utilities about 32%. “Defensive” means falling less, not rising.

Valuation undermines defense. By his own margin-of-safety logic, Walmart and Costco at 40–50 times earnings may have more downside than their beta suggests if their multiples compress.

Taleb’s barbell is different. Taleb’s safe side is truly safe (Treasuries, cash), so the worst case is known. A 30% sleeve of defensive stocks still has equity risk. T-bills are the closest thing to Taleb’s version in his portfolios.

What survives: mixing high-growth and low-beta quality businesses meaningfully reduces drawdowns, and judging defensive businesses by cash flow rather than short-term price is sound.

Chapter 21: Asset Quality and the Pricing-Power Filter

Videos: Hardening the Collateral; CPAs Give Terrible Advice; What a BBD Portfolio Looks Like; Margin Call Mathematically Impossible; How NOT to Use Leverage

The last piece of portfolio construction is the one he treats as non-negotiable: the quality of what you borrow against. “What are you invested in? Are you invested in stocks that are blue chip, or are you invested in speculative, overvalued growth stocks?” The same 20% loan is safe against a portfolio of monopolies and reckless against a portfolio of unprofitable small caps. Quality is part of the margin-call math, not separate from it.

One Question

His single filter for a stock is a question: “Can they raise prices without losing a single customer?” “That removes like 80% of companies. It really does. But it’s a good filter.” He borrows the idea from Chris Hohn, founder of the hedge fund TCI, whom he cites as a model: a concentrated, high-conviction investor who only owns businesses with absolute pricing power. Pricing power is what lets a company pass inflation to its customers, which is why these companies soak up new money first (Chapter 7) and why they can outgrow both inflation and a loan.

What Quality Looks Like

Across the videos, the traits he looks for are consistent. Monopoly or near-monopoly position (ASML, Visa, Google Search). High and growing free cash flow, with large net margins; he often notes the Mag 7 run 40% net margins. High return on invested capital. Clean accounting, with no reliance on cheap debt to survive. And resilience to rate hikes: in his rate-rise video he says only “zombie companies,” those that survive only because debt is cheap, are truly destroyed by high rates, while the businesses “that push civilization forward” come through.

Single-Stock Risk

He is candid that borrowing against a single stock is a different risk from borrowing against an index. “I can’t help you with a single stock. How do I know if there’s going to be a DOJ investigation? What if the company is committing fraud? What if the CEO committed a crime?” That idiosyncratic risk is exactly what an index diversifies away. Hence his emphasis on blue chips with clean accounting, and his use of the Nasdaq-100 for anyone not doing deep research. In his leverage video he lists the kind of names he means: Mastercard, Apple, Google, Meta, Broadcom, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon. “They would have to crash about 60% before you get margin called, and they have earnings to protect them.”

Concentration Is Not the Risk

He pushes back hard on the idea that concentration is inherently dangerous. The Gates Foundation trust holds about 80% of its equity portfolio in five companies; Chris Hohn runs a concentrated book. A traditional advisor would call both reckless. “When you really look at it, it’s more likely that everyone they know would go out of business and lose their jobs before any of these companies faced bankruptcy.” He makes the same point about employment: “People say it’s risky to invest in Google, but their own job isn’t risky? The company they work for is more likely to go bankrupt than Google is.”

Sleep-at-Night Holdings

The case study of a leveraged hedge fund blowup (Chapter 25) ends with his summary rule for quality. “You want to hold stocks where you can actually sleep at night. There’s no point in buying highly volatile, high-risk companies that are not predictable. Why play that game?” And then a line worth remembering: “I would rather be right than be right first.” The goal is not to find the next Nvidia before anyone else. It is to own businesses whose earnings will predictably be larger in ten years, and to borrow modestly against them.

The Other Side

Today’s monopolies are yesterday’s too. GE, IBM, Intel, Kodak and Nokia all once had unassailable positions. Pricing power can be lost to technology shifts, regulation (FICO) or antitrust action (Google’s 2024 search ruling).

“60% before a margin call” assumes a diversified basket. Meta fell 76% in 2022, Nvidia 66% in 2022, Amazon 94% in 2000–01. Single mega-caps have exceeded 60% drawdowns repeatedly.

Gates and Hohn are not borrowing. Concentration without leverage can wait out a drawdown. Concentration with leverage may not get the chance.

What survives: quality of collateral really is central to safe borrowing, and a pricing-power test is a simple, durable way to screen for it.

Risk

The part of BBD that frightens people most is the margin call. These chapters work through the math, the liquidity signals, the psychology, and the case studies of people who got it wrong.

Chapter 22: Making a Margin Call Mathematically Impossible

Videos: What If the Market Crashes and You Get Margin Called; What a BBD Portfolio Looks Like; How to Make a Margin Call Mathematically Impossible; Inside a Live BBD Portfolio

He calls the margin call the “second biggest objection” to borrowing against stocks, after “how do you repay it.” His reply starts with a reframe: “A margin call is a management failure. It’s not a market failure.” If the market crashes and you get margin called, “it’s your own fault, because you did not manage your debt wisely.” The simple reason people get called is that they borrowed too much. “A lot of people genuinely cannot tell the difference between investing and gambling.”

The Formula

The math is simple enough to do on a napkin. A broker requires your equity (portfolio minus loan) to stay above some fraction of the portfolio, the maintenance margin, usually 25% for diversified stocks. If the portfolio falls to value V with loan L, you are called when (V − L) ÷ V drops below 25%, which is when V falls to L ÷ 0.75. So the crash required is one minus that level divided by the starting value.

Crash needed for a margin call (25% maintenance) Crash % = 1 − LTV ÷ (1 − maintenance) LTV 10%: 1 − 0.10/0.75 = 86.7% LTV 15%: 1 − 0.15/0.75 = 80.0% LTV 20%: 1 − 0.20/0.75 = 73.3% LTV 30%: 1 − 0.30/0.75 = 60.0% LTV 50%: 1 − 0.50/0.75 = 33.3% LTV 80%: 1 − 0.80/0.75 = already called

His Ladder

He presents the same math as a ladder by portfolio size, because he argues the percentage you borrow should fall as the portfolio grows. A $2 million portfolio with a 20% loan ($400,000) needs a 73% crash, a fall to about $533,000, before a call. A $20 million portfolio borrowing 15% needs an 80% crash, to $4 million. A $200 million portfolio borrowing 10% needs an 86% crash. For comparison, the S&P 500 fell about 57% from its October 2007 peak to its March 2009 low; its worst calendar year was 2008, at about −38.5%. “You would actually still survive a 2008 crash with a 20% margin loan.”

Three Defenses

In the earlier margin-call video he lists three defenses, in order of importance. First, conservative loan-to-value. “If you have a $100,000 portfolio and borrow $80,000, the market doesn’t even need to crash that much. If you borrow $10,000, it could crash 70% and you’re still covered.” Second, a cash reserve outside the account, ready to deposit if the portfolio falls sharply. Crashes of this size take weeks, not minutes, so there is time to add cash. Third, the quality of the collateral: blue chips with clean accounting, not speculative stocks whose single-company risks cannot be modeled.

The 85% Apocalypse Test

In his portfolio video he runs the stress test that convinced him. A $2 million portfolio: $1.4 million in the Nasdaq-100, $600,000 in defensives, a $400,000 loan. Scenario one: Nasdaq −35%, defensives −10%. “Portfolio is still absolutely safe.” Scenario two, “the apocalypse”: Nasdaq −85%, defensives −24%. The Nasdaq sleeve falls from $1.4 million to $210,000. The defensives fall to about $456,000. Gross value: about $666,000. Loan: $400,000. Equity: $266,000, or 40% of the portfolio, still above a 25% maintenance requirement. “I was double-checking, double-checking. How is this possible? It’s because of the defensive stocks.”

House Margin and Index ETFs

The live-portfolio video adds the insight most explanations miss. The 25% maintenance figure is a minimum; brokers can raise their own house requirements at any time, and in a panic they do, especially on individual stocks. “On an absolute doomsday, everyone just sells everything, and the broker will hike the maintenance margin on single stocks.” But he observes that Interactive Brokers kept the major index ETFs at 25% through COVID, because raising margin on the whole market would trigger a death spiral. The one exception he recalls was a temporary rise to about 33% around the 2020 election, announced in advance. “In a doomsday situation, the ETF protects, because its maintenance margin doesn’t go up.”

What the Broker’s Algorithm Thinks

The same video shows the broker’s own risk numbers for his $10 million barbell. Interactive Brokers calculates Value at Risk and Expected Shortfall: the loss it expects not to be exceeded on 99% of days, and the average loss on the worst 1% of days. For that portfolio, the worst expected single-day loss was about 4.5%. “Retail traders have been programmed to believe the stock market is like a casino where it can crash tomorrow. It’s really just not like that.” The broker, whose own money is at risk if your account goes negative, does not price your portfolio as a casino.

“1929 Can’t Happen Again”

He addresses the fear of a 1929-style crash, when US stocks eventually fell about 89%. “1929 can’t happen in 2025.” His reasons: the Fed now uses quantitative easing; deposit insurance prevents the bank-run cascades of the early 1930s; Basel III rules force banks to hold far more capital; and there is simply far more liquidity in the system. “The Federal Reserve wouldn’t allow it.” COVID is his proof: markets crashed, then rallied in the fastest bull run in history as trillions were injected.

Broker vs Private Bank in a Crash

At a discount broker, a margin call is often not a call at all. “A broker is just automatic. Margin call, boom. They don’t even give you a chance.” At private-bank scale, the same loan becomes a securities-backed loan managed by a relationship manager, with more leeway and time to post collateral. He notes in a later video that you pay for that leeway through management fees (Chapter 14). Either way, his rule is the same: keep the loan small enough that neither the algorithm nor the banker ever has a reason to call.

The Other Side

“1929 can’t happen” is strong. With the Fed, deposit insurance and modern capital rules in place, the Nasdaq still fell 83% in 2000–02 and the S&P 57% in 2007–09. Japan’s Nikkei took 34 years (1989–2024) to regain its peak. Deep, long drawdowns are rarer but not impossible.

The 85% test is harsh on defensives, which helps him. In 2000–02, when the Nasdaq fell 83%, many value and defensive stocks rose. The scenario that would hurt more is 2008, when almost everything fell together.

A 99% one-day VaR says nothing about years. 2022’s 33% Nasdaq loss came as a slow grind, never a single catastrophic day.

Brokers do raise margin. During the 2021 meme-stock episode and the 2020 crash, many brokers raised requirements on hundreds of stocks within hours. His index-ETF observation is plausible but not a contractual guarantee.

What survives: at 10–20% loan-to-value on a diversified, quality portfolio, the crash required for a margin call really is far beyond anything the S&P 500 has experienced since the 1930s.

Chapter 23: Liquidity and Strategic De-leveraging

Videos: Repaying the Loan; What If the Market Crashes; What a BBD Portfolio Looks Like; When Rates Rise (supplementary)

Across several videos he repeats a claim that sounds extreme: “Liquidity is all that matters.” “I don’t care how overpriced the S&P 500 is. If liquidity is expanding, it will continue to rise.” Assets rise during liquidity expansion, when rates are low and money is being injected, and fall during liquidity contraction, when the Fed raises rates and drains money from the system. For a borrower, this cycle decides when to borrow more and when to pay down.

COVID: Chaos, and a Bull Market

His favorite illustration is 2020. A pandemic, businesses closed, unemployment around 14%, chaos. “But the stock market rallied. How do you explain that?” Liquidity. The Fed cut rates to zero and bought trillions in bonds; Congress sent stimulus checks. The money supply grew about 25% in a year. “It was the fastest bull run in history.” The same money, spent by households on too few goods, then produced the inflation of 2021–22, which forced the Fed to hike, which drained liquidity and ended the rally. He calls the COVID boom “the greatest wealth transfer in history,” one that “always benefits people that hold assets.”

April 2025: The Tariff Crash

His second example is more recent. In April 2025, after sweeping tariffs were announced, markets fell sharply and people cashed out their 401(k)s in panic. “But if they just looked and tracked liquidity, they would have seen that liquidity was still expanding, and this will recover.” It did, hard: he notes some large stocks rose 30–60% from the lows within months. A crash during liquidity expansion is, in his view, a buying opportunity, not a reason to de-lever.

2022: The Predictable Crash

The crash he says to fear is the other kind. “2022 was a liquidity contraction crash, and it was extremely predictable.” His evidence: “Money market funds were parking their money safely at the Federal Reserve months before the crash.” He is referring to the Fed’s overnight reverse repo facility, where money-market funds can lend cash to the Fed overnight. Balances there rose from near zero in early 2021 to over $1.5 trillion by late 2021, and past $2 trillion in 2022. Cash sitting at the Fed is cash not chasing assets. Then the Fed hiked, the money supply shrank by about $1 trillion (the only real contraction in its modern history), and tech stocks fell more than 30%.

His De-leveraging Rule

The practical rule is stated most clearly in his portfolio video. “When would I actually pay money off the margin? There is one situation, and I’ve thought about this extensively. The only time I would actually pay down is when the Fed hikes rates. That is it.” A hike cycle “reverses the rules”: money contracts, tech falls, and the loan gets more expensive at the same time. “From our assets outside of the portfolio, we’ll begin to pay some cash.” Crucially, he pays down with outside cash, never by selling the portfolio. “As the game changes, we change.”

Two Investors in 2022

His rate-rise video runs the scenario directly. Two investors enter 2022 with the same BBD portfolio. Rates rise, the loan gets expensive, stocks fall. Investor A panics: the strategy has failed, so they sell stock to pay off the loan, paying capital gains tax and permanently shrinking the portfolio. Investor B does not sell. Their businesses are still earning, still generating cash. The pain lasts about as long as the hiking cycle. When rates eventually fall, B’s portfolio recovers fully and the loan cost drops. “The Fed hiking rates is not an extinction-level threat. It is annoying. It is more of a nuisance.”

“We do not fear the Fed. We do not fight the Fed. And when your portfolio is structured the right way, when the rates do cut, we’re already going to be way ahead.” — BigBankTheory

Why He Thinks Hikes Won’t Last

His confidence that hiking cycles are short comes from the debt. With roughly $40 trillion of federal debt, high rates become unaffordable quickly, because interest costs compound. “We’re past the days of the Volcker era,” he says, referring to the early 1980s when the Fed held rates near 20% to break inflation. “Everything in the American system wants low interest rates.” And when rates are cut, “the strategy gets amplified like double steroids”: the portfolio rises as cheap money returns and the loan cost falls at the same time.

How to Track Liquidity (Filling His Gap)

He repeatedly promises a video on exactly how he tracks liquidity, but none of the videos in this guide shows his method. The widely used public version is a net liquidity measure built from three Federal Reserve series, all free on FRED: the Fed’s total balance sheet, minus the Treasury General Account (the government’s cash at the Fed), minus the reverse repo facility. When the balance sheet grows, or cash flows out of the Treasury account and reverse repo into markets, net liquidity rises. Analysts pair it with the direction of the Fed funds rate, M2 growth, and credit spreads. Treat this as a research-based fill-in, not his system.

The Other Side

“Extremely predictable” is hindsight. In late 2021 the Fed itself was still calling inflation transitory. The reverse repo balance was also rising for technical reasons (a glut of bank deposits and too few T-bills), not only as a warning signal.

Liquidity is not all that matters. The Nasdaq peaked in March 2000 while the Fed was only modestly tightening; valuations did most of the damage.

The hike cycle was longer than he says. The Fed raised rates from March 2022 to July 2023 and did not cut until September 2024, about two and a half years of high rates, not fourteen months.

What survives: rate-hiking cycles are genuinely the worst environment for leveraged equity investors, and paying down with outside cash instead of selling is a sensible rule.

Chapter 24: The Psychology

Videos: BBD vs FIRE; Why I Never Sell; Repaying the Loan; Margin Called; When Can You Retire; Stop Using the S&P 500; The Silent Margin Call

Every one of his videos, whatever its topic, eventually turns to psychology. He believes the strategy fails far more often in the investor’s head than in the spreadsheet. “Finance and numbers are just logical. It’s just math. But money is more psychology than anything else.” This chapter collects the psychological ideas scattered across the videos, because they are as much a part of his system as the loan ratios.

Volatility Is Not Risk

His most repeated line is “volatility is not risk.” He defines risk instead as permanent loss of purchasing power. “If you have $100 in cash, it has zero volatility but maximum risk, because there’s guaranteed loss of purchasing power.” A great business whose price swings 30% in a year is, in his definition, less risky than cash, because over time it compounds while cash erodes. He compares currency printing to a company that keeps issuing new shares every month: “You would be very unhappy. It’s the exact same when it’s applied to a currency.”

Risk Is a Behavior

The partner line is “risk is a behavior, not a tool.” “You can’t just say margin loans are risky. Risk is the individual’s behavior.” Most people given a credit card will max it out; some pay it off every month. Most people given a margin account will borrow too much; some borrow 20% and sleep soundly. The tool is the same. He adds a wry observation: “The people that are terrified to get margin called are the ones that definitely won’t.” Fear, in moderation, is protective.

Knowledge Is Peace of Mind

“Financial peace of mind is not a paid-off mortgage,” he says, taking a swipe at the Ramsey school. “True financial peace of mind is financial knowledge.” People fear what they do not understand, and most people do not understand the stock market, so they call it gambling. “The more you learn, the less fear you will have.” His broader claim is that “the wealth gap is a knowledge gap.” He adds that only a small fraction of people genuinely understand investing, which is why seeing a broker’s risk model or a spreadsheet stress test can dissolve fears that years of news coverage created.

Scarcity vs Abundance

He frames most objections to BBD as mindset rather than math. “It’s not a financial debate. It’s a worldview debate.” The scarcity mindset sees debt as a threat, the future as something to be feared, and money as something never to be spent. The abundance mindset sees debt as a tool and assets as something to be structured. His sharpest examples come from the FIRE and Ramsey worlds: people with $16 million too frightened to spend it, callers worth millions asking permission to buy a car, a Reddit post from someone with $70 million unsure whether they could retire. “It’s never enough.”

Selling Winners Is the Same Fear as Selling Losers

One of his more insightful observations: the fear that makes people sell a winner is the same fear that makes them sell a loser, pointed in the opposite direction. “When a stock goes up 20, 30, 40%, they think, if I don’t sell now, what if it goes down and I lose all the money I could have sold it for?” Peter Lynch called this “cutting the flowers and watering the weeds.” Buffett admits selling too early was among his biggest mistakes. He notes that social media is full of posts saying “if you’d invested $X in Nvidia you’d have $Y,” but almost nobody actually held through.

Close Your Eyes

His practical advice for handling volatility is almost comically simple: stop looking. For Renee, the doctor in Chapter 29: “We just get ready to close her eyes and not look at that QQQ. It’s volatile, but she’ll get used to it after five to seven years.” The cash buffer (T-bills and rental income) is what makes this possible: because her living costs are covered for years, a crash has no effect on her life. In the FIRE video he pictures the BBD retiree on a beach during a crash: “They probably don’t even know the market crashed.”

The Nominal Illusion

The deepest psychological trap, in his framework, is the nominal illusion: judging wealth by the number of dollars rather than by what those dollars can buy. “The masses are pacified by the nominal illusion. They’re all trapped staring at the numbers on a screen.” It is why Argentines understood their stock market boom was fake while Americans do not notice a slower version. It is why a house that doubled feels like a win. “Wealth is about the weight”: shares owned, ounces of gold, a stake in a business, not the number next to them.

The Other Side

Volatility is risk for a borrower. For an unleveraged holder with a long horizon, his definition is fair. For someone with a loan, volatility is exactly what triggers a margin call; his own Chapters 19 and 22 are about managing it. Volatility also matters for anyone who might need to sell at the wrong time.

Fear is sometimes information. Some of the frugality he mocks is rational caution by people without pensions facing long-tail health costs. Behavioral finance shows most investors overestimate their tolerance for losses until they experience them.

What survives: the distinction between volatility and permanent loss, the reality of the nominal illusion, and the observation that most investing mistakes are behavioral are all well supported by research.

Chapter 25: How NOT to Use Leverage

Videos: How NOT to Use Leverage (supplementary); The Silent Margin Call; BBD vs FIRE

He thinks people treat leverage as all or nothing. “They’re either levered up to their eyeballs, or they don’t use it at all because they’re terrified to get margin called.” His answer is conservative leverage in between, and the best way to understand it is to see what the “eyeballs” end of the spectrum looks like.

Case Study: The Hedge Fund Liquidation

In a supplementary video he analyzes a hedge fund whose manager he names as “Leopold Ashworth.” This guide could not verify the name or the fund, so treat the details as his account rather than established fact. He loads the fund’s disclosed long positions into the Interactive Brokers risk terminal. Net liquidation value on the longs: about $896 million. Maintenance margin required: $2.5 billion. Leverage: about 4×, a loan-to-value around 75%. Net exposure: 558%. Weighted beta: 3. Multiply the two and a 1% fall in the S&P 500 implies roughly a 17% fall in the portfolio. He controlled more than $5 billion of stock with less than $1 billion of equity.

He was concentrated in some of the most volatile names in the market, including SanDisk and Bloom Energy, and the collapse of the memory-chip trade (which hit South Korea’s market hard) did the rest. He was fully liquidated: the fund’s long book went to zero holdings. What struck him most was the prior year. The manager had made about 2,000%, sat on $1.46 billion of profit, and “was supposedly a genius.” The winning streak created a false sense of security. “The market just has to sneeze and you’re margin called.”

The Incentive Problem

His explanation is incentives. “You would only do this if it wasn’t your own money.” A hedge fund manager collects management and performance fees every year. After a 2,000% year, those fees were hundreds of millions, already banked. If the fund later blows up, the outside investors who joined late take the loss. “They’re incentivized to go for broke because it’s not their money. No human would do that with their own money.” The lesson for an individual is that the leverage you see in the professional world is often set by incentives you do not share.

What Conservative Looks Like

Against that he sets his own rules. For lifestyle borrowing, about 20% loan-to-value once the portfolio is large enough. For buying more stock, about 30% debt against equity. $100,000 of stock plus $30,000 borrowed buys $130,000 of stock, a loan-to-value of 23%. At 5.1%, the loan costs about $80 a month; dividends offset part of it. “In the normal world, no one is going to give you $30,000 of assets for $80 a month.” It only works on big, cash-rich monopolies, held, not traded. And even if the stocks are flat for a year, “you are still making intrinsic value,” because the earnings keep compounding underneath.

Other Ways to Get It Wrong

The other videos add three more failure modes. Borrowing against low-growth assets, the silent margin call of Chapter 19: loan-to-value rises slowly for a decade until the strategy stops working. Borrowing against the wrong entry price: in the FIRE video he describes dot-com buyers paying 75 times forward earnings for companies with no revenue, who “deserved to get an 80% crash” because they had no idea what valuation meant. And borrowing on a retail platform at 8–12% interest, where the loan can never be outgrown.

Chapter 26: Escape Velocity and the Net-Worth Road Map

Videos: When Can You Borrow for Lifestyle; What Net Worth Is Required for BBD (USA Only)

He draws “a line in the sand” about who can and cannot use the strategy, and he draws it by net worth. The road map matters because using the right tool at the wrong stage is one of the easiest ways to fail. “The strategy truly rewards people that are proactive in planning ahead, because the system truly punishes late, reactive planning.” The concept that ties the stages together is escape velocity: the point at which a portfolio compounds faster than you could ever feed it from your salary.

Escape Velocity

Once a portfolio is compounding faster than you can add to it, your labor stops being the main driver of your wealth. He puts the threshold at about $2 million in one video and $1–2 million in another, depending on what the portfolio holds. A $2 million portfolio growing 15% adds $300,000 a year, more than most people could save. He argues this happens at every income level: someone earning $100,000 and someone earning $1 million, each investing 20% of income, both eventually see the portfolio out-earn them. “Your stock portfolio has the mathematical potential to outperform your physical labor.”

The Dynasty Math

To show why the early stage matters, he runs a compounding example. Start with $100,000, add $1,000 a month, and it takes about 22 years to reach $2 million at 10%, or 19 years at 12%. Then stop contributing entirely and leave it alone for sixty years. At 10% it becomes about $608 million. At 12% it becomes about $1.8 billion. “Look how much difference just 2% makes.” The sixty years is deliberate: nobody lives to see it, but their children and grandchildren do. That is the scale at which the step-up and the loan become a dynasty strategy.

Who Qualifies

He adds a behavioral qualification to the numbers. “If you are someone in the $1–2 million sector, I know for a fact you did not get there without financial discipline.” The people who misuse margin, he argues, are rarely the ones who reached seven figures slowly. “The people with no money are the ones gambling with margin.” Those who have built the engine have already shown the discipline the strategy needs; for them, it is about “preserving it, keeping it, and building generational wealth.” Everyone else can use it as “motivation, inspiration, and a plan for the future.”

The Other Side

Sequence matters. Reaching $2 million just before a 2000- or 2008-style crash and starting to borrow immediately is very different from reaching it in 2009. The threshold should be judged after a stress test, not just a balance.

Human capital counts too. A 40-year-old surgeon with $800,000 has a very different risk capacity from a 70-year-old retiree with $800,000. His thresholds ignore income, job security and age.

What survives: refusing to borrow for lifestyle until the portfolio is large enough to absorb shocks and outgrow the loan is prudent, and the emphasis on building the engine first is exactly right.

Chapter 27: Funding Lifestyle — Credit Card Float and Outside Credit

Video: Margin Loan vs Credit Card: How Family Offices Float Cash

This is a small, practical video, and it reveals how he thinks about the margin line. “In every finance niche, they view credit cards and debt as the enemy. It’s only the enemy for the wrong people.” His question is simpler: if a family needs to spend $10,000 a month, where should that money come from so that the portfolio is disturbed least? His answer is that the margin loan is not the first choice.

The Margin Line Is the Last Line of Defense

“If we are using margin for everything, we are putting our portfolio on the line, and we want it to be the last resort.” Every dollar borrowed on margin raises the portfolio’s loan-to-value and starts accruing interest immediately. He wants the margin line kept unused, or lightly used, for two purposes: as a last line of defense for real emergencies, and as dry powder to buy assets in a crash or to bridge a real estate purchase. “If the market crashed, there is margin there if you want to buy more assets at very attractive valuations.”

The Credit Card Float

So he routes day-to-day spending through a credit card instead. A card gives 30–45 days of interest-free float between purchase and due date, and cash back on top. Margin charges interest from the first day. In his example family office, the portfolio holds growth stocks, defensive dividend payers and T-bills. The family spends $10,000 a month on the card. The dividends and T-bill coupons pay the card off in full each month. The portfolio stays 100% invested, the margin line stays untouched, and because of the cash back, the arrangement is a small net gain over borrowing on margin.

$120,000 of annual spending, two ways On margin at 5%: interest ~$3,300/yr, LTV rises On card, paid off: interest $0 cash back 1.5–2% = +$1,800–2,400/yr LTV unchanged

The card only wins if it is paid in full every month. Carry a balance at 25% APR and it becomes the worst debt in the household.

Outside Credit Before Portfolio Credit

The broader principle extends beyond cards. “If you’ve ever got an option to use credit from someone else, and it’s cheaper than the rate of inflation, a competitive APR, and it’s secured against another asset rather than your portfolio, that’s far more advantageous.” This is how he says wealthy families buy cars, boats and yachts: financed against the thing itself, not the family portfolio. “Technically, the cars and the boats are all liabilities. Rather than using the portfolio to fund them, you would much rather the collateral was on whatever it is you were buying.” The family wealth stays insulated from the family’s toys.

When Margin Fills the Gap

Margin still has a role. If the family overspends in a month and income does not cover the card, a small margin draw bridges it. If a genuine lifestyle event comes up (a wedding, a medical bill, a house deposit), that is “what the margin is for: quick access to cash without selling the assets.” And he allows that someone in the late years of life could reasonably fund everything from margin, because their horizon is short and the step-up is close. In his example, the family’s portfolio keeps growing (he assumes 12% for the Nasdaq sleeve and 8% for the defensive sleeve) while covering their lifestyle, with no interest paid.

The Other Side

Behavior is the risk. He admits it: studies consistently find people spend more with cards than with cash. The float only helps someone who genuinely pays in full every month.

The income has to exist. The example works because dividends and T-bill coupons cover the spending. A pure growth portfolio yielding 0.5% generates little income to pay a card. In that case, the card is just a 30-day delay before a margin draw.

What survives: using the interest-free float, keeping the margin line unused as a reserve, and financing depreciating purchases against themselves rather than the portfolio are all sensible habits.

Chapter 28: Buy Borrow Die vs FIRE

Video: Why Buy Borrow Die Utterly Destroys the FIRE Movement

The FIRE movement (Financial Independence, Retire Early) teaches people to save aggressively, invest in index funds, and retire once the portfolio can support a withdrawal of about 4% a year. He calls it “almost like a cult following.” Its followers, in his telling, “become a slave to a spreadsheet,” eat “rice and beans, beans and rice,” view debt as a moral failure, and feel morally superior to people who enjoy life. His objection is not to the discipline, which he respects, but to the result: a life of permanent monitoring. “You can actually do both.”

The Setup: Two People, $2 Million

He runs a head-to-head. Two people, both 60, both with $2 million, both retiring for the rest of their lives. The FIRE retiree holds a blend of 401(k) and Roth IRA and withdraws 4% a year, adjusted for inflation: $80,000 a year, about $6,600 a month gross, about $6,286 net. He notes that US households spend roughly $6,500 a month on average, so this retiree will survive, but carefully. At 90 the portfolio is worth about $16 million, money they will never spend because they are too afraid to.

The BBD retiree splits the $2 million in half. $1 million goes into a 30-year Treasury yielding 5.05%, a fixed, guaranteed income for thirty years. He prefers it to dividends because a Treasury coupon is a legal obligation, while a dividend is a board decision. The other $1 million goes into a portfolio of 80% Nasdaq-100 (assumed 15% a year) and 20% defensive stocks (8%). The goal is $10,000 a month after tax, rising with inflation.

How the BBD Retiree Funds $10,000 a Month

The Treasury pays about $50,500 a year; after federal tax, about $3,800 a month. The rest of each month’s $10,000 comes from a margin loan on the growth portfolio. Because it is a loan, it is not taxed. He deliberately stress-tests with an expensive 6.5% margin rate and assumes the Fed never cuts again. A sophisticated borrower using box spreads might pay 3.75%. Loan-to-value starts tiny (3%, 6%, 9%) and climbs to a peak of about 24–25%, then falls as the growth portfolio’s compounding turns exponential. By 90 the retiree is borrowing about $200,000 a year to keep up the inflation-adjusted $10,000 a month.

The Crash Asymmetry

The part of the comparison he finds most important is not the ending net worth but what happens in a crash. The FIRE retiree has no other source of money. If the market falls 30–50%, they must sell shares at the bottom to live, locking in losses and shrinking the base that has to recover. The BBD retiree has guaranteed bond income, has already drawn the year’s cash, and refuses to sell. “They’re just chilling on a beach. They probably don’t even know the market crashed.” Next year they borrow again, and often the market has recovered. This is the classic problem of sequence-of-returns risk, and he argues BBD handles it better than the 4% rule.

A Cold Start

He stresses that his example is a cold start: someone beginning to invest at 60 with no taxable portfolio. “Think about if they just started at 40.” Someone who starts a taxable brokerage account at 40 arrives at 60 with a portfolio so large that borrowing $10,000 a month is about 1% of its value. The whole trajectory of their life changes. This connects to the road map in Chapter 26 and the capital-location argument in Chapter 16: the time to fund the taxable account is decades before you need to borrow against it.

The Dot-Com Objection

He anticipates the objection to putting a retiree’s money in the Nasdaq: the dot-com crash. His response is that 1999 buyers were gambling, paying about 75 times forward earnings with no understanding of valuation. “They deserved to get an 80% crash.” He also points out that the QQQ ETF launched in 1999, so its history begins with the worst possible entry point, “like an Olympic sprinter who slips when the gun goes off, and people never forget it.” Refusing to own the Nasdaq because of 2000, he says, is “like refusing to fly in a commercial airliner because one crashed before.”

The Other Side

15% for thirty years is the whole result. Rerun the BBD side at a more conservative 10% for the Nasdaq and the ending net worth falls sharply, and loan-to-value peaks much higher. At 7–8%, with 6.5% interest, the strategy can fail.

The bond is not inflation-proof. A 30-year Treasury paying a fixed $50,500 loses purchasing power every year; by year 30, at 3% inflation, it buys about 40% of what it did at the start. That is why the margin draws must grow so fast.

The comparison mixes accounts. The FIRE retiree’s money is in a 401(k)/Roth; the BBD retiree’s is in a taxable account. Pulling money out of a traditional 401(k) to fund BBD would trigger a large income-tax bill that the example skips.

The FIRE retiree can also hold bonds. A 4% rule portfolio with 30–40% in bonds handles crashes much better than the all-equity version he describes.

What survives: having a guaranteed income floor so that you never sell in a crash, and borrowing rather than selling in down years, are genuinely strong answers to sequence risk.

Chapter 29: Case Study — Renee, the $7.5 Million Doctor

This is his most complete worked example, and the best single demonstration of what he means by “architecture.” It starts with a real call to The Ramsey Show. A 47-year-old doctor from San Jose, California, whom he calls Renee, asks how to find the motivation to work another thirteen years, until 60, so she can get free lifetime health insurance and start her pension five years early. She has never married, has no children, and supports her parents, who are in their eighties. Her passion is music: violin and piano. Her job leaves no time for it.

You cannot negotiate with math.” She is so used to hospital hours that she thinks 50 to 60 hours a week is part-time.

“Free” Health Care Isn’t Free

The first thing he attacks is the premise. “Is anything really free? It’s definitely not if you have to work 50,000 hours for it.” Thirteen more years of full-time medicine is the price of the “free” insurance. The alternative is to buy top-tier private coverage herself. He estimates about $24,000–25,000 a year, and about $425,000 in total over thirteen years including medical inflation. Against a net worth of $7.5 million, that is a solvable line item, not a reason to keep working.

Engine 1: Rentals Into a Delaware Statutory Trust

Next, the two rental houses. After property tax, insurance, maintenance, plumbers, boilers and tenants, he estimates their true net yield at 0.5–1%, and they demand her time. Selling them outright in California would trigger a huge capital gains bill: he estimates about $670,000 (in the companion video, up to $900,000), several years of her income. So he uses a 1031 exchange into a Delaware Statutory Trust, covered in detail in Chapter 30. The houses are sold, the proceeds go to a qualified intermediary in escrow (“the ground is lava,” the money must never touch her account), and are reinvested into a fractional interest in institutional property, such as an Amazon distribution center.

At a 5% yield, $2.5 million produces about $125,000 a year, fully passive. Because commercial real estate generates large depreciation deductions, he estimates about 60% of that income is sheltered as a paper loss, leaving roughly $110,000 after tax, equivalent to about a $160,000 salary. “We have nearly found Renee her annual income doing zero hours a week.” The DST’s rents also rise with inflation, about 2–3% a year.

Engine 2: T-Bills and the Nasdaq

The $1.6 million taxable brokerage becomes “the saving grace.” He splits it: about $1.33 million in T-bills, paying roughly $49,000 a year, and the rest in the Nasdaq-100 (QQQ). The T-bills are chosen for two reasons. They barely move in price, unlike a 10-year note. And Treasury interest is exempt from state income tax, so California, with its top rate above 13%, cannot touch it. In the companion video he uses a 10-year Treasury at 4.35% on half the account instead, producing about $34,800 a year; either way the principle is the same. Total passive income: roughly $146,000–150,000 a year, more than her $131,000 take-home from working.

The QQQ sleeve is where the growth lives, and it is also where the psychology lives. “We just get ready to close her eyes and not look at that QQQ. It’s volatile, but she’ll get used to it after five to seven years of not even looking at it.” She can afford not to look because the T-bills and the DST cover her life. The two sleeves also hedge each other: if the Fed cuts rates, T-bill income falls, but cheaper money lifts tech stocks. “It’s a perfect hedge.”

Engine 3: The Roth Conversion Ladder

The $2.1 million in 401(k)s is the third engine. As a working doctor in California, her marginal tax rate was about 37% federal plus about 13% state, around half of each extra dollar. Once she stops working, her taxable income collapses. That opens a window for a Roth conversion ladder: each year, move a slice of the 401(k) into a Roth IRA, paying income tax at her new, low bracket. Over about fifteen years the 401(k) is siphoned into the Roth, which is also invested in the Nasdaq-100, and from then on it grows and can be withdrawn tax-free. In the companion video he projects the 401(k) at about $2.8 million and the Roth at about $2.7 million by the end of thirteen years.

Engine 4: BBD Switches On

Only after five to seven years does the Buy Borrow Die part begin. By then, if the QQQ has performed near its history, the sleeve has grown to around $750,000. She can take a small margin loan against it, about $28,000 a year, to raise her income further. In the companion version, the loan funds her private health insurance, and by year thirteen the debt is only about 8.6% of the QQQ sleeve. After the conversions end, around age 61, her cash flow rises again because she is no longer paying tax on the conversions. The projected result is a net worth over $10.7 million in thirteen years, excluding her home, versus working 50,000 more hours.

Renee’s architecture

DST (from rentals): ~$125K/yr, 60% sheltered by depreciation.

T-bills: ~$49K/yr, state-tax exempt.

QQQ: growth, untouched for 5–7 years, then light margin.

401(k) → Roth ladder: tax paid at low brackets, tax-free growth after.

Health insurance: ~$25K/yr, paid, not earned with 50,000 hours.

Result: ~$146–150K/yr of income with zero working hours.

His conclusion is that structure beats income. “It’s all about the architecture. There is always a solution.” “Borrowing a debt that costs 4% against an asset growing at 15% is prudent, intelligent financial management.” And the objection he expects (“what if the market crashes right after she moves money into QQQ?”) is answered by the design itself: “That’s why we’ve got the cash buffer.”

The Other Side

DSTs have serious drawbacks. They are illiquid (typical holds of 5–10 years with no early exit), carry high upfront fees and loads (often 5–10% or more), depend on a single sponsor, are restricted to accredited investors, and cannot raise new capital or renegotiate leases. A 5% yield is not guaranteed.

A 1031 exchange only defers the tax. The deferred gain and depreciation recapture follow the DST. The real exit is the step-up at death.

Health insurance before Medicare is complicated. Before 65 she would likely buy coverage on the ACA marketplace, where subsidies depend on income. Roth conversions raise her reported income and can reduce or eliminate those subsidies. $25,000 a year may also be optimistic for a comprehensive California plan at 50–60.

The pension is ignored. Retiring at 47 forfeits or reduces the pension she was working toward; its value should be in the comparison.

What survives: she very likely could retire, and the core insight (rearranging existing assets to generate income, sheltering it from tax, and never selling growth assets in a panic) is exactly the kind of advice she needed and did not get.

Chapter 30: Real Estate — The Reality Check and the 1031/DST Play

Videos: When Can You Borrow for Lifestyle; CPAs Give Terrible Advice; Repaying the Loan, Part 2; When Can You Retire; Inside a Live BBD Portfolio; When Rates Rise

Real estate is where his views are most contrarian, because most people think of property as the classic borrowed-money wealth builder. He does not dismiss it entirely, but he holds it to the same money-supply hurdle as everything else, and most property fails. “People think all real estate goes up. This is again another lie.”

Most Property Just Matches the Money Supply

His central claim is that ordinary real estate merely tracks money growth. “You have people that have real estate, and they think, oh, it went up 10% this year. It’s actually just kept up with the money supply. More dollars were issued into the economy. All the assets have gone up the same.” Only prime real estate, in places where new supply is physically impossible, outruns it. He names central London (Mayfair, Zone 1), prime Manhattan, Monaco, Miami Beach. “There’s only like 5% of properties in the world that actually outperform the money supply.”

Real Estate Is an Entropic Asset

In the rate-rise video he adds a physical argument. “All buildings are subject to the second law of thermodynamics. The moment a house is finished, nature begins to destroy it.” The roof decays, pipes corrode, boilers fail. The owner pays capital expenditure, insurance and property taxes forever. “Structurally, it is a depreciating asset.” Stocks in software-heavy businesses, by contrast, are “productive capital compounders” that do not need to be re-roofed. The land may appreciate; the building is always decaying.

Take Away Cheap Debt

He says real-estate gurus “come out in full force” when rates are low, because cheap debt makes almost any property look good. “When you take cheap debt out of the picture completely and look at property for what it truly is,” the returns look much weaker. His estimate for a typical rental after all costs is a net yield of 0.5–1%, which is why he advises Renee to exit hers. He also mentions, in the title of one video, that the wealthy are liquidating short-term rentals, and in another that he considers all real estate, on its own terms, to be a liability rather than an asset.

Prime vs Growth Stocks Since 1996

In the live-portfolio video he compares prime real estate with the Nasdaq. Since about 1996, he says, Manhattan prices rose about 500%, prime London about 1,000–2,000%, Monaco over 1,000%. The Nasdaq rose about 6,400%. His point is that the people who can afford prime property today are disproportionately those who held growth stocks, because tech “gets the money first” when it floods the economy. “You deserve to be able to buy prime real estate. If your assets do not go up fast enough, you will never be able to buy the assets you truly want.”

The 1031 Exchange

For someone who already owns investment property, his favored exit is the 1031 exchange, named after section 1031 of the tax code. If you sell investment real estate and reinvest the proceeds in “like-kind” real estate, you can defer the capital gains tax and depreciation recapture. The rules are strict. The proceeds must go to a qualified intermediary; if you take possession of the cash, the exchange fails. You must identify replacement property within 45 days of the sale and close within 180 days. And the replacement must be investment real estate of equal or greater value, with equal or greater debt, to defer all of the gain.

The Delaware Statutory Trust

The problem for someone like Renee is that a 1031 exchange normally means buying another property to manage. A Delaware Statutory Trust solves that. A DST is a trust, sponsored by a real estate firm, that owns one or more institutional properties: distribution centers, apartment complexes, medical offices. Investors buy fractional beneficial interests, which the IRS treats as direct real estate ownership for 1031 purposes (Revenue Ruling 2004-86). The investor gets a share of the rent and the depreciation, and the sponsor handles everything else. “That is truly passive, because it’s in the trust.”

How Family Offices Buy Property

One video title in his catalog describes how a family office “weaponizes Lombard loans” to buy real estate, and the idea appears throughout. Instead of selling stock to buy a property, the family borrows against its portfolio to fund the purchase or bridge it until a mortgage is arranged. The Other Side

Leveraged real estate has made many fortunes. With a 20% down payment, a house that rises 4% a year returns about 20% a year on the equity before costs. His “just matches M2” framing ignores that most property is bought with a fixed-rate mortgage that inflation erodes, exactly the debt dynamic he praises elsewhere.

Rental yields vary. 0.5–1% net is plausible for a San Jose house bought long ago at today’s value; in lower-cost markets net yields of 4–6% are common.

Housing is also consumption. A primary residence provides shelter you would otherwise rent. Comparing it only with the Nasdaq omits that.

What survives: most real estate returns are driven by leverage and inflation rather than real growth; landlording is costly in time; and the 1031-to-DST route is a legitimate way to exit active management without triggering a tax bill.

Chapter 31: Beyond Margin — Private Credit, Mezzanine Debt and Business Owners

Videos: How to Make a Margin Call Mathematically Impossible; 97% of the Stock Market

Near the end of his margin-call video he describes what very large investors do next. At around $200 million, borrowing 10% of the portfolio, the crash needed for a margin call is 86%. At that scale, he says, many investors “move away from the stock market and move into private credit.” The reason is the math of the loan. Larger borrowers get cheaper margin (the spread over SOFR shrinks), and interest on money borrowed to invest can be deductible, so the after-tax cost of their debt falls, in his example to about 2.6%. A lower cost of debt means a lower hurdle, and it opens up a different kind of asset.

What Mezzanine Debt Is

Private credit is lending to companies directly, outside banks and public bond markets. Mezzanine debt is a layer of it that sits between senior bank loans and equity: it gets repaid after the bank but before the shareholders, and charges accordingly. He says mezzanine lenders charge 15–18%. “You might think, why on earth would someone accept an 18% loan? The business must have a terrible credit score.” The answer, he says, is that for many excellent businesses it is the cheapest money available.

Why a Great Business Pays 18%

Imagine a business with strong cash flow and a cash return on invested capital (CROIC) of about 37%: invest $6.5 million and it throws off $2.4 million a year. It wants to grow, and it wants as much capital as it can get, because each dollar earns 37 cents a year. It goes to its bank, which is heavily regulated and will lend only about two to three times EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization). It could raise equity instead, but that means giving up ownership, which he says in his Shark Tank video can be “up to 900% more expensive than a bank loan.” So it borrows the rest from a private credit fund at 15–18%, knowing that money invested at 37% more than pays for itself. “It can be a win-win for both.”

Why 18% debt can be cheap Business return on new capital: 37% Mezzanine interest: 18% Spread kept by the owner: 19% a year Equity alternative: give away 25% of a company worth $30M = $7.5M of value, forever

Debt has a fixed cost that ends when it is repaid; equity gives up a share of every future dollar. For a business growing fast, that share is the most expensive money there is.

How Retail Investors Get Exposure

He notes that ordinary investors can buy exposure to private credit through listed firms, usually business development companies (BDCs), some yielding close to 10%. This is one place where he makes an exception to his rule against chasing dividends. BDCs are legally required to distribute most of their taxable income (at least 90%, under the rules for regulated investment companies), so the yield is the business model, not a warning sign. But he warns about credit risk: the one he shows fell sharply in 2008 and 2020. When borrowers default, these vehicles fall with them.

Business Owners: Reinvest or Invest?

The 97% video ends with a point aimed at business owners. Large public companies earn lower returns on capital as they grow: “The larger they get, the smaller the return on invested capital actually becomes.” A small, focused business can earn very high returns. So an owner should compare the return on the next dollar invested in their own business with what the market offers, and put the dollar wherever it earns more. If the business earns 40% on new capital, reinvest. If it earns 8%, the market (or the Nasdaq) is probably the better home. His catalog includes several videos on owners who destroy wealth by scaling, franchising or discounting, all variations on this theme.

The Other Side

Private credit has boomed, and booms attract risk. The asset class grew past $1.5 trillion by 2024, attracting concerns from regulators about opaque valuations, rising payment-in-kind interest and weaker lending standards.

BDC yields are not free money. Distributions can be cut, many trade below net asset value in downturns, and their income is taxed as ordinary income, not at dividend rates.

The 2.6% after-tax figure depends on the deduction rules described in Chapter 16: the loan must fund taxable investments, and the deduction is limited to net investment income.

What survives: the logic that high-return businesses rationally pay high rates, the comparison of internal returns with market returns for owners, and the observation that the wealthy diversify into lending once their equity base is large.

Chapter 32: The Dynasty — Step-Up, Heirs, Trusts and Education

Videos: Repaying the Loan, Parts 1 and 2; What Net Worth Is Required; What a BBD Portfolio Looks Like

The last word in Buy Borrow Die is “die,” and he treats it as the purpose rather than an afterthought. “This is how generational wealth is built.” He speaks of it in almost moral terms: “We are fulfilling our purpose.” “You could be the first one to change the whole bloodline.” His portfolio video ends with a picture of the grandchildren “chilling on a yacht in Monaco, thankful that we used the strategy.” Underneath the rhetoric is a concrete mechanism with three parts: the step-up, the choice heirs face, and structures that make the loop outlast one generation.

The Step-Up in Practice

When the owner dies, every asset in the taxable account gets a new cost basis equal to its market value that day. Decades of gains disappear for income-tax purposes. In community-property states such as California, a surviving spouse can receive a step-up on the entire community property, not just the deceased spouse’s half. The estate then repays the margin loan, either by selling some now-untaxed assets or with a life insurance payout, and the rest passes to heirs. Federal estate tax applies only above the exemption, $15 million per person from 2026 (so $30 million for a married couple), under the 2025 tax law.

What the Heirs Do

He describes two paths for heirs. They can sell enough stock to repay the loan, paying no capital gains tax thanks to the step-up, and keep the rest. Or, “if they’re well educated in the strategy,” they can keep the loan and continue the loop: hold the portfolio, borrow against it, never sell, and pass it on again. This is how dynasty wealth compounds across generations instead of being liquidated at each death. His compounding example (Chapter 26), $2 million left for sixty years becoming $600 million to $1.8 billion, is really an argument for this second path.

Education Is the Most Important Part

He is emphatic that the structure is not enough. “The most important thing is the education to the children. If the children understand the strategy, they can just carry it on.” The old saying that family fortunes go “shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations” describes exactly what happens when heirs inherit assets without the understanding to keep them. The step-up and a trust can pass on the money. Only education passes on the discipline: not selling, borrowing modestly, measuring against the money supply, and owning businesses with pricing power.

Trusts (Filling His Gap)

He mentions trusts repeatedly (an irrevocable trust for asset protection and dynasty building, a portfolio held in a trust “alongside your own”) but says each time that it is “for a different video.” One of his videos asks whether you need a trust for BBD at all. The research-based summary is this. A revocable living trust avoids probate but changes nothing for tax; assets still get a step-up. An irrevocable trust can remove assets from your estate for estate-tax purposes, protect them from creditors, and hold them for generations (a dynasty trust), but assets given to it during life generally do not get a step-up at death. That is the core trade-off: estate-tax savings versus the income-tax step-up. Below the $15 million exemption, most families keep appreciated assets in their own name to get the step-up.

The Other Side

The step-up is the most-criticized provision in the tax code. Economists across the spectrum note that it lets the largest fortunes escape income tax on appreciation entirely. It has survived repeated repeal attempts, but a strategy built on it carries political risk.

Heirs rarely continue the strategy. Studies of inherited wealth find much of it is spent or dispersed within two generations. His emphasis on education is the right answer to a real problem, but a hard one to execute.

What survives: for any family expecting to pass on appreciated assets, holding them until death and borrowing modestly for liquidity is among the most tax-efficient paths the US code offers.

Chapter 33: Does It Work Outside the US?

Videos: Repaying the Loan, Part 2; What Net Worth Is Required; Why I Never Sell Stocks

He is British, and he repeats that his framework is built on US rules. “This video applies purely to US citizens. It’s going to rely fully on the IRS tax codes and tax wrappers.” “If you’re UK, the strategy is completely different. You cannot just borrow margin like in America.” His catalog includes a video titled “Why Buy Borrow Die Fails in the UK and Europe,” which is not among the ones this guide is built from. The differences can be reconstructed from the tax systems themselves; treat this chapter as research, not his words.

The UK

The biggest difference is not the step-up. The UK actually has something similar: capital gains tax is not charged on death, and heirs inherit assets at their market value. The problem is inheritance tax, charged at 40% on estates above a nil-rate band of £325,000 (plus up to £175,000 more for a main home passed to descendants). The US exempts $15 million per person; the UK exempts a few hundred thousand pounds. So in the UK, the “die” step saves capital gains tax but then hands a large share of the estate to inheritance tax, and from April 2027 unused pension funds are brought into the inheritance tax net too.

The borrowing side is also harder. UK retail investors mostly hold stocks in ISAs and SIPPs, which cannot be pledged as collateral. Retail margin lending in the UK is limited and expensive compared with Interactive Brokers-style institutional rates. Lombard lending exists, but mainly through private banks with high minimums and the fees he criticizes. He also notes a UK advantage: unlike Americans, UK residents can emigrate and genuinely leave the tax system behind, which US citizens cannot.

Foreign Investors in the US

One more title in his catalog covers how foreign investors deal with the US estate tax. Non-resident, non-citizen investors who hold US stocks directly face US estate tax on US-situs assets above only $60,000, at rates up to 40%. Many avoid this by holding US exposure through funds domiciled in Ireland or elsewhere, which are not treated as US-situs assets. It is a reminder that capital location, the theme of Chapter 16, applies across borders too.

The Other Side

Rules change often. The UK has changed inheritance-tax treatment of pensions, business assets and non-domiciled residents repeatedly since 2024. Anyone outside the US should treat BBD as a concept to test against local law, not a recipe.

What survives: the core ideas (hold growth assets, avoid selling, borrow modestly against diversified collateral) travel; the tax payoff at the end depends heavily on the country.

Synthesis

Pulling the fact-checks together into one honest scorecard, and turning the framework into a sequence of steps.

Chapter 34: Where He Is Right, Where He Is Wrong

Across twenty videos, a consistent framework emerges, and it is more coherent than a typical YouTube finance channel. It also rests on a handful of assumptions that do most of the work. Separating the durable insights from the optimistic assumptions is the most useful thing a careful reader can do, because it tells you which parts of the strategy to adopt as they are, which to adopt with a safety margin, and which to test against your own numbers before trusting.

Where He Is Right

Selling is expensive. Capital gains tax, interrupted compounding, bad timing and re-entry decisions are real costs that most investors underestimate. Borrowing modestly against a portfolio instead of selling it is a legitimate, widely used technique, and the US step-up in basis makes it unusually powerful for assets held until death. Loan size is the risk. His margin-call math is correct: at 10–20% loan-to-value on a diversified, quality portfolio, the crash needed to trigger a call is far beyond anything the S&P 500 has suffered since the 1930s. Tax on nominal gains is punitive. The 0.65% reconstruction reveals a genuine feature of the tax code that most investors never notice.

CPI is too generous a benchmark for asset owners. Scarce assets have risen much faster than consumer prices for decades, and anyone borrowing against a portfolio needs a hurdle above CPI. Capital location matters. The taxable brokerage account is badly underrated by standard advice for anyone expecting to hold appreciated assets for life. High-yield, low-growth portfolios are poor collateral. The silent margin call is a real failure mode, and covered-call and high-dividend funds are structurally unsuited to a borrowing strategy. Behavior dominates. His psychology is consistent with decades of behavioral-finance research.

Where He Is Optimistic

Return assumptions. Most of his spreadsheets assume the Nasdaq-100 compounds at 15% a year for decades. That was roughly true from 2010 to 2025, one of the best periods in market history. From 2000 to 2015 it was roughly zero. Every major conclusion (FIRE vs BBD, Renee, the dynasty math) should be rerun at 8–10%. M2 as the hurdle. Money growth partly reflects real economic growth; a more defensible debasement estimate is closer to 4–5% than 7%. This softens, but does not eliminate, his case against borrowing on the S&P 500. Hindsight. The four “winning” markets and the obvious monopolies are identified using the same period in which they won.

Floating rates. Margin loans float. In a hiking cycle the loan gets more expensive exactly when assets fall, and hiking cycles have lasted longer than he suggests. Broker behavior in crises. His observation that index ETFs keep 25% maintenance is plausible but not guaranteed; brokers can and do change requirements at short notice. Tax fine print. Margin interest used for lifestyle is not deductible; DSTs carry heavy fees and illiquidity; Roth conversions affect health subsidies; the step-up is politically exposed.

The Honest Verdict

Strip away the rhetoric (“cult,” “slave to the spreadsheet,” “blue pill”) and his framework reduces to a sound core: own high-quality assets that can grow faster than money is debased, never sell them to fund consumption, borrow a small fraction against them when you need liquidity, keep a cash buffer and defensive ballast so that no crash forces your hand, pay down with outside cash when the Fed turns hostile, and let the step-up finish the job. The danger lies not in the structure but in the assumptions plugged into it. Use his structure; use your own, more conservative numbers.

Chapter 35: The Implementation Checklist

This is the strategy as a sequence, with each step pointing back to the chapter that explains it. It is a map of his framework, not personal advice.

Stage 1: Build the Engine ($0–$1M)

  1. Take any employer 401(k) match first; it is a guaranteed 100% return (Ch. 16).
  2. Open a taxable brokerage account at a low-cost broker with institutional margin rates, and fund it alongside retirement accounts (Ch. 14, 16).
  3. Invest for growth: a growth ETF such as the Nasdaq-100, or monopoly businesses you understand; check entry valuation with PEG (Ch. 13, 18).
  4. Do not borrow for lifestyle. Below roughly $500,000, never (Ch. 26).
  5. From about $100,000, start “pouring the concrete”: add some T-bills and defensive quality businesses (Ch. 20, 26).
  6. Clear any high-rate consumer debt; keep low-rate fixed mortgages (Ch. 9, 10).

Stage 2: Structure the Portfolio ($1–2M)

  1. Target roughly 70% growth / 30% defensive, including a T-bill cash buffer (Ch. 20).
  2. Check weighted beta: aim for ~1.15–1.3, not 1, and not 2+ (Ch. 18).
  3. Screen every holding with the pricing-power question (Ch. 21).
  4. Avoid high-yield and covered-call funds as collateral (Ch. 19).
  5. Run the margin-call calculator at 25%, 30% and 50% maintenance before borrowing (Ch. 22).
  6. Decide your benchmark honestly: somewhere between CPI and M2, and compute your real after-tax return (Ch. 4, 5).

Stage 3: Borrow (Escape Velocity)

  1. Route everyday spending through a credit card paid in full from portfolio income (Ch. 27).
  2. Finance cars and boats against themselves, not the portfolio (Ch. 27).
  3. Borrow up to ~20% LTV for lifestyle (25% max); up to ~30% only if the loan buys more assets (Ch. 9, 15).
  4. Let interest capitalize; let dividends and income chip away at it (Ch. 15, 17).
  5. As the portfolio grows and LTV falls, top the loan back up to the target rather than taking one large loan (Ch. 15).
  6. Keep outside cash ready to deposit in a crash (Ch. 22).

Stage 4: Manage the Cycle

  1. Track liquidity: Fed direction, balance sheet, reverse repo, M2, credit spreads (Ch. 23).
  2. When the Fed begins hiking, pay down the loan with outside cash; never sell to repay (Ch. 23).
  3. In crashes during liquidity expansion, hold, and consider using spare margin to buy (Ch. 23, 27).
  4. Sell a holding only if its moat or pricing power is genuinely gone, or to rotate an outsized winner into an ETF (Ch. 12).
  5. Do not look at the growth sleeve daily; the cash buffer exists so you do not have to (Ch. 24).

Stage 5: The Finish Line

  1. Consider life insurance sized to the loan, possibly in an ILIT (Ch. 17).
  2. Keep appreciated assets eligible for the step-up; understand the trade-off before moving them into an irrevocable trust (Ch. 32).
  3. Consider converting traditional 401(k) money to Roth in low-income years (Ch. 29).
  4. Teach the next generation the framework, not just the account numbers (Ch. 32).
  5. Get a qualified tax and estate professional to review the whole structure (everything here).

Trading & Investing Lessons

Always Think in Real Rates

Every trade and every loan has a real rate: nominal return minus inflation, loan cost minus inflation. Traders who think only in nominal terms mistake money illusion for alpha. When real rates are deeply negative, as in 2021, leverage and long-duration assets are rewarded; when they turn positive, as in 2023, cash becomes a genuine competitor and leverage becomes expensive. Tracking the real policy rate is one of the most useful single macro inputs for positioning.

Liquidity Regimes Beat Headlines

His COVID and April 2025 examples make the trading point: headline panic during expanding liquidity is usually a buying opportunity, while calm during contracting liquidity (late 2021) is a warning. A trader can treat net liquidity and the direction of the Fed as a regime filter, leaning into dips in expansion and reducing leverage in contraction, instead of trading the news flow directly.

Size to the Drawdown, Not the Average

The margin-call formula (crash needed = 1 − LTV ÷ (1 − maintenance)) is a position-sizing rule in disguise. It asks the right question: what loss can this position survive? Traders who size by expected return blow up in the tail; traders who size by the historical worst drawdown of the instrument, plus a margin, survive to compound. The hedge fund case in Chapter 25 is what sizing to the average looks like.

Coiled Springs Are Mean-Reversion Setups

A stock whose price is flat while owner earnings grow 15–20% is compressing its valuation every month. In trading terms, the fundamental trend is up while price consolidates, which is the classic setup for a breakout once the market re-rates it. Screening for falling P/E with rising free cash flow is a systematic way to find his coiled springs.

The Elastic Band as a Valuation Oscillator

The S&P-to-M2 ratio behaves like a slow oscillator: stretched in 1999, 2021 and today, compressed in 1982 and 2009. It is useless for timing entries within a year, but useful for sizing: invest more and borrow more when the band is compressed, slow contributions and avoid new leverage when it is stretched. His PEG rule (slow buying when the index PEG passes 2–3) is the same idea at the index level.

Correlations Go to One in a Panic

His COVID observation, that the diversified all-world fund fell more than the Nasdaq, is a reminder that diversification across equities offers much less protection in a crash than in normal times. Only assets with genuinely different drivers (T-bills, long Treasuries in a deflationary shock, sometimes gold) reliably diversify in a panic. Risk models built on calm-market correlations understate tail risk.

Tax Is a Guaranteed Return

Deferring or eliminating tax is one of the few “returns” that is certain. Turnover in a taxable account quietly costs 1–2 points a year for an active trader. Keeping trading activity inside tax-advantaged accounts and long-term holdings in taxable ones, where they can reach the step-up, is free alpha.

Key Takeaways

Wealth Is Measured Against Money, Not Groceries

The first decision is the benchmark. Against CPI almost every asset looks like a winner; against the growth of money itself, most do not. Pick a hurdle between the two and judge everything by it.

BBD Extracts Wealth; It Does Not Create It

The loan only helps if the portfolio underneath grows faster than the loan and inflation combined. Asset selection comes first.

Never Sell Assets to Buy Liabilities

Selling stops compounding, triggers tax and surrenders margin of safety. Borrow instead, modestly.

The Loan-to-Value Is the Risk Control

At 20% the portfolio must fall about 73% before a margin call; at 50% it takes only 33%. The size of the loan matters more than anything else.

Long-Held Quality Is the Best Collateral

Every year a great business keeps compounding, the price that would take you back to your cost implies a more absurd valuation. Old positions in great companies are tier-one collateral.

Dividends Are Your Own Money Back, Taxed

High-yield portfolios feel safe but grow too slowly to carry a loan, producing the silent margin call.

The Barbell Lets the Engine Run

Roughly 70% growth and 30% defensive ballast plus T-bills survived even his 85% Nasdaq crash test.

Cheap Debt Shrinks; Expensive Debt Grows

Debt below the inflation rate erodes on its own. Debt above it compounds against you. Pay off the second, not the first.

Hikes Are the Enemy

Rate-hiking cycles hurt the strategy twice: higher loan costs and falling assets. Pay down with outside cash when the Fed turns.

Capital Location Is Half the Game

The taxable account is the BBD engine because it can be borrowed against and reaches the step-up.

Structure Beats Income

Renee earned more retired than working, purely by rearranging assets she already owned.

The Step-Up Is the Finish Line, and Education Keeps It Going

Assets held until death pass with the gain erased; heirs who understand the framework can keep the loop running for generations.
