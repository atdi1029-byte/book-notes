Chapter 1 — How to Use This Guide

Every technique here has an author, and most of those authors published in places nobody reads anymore: a self-printed book that sells for hundreds of dollars used (Crabel), a seminar series that was never turned into a book (Cardwell), a 1997 magazine article (Zamansky and Stendahl), a proprietary manual for institutional clients (DeMark), a mimeographed course from the 1950s (Taylor). The mainstream trading shelf recycles the same forty indicators with the same default settings. This guide is the other shelf — the specific rules the originators actually used, which are almost always sharper and stranger than the versions that survived into charting software.

Each technique is a card. The origin line says who and where, so you can check the source. The body says what the indicator actually measures — because an indicator whose mechanism you cannot state is one you cannot tell has stopped working. The bulleted rules are the originator's, with their settings. And every card ends with the trap: the way the technique fails, which is usually the way it is taught.

A word on honesty. Very few of these were ever tested with the rigor you would want. Some have been (Crabel's patterns, Connors' RSI work, the Kaminski-Lo stop analysis); most are practitioner claims that worked for the practitioner in the practitioner's market. Treat every card as a hypothesis with a pedigree. The settings are a starting point for your own data, not a finding.

Chapter 2 — RSI Beyond 70/30

Wilder introduced the Relative Strength Index in 1978 and the world reduced it to two horizontal lines. Wilder himself used it differently, and Andrew Cardwell — who taught RSI to institutional desks through the 1980s and 90s and never wrote a book — turned it into a complete trend-analysis system. Constance Brown, who studied under Cardwell, published the pieces in Technical Analysis for the Trading Professional (1999), which is where most of this chapter lives.

Trendlines on the RSI

Cardwell, seminars (1980s–90s); Brown (1999).

RSI is a bounded, smoothed measure of the ratio of up-closes to down-closes. A trendline drawn on it connects points of equal momentum ratio, and momentum turns before price does — so an RSI trendline usually breaks several bars before the equivalent price trendline. The technique is to draw and trade the RSI line, not the price line.

  • Use the 14-period RSI (Cardwell's setting). Draw trendlines across RSI swing lows in an uptrend and RSI swing highs in a downtrend, exactly as you would on price. Two touches minimum; three is better.
  • A break of an RSI trendline is an early warning that the price trendline is about to break. Treat it as a "get ready" signal, and enter when price confirms — a close through the price line, or a lower low.
  • Cardwell's second use: RSI trendlines act as support and resistance for the RSI itself. A pullback in RSI to a rising RSI trendline that holds is a continuation entry in the direction of the trend.
  • RSI trendlines are more reliable on higher timeframes (4-hour and up) where a swing in RSI represents a genuine change in the balance of closes rather than a handful of ticks.

Trap: Drawing the RSI line through minor wiggles. RSI swings are the ones that reach the range edges (Chapter 2's next card); a line through three mid-range bumps is a line through noise.

RSI range shifts

Cardwell; Brown (1999).

RSI does not oscillate between 0 and 100 in practice. It oscillates inside a range that depends on the trend. In a bull market RSI holds between roughly 40 and 80 — pullbacks bottom near 40 to 50, rallies top near 80 to 90 — and a reading of 30 almost never appears. In a bear market the range is 20 to 60. This reframes "oversold": an RSI of 40 in a bull range is a buy zone, not a warning, and an RSI of 60 in a bear range is a sell zone, not strength.

  • Identify the range from the last several swings. If RSI has been finding support at 40–50 and topping at 80–90, you are in a bull range regardless of what the price chart looks like.
  • A range shift — RSI breaking below 40 and then failing to recover above 60 on the next rally — is the earliest reliable signal of a trend change. The mirror (RSI breaking above 60 and then holding 40 on the pullback) signals a bear-to-bull shift.
  • Trade continuation from the range edges: buy a bull-range pullback to 40–50; sell a bear-range rally to 55–65.

Trap: Applying the 70/30 rules from the software defaults in a trending market. In a bull range, "overbought" at 70 is where the strongest legs begin.

Positive and negative reversals

Cardwell; the technique that made his name.

Divergence is the well-known signal: price makes a new high, RSI does not, and a reversal is expected. Cardwell discovered the opposite pattern, and argued it is more reliable. A positive reversal is when RSI makes a lower low but price makes a higher low. Momentum looks weaker; price is actually stronger. It is a bullish continuation signal, and it comes with a target. A negative reversal is the mirror: RSI makes a higher high, price makes a lower high — bearish continuation.

  • Find two RSI lows where the second is lower than the first, and check that the corresponding price lows have the second higher than the first. That is a positive reversal.
  • Target: measure the price rise from the first low to the highest high between the two lows. Add that distance to the second low. Cardwell's "X" measurement.
  • Entry: on the second low, with a stop below it. The reversal is invalid if price breaks the second low.
  • Reversals occur in the direction of the trend and are most reliable when RSI stays inside the trend's range (a positive reversal with RSI holding above 40 in a bull range).

Trap: Confusing a reversal with a divergence. Divergence is counter-trend and RSI is stronger than price; reversal is with-trend and RSI is weaker than price. They look similar and mean opposite things.

Wilder's failure swings

Wilder, New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems (1978). In the original book, almost never used.

A failure swing is a divergence signal that lives entirely on the RSI, without reference to price, which is what makes it unusual. Wilder considered it one of the RSI's strongest signals.

  • Top failure swing: RSI rises above 70, pulls back, rallies again but fails to exceed the first peak, then breaks below the pullback low. Sell on that break.
  • Bottom failure swing: RSI falls below 30, bounces, drops again but holds above the first trough, then breaks above the bounce high. Buy on that break.
  • In Cardwell's framework, substitute the range edges for 70 and 30 — a failure swing at 80–90 in a bull range, or 20–30 in a bear range.

Trap: Failure swings inside the middle of the range (between 40 and 60) are meaningless; the whole point is that the extreme was reached and could not be repeated.

The Composite Index

Constance Brown (1999).

RSI is a bounded ratio, so it compresses at the extremes: in a strong trend it can sit at 85 while momentum is still rising, and it cannot show a divergence because it has no room to. Brown's fix combines the momentum of the RSI itself with a fast, smoothed RSI, producing an unbounded indicator that shows divergences the RSI cannot.

  • Composite Index = (RSI(14) − RSI(14) nine bars ago) + a 3-period average of a 3-period RSI. Plot with 13- and 33-period simple moving averages of the Composite Index as signal lines.
  • Look for divergences between the Composite Index and the RSI, not just between the index and price. When RSI makes a higher high and the Composite does not, momentum has peaked.
  • Crosses of the Composite through its 13-period average are early entries; the 33-period average is trend direction.

Trap: Using it standalone. Brown always read it against the RSI and its range. Alone it is a fast oscillator with all the whipsaw of one.

Dynamic overbought/oversold zones

Zamansky and Stendahl, "Dynamic Zones," TASC, July 1997.

Fixed thresholds — 70/30, 80/20 — ignore that an indicator's distribution changes with the market. Dynamic zones replace the fixed lines with rolling percentiles of the indicator's own recent history, so "overbought" always means "in the top decile of what this indicator has done lately."

  • Choose a lookback (70 bars in the original). Each bar, compute the 90th and 10th percentiles of the indicator over that window. Those are the zones.
  • Apply to RSI, stochastic, CCI, or anything bounded or semi-bounded. The signal logic is unchanged — only the thresholds move.
  • Wider zones in a trending market and tighter zones in a range fall out automatically; this is the entire point.

Trap: A lookback so short that the zones chase the indicator and every bar is "extreme." Seventy bars was chosen for a reason.

Connors' RSI(2), Cumulative RSI, and Double 7s

Larry Connors and Cesar Alvarez, Short Term Trading Strategies That Work (2008); among the few short-term systems published with full test statistics.

A 2-period RSI is so fast it is barely an indicator — it reads near 0 after two down closes and near 100 after two up closes. Connors' finding was that on stock indices and liquid ETFs, extreme readings of this hypersensitive RSI, taken with the long-term trend, produced a consistent one-to-five-day reversion edge. The three variants differ only in the trigger.

  • RSI(2): price above its 200-day moving average; RSI(2) closes below 10 (below 5 for a stronger version). Buy the close. Exit when price closes above its 5-day moving average, or when RSI(2) exceeds 70.
  • Cumulative RSI: sum the last two days' RSI(2) values. Buy when the sum is below 35 (above the 200-day MA); exit when the sum exceeds 65. Smoother, fewer trades.
  • Double 7s: price above the 200-day MA and closes at a 7-day low. Buy. Exit on a close at a 7-day high. No oscillator at all — the same logic in price terms.
  • All three take the mirror side (short) only below the 200-day MA, and Connors' tests showed the short side was markedly weaker.

Trap: The originals use no stop-loss, and the test statistics depend on that. Adding a tight stop destroyed the edge in Connors' own tests, because the strategy is a bet on reversion and the stop exits before it. Size small enough to survive the occasional 10% adverse move instead.

Chapter 3 — Stochastics Reinvented

George Lane's stochastic oscillator measures where the close sits inside the recent range. Lane's own use of it — divergence, the "hinge," the right-hand crossover — bears little resemblance to the overbought/oversold crossover that ships with every platform. The traders who did the most with stochastics after Lane were Jake Bernstein and Linda Raschke, and both of them used it as a trend tool, not a reversal tool.

The Stochastic Pop

Jake Bernstein; described in his Compleat Day Trader series and seminars (1990s).

Every textbook says a stochastic above 80 is overbought. Bernstein noticed that the strongest, fastest price moves happen while the stochastic is above 80, because that is what a market closing at the top of its range every bar looks like. So he bought the cross above 80 and held while it stayed there.

  • 14-period stochastic, slow version. When it crosses above 80 (Bernstein used thresholds in the mid-70s to 80), buy. Exit when it crosses back below 80.
  • Mirror for shorts: sell the cross below 20, cover on the cross back above.
  • Filter with a longer-timeframe trend: take Pops only in the direction of the weekly or daily trend, depending on your bar size.

Trap: In a range, the stochastic crosses 80 and immediately turns down. The Pop only works when the range is expanding; combine with a squeeze release (Chapter 8) or an efficiency-ratio filter (Chapter 7).

The Anti

Raschke and Connors, Street Smarts (1995).

A pullback entry that uses the two stochastic lines as fast and slow trend measures. The slow line (%D) defines the trend; the fast line (%K) pulls back against it and then hooks back. You enter on the hook, which puts you in the direction of the slow trend just as the short-term counter-move ends.

  • Settings: 7-period stochastic, %K smoothed by 4, %D as a 10-period moving average of %K. These are non-standard on purpose.
  • Setup: %D is rising steadily (uptrend). %K pulls back toward or below %D for three to four bars.
  • Entry: when %K hooks back up toward %D's direction, buy the break of the hook bar's high. Stop below the pullback low.
  • Expected hold: a few bars — the Anti catches the resumption, not the whole trend.

Trap: Taking the Anti when %D is flat. The setup is a pullback within a trend; without a trending %D, it is a random crossover.

Lane's hinge and the right-hand cross

George Lane, seminars and TASC interviews (1980s).

Lane's own three rules, in order of the weight he gave them. Divergence first (Chapter 4). Then the hinge: a flattening of the stochastic at an extreme — the lines lose slope while still above 80 or below 20 — which he read as momentum exhaustion before the crossover. Then the right-hand crossover: %K crossing %D after %D has already turned, which he considered far more reliable than a "left-hand" crossover where %K crosses a still-rising %D.

  • Hinge: watch for the slope of %K to decrease sharply while at an extreme. This is the earliest warning; do not act on it alone.
  • Right-hand cross: wait until %D has turned, then act on %K crossing it. The cross should occur on the right side of %D's peak.
  • Lane's "warning": a stochastic that rises above 80, falls below, and then re-rises above 80 without price making a new high — a failure pattern equivalent to Wilder's failure swing.

Trap: Left-hand crossovers. They occur while the slow line is still going the other way and are the majority of stochastic whipsaws.

Cycle-tuned stochastic length

Hurst (1970); formalized by Ehlers, Cybernetic Analysis for Stocks and Futures (2004).

The default 14 is arbitrary. An oscillator's lookback should be tied to the cycle it is trying to catch. Ehlers' rule: set the oscillator period to half the dominant cycle length, because a half-cycle window is what makes the indicator's turning points align with the cycle's turning points instead of lagging or leading them.

  • Measure the dominant cycle: count bars between the last several significant swing lows (Hurst's method), or use a Hilbert-transform cycle-period indicator (Ehlers').
  • Stochastic and RSI period = half that count. A 40-bar cycle wants a 20-period oscillator. A 10-bar cycle wants a 5.
  • Re-tune as the cycle length changes; a fixed 14 is only right when the dominant cycle happens to be 28.

Trap: Trusting an automated cycle-period reading in a trending market. When there is no cycle, the estimate is noise and the tuned oscillator is worse than the default.

StochRSI

Chande and Kroll, The New Technical Trader (1994).

The stochastic formula applied to RSI instead of price: where the current RSI sits inside its own recent range, scaled 0 to 1. It exists to solve the bull-range problem from Chapter 2 — an RSI that spends months between 50 and 75 never reaches 30, so its pullbacks are invisible to fixed thresholds. StochRSI reads those pullbacks as 0.

  • StochRSI = (RSI − lowest RSI over n) / (highest RSI over n − lowest RSI over n), with n = 14 for both RSI and the range.
  • Below 0.2 in a bull range = pullback buy zone; above 0.8 in a bear range = rally sell zone. Read the direction from the RSI range, the timing from StochRSI.
  • Smooth with a 3-period average; the raw version flickers.

Trap: Trading StochRSI extremes against the RSI range. A StochRSI of 0.9 while RSI sits at 62 in a bull range is a strong market, not an overbought one.

Chapter 4 — Divergence Done Properly

Divergence is the most-cited and worst-executed signal in technical analysis. Most of the failures come from three mistakes: trading a divergence without waiting for a trigger, ignoring the difference between regular and hidden divergence, and looking for it on an indicator that cannot show it (Chapter 2's Composite Index card). The cards below fix each.

Hidden divergence as a continuation signal

Popularized by Barbara Star, TASC (1990s); the same pattern Cardwell called a reversal.

Regular divergence: price makes a new extreme, the oscillator does not — reversal warning. Hidden divergence: price makes a less extreme swing (a higher low in an uptrend) while the oscillator makes a more extreme one (a lower low). The oscillator has reset while price held its structure. It is a with-trend pullback signal, and in trending markets it produces more winning entries than regular divergence produces winning reversals.

  • Uptrend: price higher low, oscillator lower low. Buy the pullback, stop below the price low.
  • Downtrend: price lower high, oscillator higher high. Sell the rally.
  • Works on RSI, stochastic, MACD histogram, and — most usefully — on volume-based oscillators (next chapter and Chapter 10).

Trap: Hidden divergence against the higher-timeframe trend is not hidden divergence; it is the first sign of a range.

Three-drive divergence

Elder, Trading for a Living (1993), on MACD-histogram divergence; the three-push structure is older desk lore.

A single divergence is a warning. A double divergence — three successive price extremes with the oscillator making successively weaker extremes — is the version Elder called the strongest signal in technical analysis, and he applied it specifically to the MACD histogram, which measures the acceleration of momentum rather than momentum itself.

  • Use the MACD histogram (12, 26, 9). Look for price making three successively higher highs while the histogram peaks are successively lower. The third peak should be barely above zero, or the histogram should fail to cross above zero at all.
  • Trigger: the histogram ticking down from the third peak. Stop above the third price high.
  • Elder's rule: the histogram must cross back through zero between the divergent peaks. If it does not, it is one extended move, not three drives.

Trap: Trading the second drive because it looks like the third. Three is the number; a two-drive divergence is a first warning and fails often.

Divergence on volume indicators

Granville (OBV, 1963); Chaikin (Money Flow, 1980s); order-flow desks (CVD, 2010s).

Price oscillators can only tell you that momentum is fading. A volume oscillator diverging from price tells you who is fading. On-Balance Volume failing to confirm a price high means the high was made on lower participation. Cumulative volume delta (Chapter 10) failing to confirm means the high was made with fewer aggressive buyers.

  • OBV: price new high, OBV lower high — distribution. Granville also drew trendlines on OBV and traded their breaks ahead of price.
  • Money Flow Index (volume-weighted RSI, 14): divergence at the 80/20 extremes is the version to trust; mid-range divergence is noise.
  • CVD: the most direct. Price higher high, CVD lower high — the buyers who made the second high were passive, not aggressive, and passive buyers do not chase.

Trap: OBV on a thinly traded symbol where a single block trade sets the indicator's level for weeks. Use a smoothed version, or CVD from a liquid venue.

Chapter 5 — Oscillators You Have Never Used

Raschke's 3-10 oscillator

Raschke, from Marty Zweig's work; described in Street Smarts and her seminars.

A MACD built from a 3-period and a 10-period simple moving average (not exponential), with a 16-period simple average of the oscillator as the slow line. The short lengths make it a short-term momentum measure; the slow line makes it a trend measure. Raschke used the relationship between them for one specific entry.

  • The first cross: after the 3-10 makes a new momentum high (a peak higher than any in the last several swings — trend confirmed), the first time it pulls back and crosses below its 16-period slow line, then crosses back above, is the buy. The first pullback after a momentum extreme is the highest-probability continuation entry.
  • Divergence between the 3-10 and price at the second and third swings warns the trend is aging.
  • Slow line above zero and rising = uptrend; take only buys. Below and falling = take only sells.

Trap: Taking the second and third "first crosses." Each successive pullback after a momentum high has lower odds; by the third, the divergence is usually already there.

True Strength Index

William Blau, Momentum, Direction, and Divergence (1995).

Momentum is noisy; smoothing it adds lag. Blau's trick was to double-smooth both the momentum and its absolute value with exponential averages and take the ratio, which produces an oscillator with far less lag per unit of smoothing than a MACD, bounded roughly between −100 and 100.

  • TSI = 100 × EMA(EMA(price change, 25), 13) / EMA(EMA(|price change|, 25), 13). Signal line: 7- to 13-period EMA of the TSI.
  • Zero-line cross = trend change, with much less lag than a 25-period MA cross. Signal-line cross = entry within the trend.
  • Blau's ergodic variants apply the same double-smoothing to stochastics and to candle bodies; the candle-body version (close minus open, double-smoothed) is a remarkably clean intrabar momentum measure.

Trap: Overbought/oversold levels. The TSI is unbounded in practice and Blau never assigned thresholds; it is a trend and divergence tool.

Schaff Trend Cycle

Doug Schaff, published 2008.

A MACD run through a stochastic calculation twice. The MACD gives you trend; the double stochastic squashes it into 0–100 and sharpens the turns, so you get a trend indicator with the responsiveness of a fast oscillator.

  • Defaults: MACD(23, 50), stochastic period 10, applied twice with 3-bar smoothing.
  • Buy when the STC crosses up through 25; sell when it crosses down through 75. Schaff's rules — not 20/80 — because the doubled stochastic saturates fast.
  • STC pinned at 100 or 0 is a strong trend, not an overbought condition; do not fade a pinned reading.

Trap: Using it in a range, where it oscillates between 25 and 75 every few bars. It is a trend tool wearing an oscillator's clothes.

The Fisher Transform

Ehlers, "Using the Fisher Transform," TASC, November 2002.

Prices are not normally distributed, which is why oscillators built on them have rounded, ambiguous turning points. The Fisher Transform maps a bounded input (price normalized to −1…+1 over a short window, or any bounded oscillator) onto a near-Gaussian output: y = 0.5 × ln((1 + x) / (1 − x)). The result has sharp, spike-like extremes that make turning points unmistakable.

  • Default: normalize the median price over 10 bars to −1…+1, apply the transform, plot with a one-bar-lagged copy as the signal line.
  • Signals: the transform crossing its own lagged value at an extreme (above +1.5 or below −1.5) marks a turn. The extremes are rare by construction, which is the point.
  • Apply it to RSI or stochastic instead of price and you get an oscillator whose extremes are statistically meaningful instead of arbitrary.

Trap: A window so long that the transform never reaches an extreme. Ehlers' 10 bars is short on purpose.

Laguerre RSI

Ehlers, Cybernetic Analysis for Stocks and Futures (2004).

A conventional RSI trades off responsiveness against whipsaw through its period length. The Laguerre filter replaces the period with a damping factor (gamma), which lets you get a very short-lookback response with heavily suppressed noise — a four-element filter that behaves like a much longer one in the flats and a much shorter one at the turns.

  • Gamma between 0.5 and 0.8; higher is smoother. Ehlers' examples used 0.5 for fast, 0.8 for swing trading.
  • Output is 0 to 1. Above 0.8 is strength, below 0.2 is weakness. Crosses down through 0.8 and up through 0.2 are the signals.
  • Its virtue is that it sits pinned at 1 or 0 during a strong trend instead of wobbling, so you can hold through the trend and exit only on the cross.

Trap: Treating a reading of 1.0 as overbought. Like the Schaff, pinned means trending.

Woodies CCI patterns

Ken Wood, online trading room and free manual (early 2000s).

Wood traded the Commodity Channel Index with the price chart hidden, on the theory that price is noise and the CCI is the signal. The method is a set of named patterns on two CCIs — a 14-period and a 6-period "turbo" — plus a strict trend definition. It has more jargon than any technique in this guide, and under the jargon, three of the patterns are useful.

  • Trend: the 14-period CCI has been on one side of zero for at least six consecutive bars. No trade until a trend exists.
  • Zero-line reject (ZLR): in an established trend, CCI pulls back to within roughly ±100 of zero, touches or nears it, and turns back in the trend direction. Enter on the turn. The zero line is a moving-average pullback in disguise.
  • Trendline break (TLB): a trendline drawn on the CCI itself is broken. The same logic as RSI trendlines (Chapter 2), on a faster indicator.
  • Ghost: a head-and-shoulders shape on the CCI above +100 or below −100 — an exhaustion pattern. Trade the break of the neckline on the indicator.

Trap: The other dozen patterns (Shamu, Vegas, Famir, HFE) are named whipsaws. The hidden-price-chart discipline was Wood's point; the naming was his community's.

Elder's Force Index, Elder-Ray, and the Impulse System

Alexander Elder, Trading for a Living (1993) and Come Into My Trading Room (2002).

Three tools that share a design: measure one thing crudely and use it as a permission filter, not a trigger. Force Index multiplies volume by the change in close — how much conviction moved price. Elder-Ray measures how far the bar's high and low sit from a 13-period EMA — the bulls' and bears' reach. The Impulse System combines trend and momentum into a three-color permission code.

  • Force Index: 2-period EMA for entries — in an uptrend (13-day EMA of price rising), buy when the 2-day Force Index dips below zero. 13-period EMA of Force Index for trend confirmation and divergence.
  • Elder-Ray: bull power = high − EMA(13); bear power = low − EMA(13). Buy when the EMA is rising and bear power is negative but rising (bears are weakening). Sell when the EMA is falling and bull power is positive but falling.
  • Impulse: a bar is green when the 13-period EMA and the MACD histogram are both rising — buying permitted; red when both falling — selling permitted; blue otherwise — no new positions. The system censors; it never enters.

Trap: Trading the Impulse color change as a signal. Elder built it to keep you out of counter-trend trades; a green bar is a permission slip, not a buy.

Chande's Aroon

Tushar Chande, TASC, September 1995.

Not a momentum measure — a measure of time since the extreme. Aroon Up is the percentage of the lookback that has elapsed since the period's high was made; Aroon Down, since the low. A fresh high puts Aroon Up at 100; a high that is 25 bars old puts it at 0. The two lines read trend age directly, which no oscillator does.

  • Period 25. Aroon Up = 100 × (25 − bars since 25-bar high) / 25; Aroon Down likewise.
  • Up above 70 with Down below 30 = a young, active uptrend. Both between 30 and 70 = no recent extreme either way — a range.
  • Crossovers are early trend-change warnings; a crossover confirmed by the crossing line reaching 100 is the trade.

Trap: Both lines pinned low. It means the extremes are old, not that the market is quiet — a breakout from that state can be violent.

The Elliott Oscillator (5-35)

Tom Joseph, Advanced GET software (1990s); the oscillator is public, the wave-count logic was his.

A 5-period simple average minus a 35-period simple average of price. Joseph's use of it was not as a momentum indicator but as a wave-counting instrument: in a five-wave move, the oscillator peaks in wave three, returns to (or through) zero in wave four, and diverges in wave five. Those three behaviors identify the waves without subjective counting.

  • Wave 3: the oscillator makes its largest reading of the sequence. If the current peak is the largest, you are in or near wave 3.
  • Wave 4: the oscillator retraces 90% to 140% of its wave-3 peak, back to zero or slightly past. A fourth wave that does not bring the oscillator to zero is not a fourth wave.
  • Wave 5: price makes a new high while the oscillator makes a lower peak. The divergence is the ending signal.
  • Joseph's rule of thumb: the 5-35 settings assume roughly 100 to 150 bars of the move are on screen. Rescale for other windows.

Trap: Using it without the five-wave hypothesis. As a plain oscillator it is a slow MACD; its value is entirely in the wave-4 zero-return rule.

Chapter 6 — Cycle Tools

J.M. Hurst's 1970 book The Profit Magic of Stock Transaction Timing is the source for most cycle analysis, and two of its tools — the FLD and the VTL — are still almost unknown outside a small circle. John Ehlers spent thirty years building the digital-signal-processing version. Between them they cover cycle identification, targets, and confirmation.

The Future Line of Demarcation (FLD)

Hurst (1970); revived by David Hickson and the Sentient Trader community (2000s).

Take a centered moving average whose period equals the cycle you are tracking, and shift it forward by half that period. That is the FLD. Because the average is centered on the cycle, the forward shift puts it where the cycle's midpoint will be, so price crossing the FLD marks the halfway point of the cycle's move — which gives you a projected target.

  • Choose a cycle (say 40 bars). Plot a 40-period simple moving average displaced 20 bars into the future.
  • When price crosses above the FLD after a cycle trough, the move is roughly half done. Target = crossing price + (crossing price − trough). Mirror for downside crosses after a peak.
  • FLD crosses of successive cycle lengths (the 20-bar FLD, the 40, the 80) nesting in the same direction confirm a larger-degree move; a short-cycle cross that fails to reach the next FLD warns of a larger-cycle peak.

Trap: Applying an FLD to a market with no measurable cycle. The projection is only as real as the periodicity behind it.

The Valid Trend Line (VTL)

Hurst (1970).

Not every trendline is a Hurst trendline. A VTL connects two consecutive troughs of the same cycle — two 40-bar troughs, say — and no others. Its purpose is not support; it is confirmation. When price breaks a VTL, the peak of the next longer cycle has formed. That is a precise and testable statement, unlike "the trendline broke."

  • Identify troughs of a chosen cycle (they should be roughly the cycle length apart). Draw the line through the last two.
  • A close below the VTL confirms that the peak of the next-longer cycle (the 80-bar, if you drew a 40-bar VTL) is in.
  • Combine with the FLD: a VTL break plus a downside FLD cross of the same cycle is Hurst's highest-confidence sell.

Trap: Drawing a VTL through troughs of different cycles. The confirmation logic only holds when both points belong to one periodicity.

Ehlers' Sinewave indicator

Ehlers, Rocket Science for Traders (2001).

The Sinewave computes the phase of the dominant cycle and plots two lines: the sine of the phase and the sine of the phase advanced by 45 degrees (the "lead sine"). In cycle mode the lines cross at every turn, and they cross before the price turn because of the lead. In trend mode the two lines flatten and stop crossing — which is the indicator's second job: telling you which mode you are in.

  • Cycle mode: buy when the lead sine crosses above the sine near a trough; sell at the mirror crossing near a peak.
  • Trend mode: when the lines run roughly parallel without crossing for more than half a cycle, stop taking cycle trades and switch to trend tools.
  • Ehlers' rule of thumb: if the dominant cycle period exceeds about 40 bars, the market is trending at your timeframe and the cycle trades will fail.

Trap: Taking cycle-mode signals during the transition to trend mode. The first non-crossing is the warning; the trade that ignores it is the one that gets run over.

MESA Adaptive Moving Average (MAMA / FAMA)

Ehlers, TASC, September 2001.

A moving average whose smoothing factor adapts to the rate of change of the dominant cycle's phase: it moves fast when the cycle is turning, and slow when it is not. FAMA is a slower, following version of MAMA. Because MAMA ratchets rather than lags, the crossover happens once per cycle turn instead of several times.

  • Defaults: fast limit 0.5, slow limit 0.05.
  • Buy MAMA crossing above FAMA; sell the reverse. Ehlers' claim, and it holds up reasonably in testing, is that the pair produces markedly fewer whipsaws than any fixed-length crossover.
  • The gap between MAMA and FAMA is a trend-strength measure; a widening gap in your direction is confirmation.

Trap: Expecting it to be early. It is a crossover; its virtue is fewer false signals, not faster ones.

The Detrended Price Oscillator for cycle length

Standard in Bressert's work; the technique of using it to measure cycles rather than trade them is less known.

The DPO subtracts a displaced moving average from price, which removes the trend and leaves the cycle. Set the period to roughly the cycle you suspect, and the DPO's troughs land on the cycle's troughs with little lag. Its most useful role is not as a signal but as a ruler.

  • Period n; the moving average is displaced back by n/2 + 1 bars. Count bars between DPO troughs; that is the dominant cycle at this timeframe.
  • Use the count to set the FLD and VTL, and to tune your oscillators (Chapter 3).
  • Re-measure every few cycles; the length drifts.

Trap: A period set far from the true cycle produces DPO troughs that are artifacts of the period, not the market. Try several and look for the one whose troughs are evenly spaced.

Ehlers' roofing filter and the indicators built on it

John Ehlers, Cycle Analytics for Traders (2013).

Ehlers' argument is that most indicators are ruined by two things they were never designed to handle: the trend (spectral content longer than your trading horizon) and aliasing noise (content shorter than about ten bars). A roofing filter removes both — a high-pass filter strips out cycles longer than about 48 bars, and a Super Smoother (a two-pole low-pass with almost no lag) strips out everything shorter than about 10. What is left is the tradeable band. Any indicator computed on the roofed series is sharper than the same indicator on raw price.

  • Roofing filter: high-pass at 48 bars, Super Smoother at 10 bars. Apply a stochastic or RSI to the output — Ehlers' "My Stochastic" — and the turns are sharp and the false extremes rare.
  • Center of Gravity oscillator: the weighted center of prices over a window, which by construction has zero lag. Signals are crossings with its one-bar delay.
  • FRAMA: a moving average whose smoothing tracks the fractal dimension of price — fast in trends, slow in chop. Instantaneous Trendline: a trend proxy computed from the dominant cycle rather than a fixed length.

Trap: The parameters are cycle lengths in bars, and they assume your bars carry a cycle of that length. On a timeframe where the dominant cycle is 200 bars, a 48-bar high-pass removes the signal.

Lindsay's Three Peaks and a Domed House

George Lindsay, newsletters and privately published papers (1950s–70s); compiled by Ed Carlson, George Lindsay and the Art of Technical Analysis (2011).

A 23-point pattern describing how a major top forms: three peaks of roughly equal height (points 1–7), a separating decline (8–10), then the domed house — a rally that builds a base, a wall, a rounded roof, and a final decline back through the base (11–23). Lindsay used it on stock indices, and the remarkable claim was about timing: the top of the domed house tends to arrive a set interval after the separating decline's low — his rule of thumb was about seven months and ten days.

  • Identify three peaks of similar height over several months. The decline that follows must go below the lowest point between the peaks — that is the separating decline.
  • From the separating decline's low, expect a rally to a new high (the domed house). Mark the projected top from Lindsay's interval.
  • The completion: a decline that breaks the base of the domed house (point 14's level) confirms the pattern; the target is below the separating decline's low.

Trap: With enough peaks on a chart, three of them are always similar. The pattern is only meaningful when the separating decline is unambiguous, and Lindsay himself misidentified it on occasion.

Chapter 7 — Trend Filters With Teeth

The difference between a technique that works and one that does not is often just a filter that keeps it out of the wrong market. These are the filters that actually measure trendiness, as opposed to a moving-average slope.

Kaufman's Efficiency Ratio

Perry Kaufman, Smarter Trading (1995); the engine inside KAMA.

Net price change over a window divided by the sum of absolute bar-to-bar changes over the same window. A straight-line move scores 1; a random walk scores near 0. It is the cleanest single-number answer to "is this trending."

  • ER(10) = |close − close 10 bars ago| / Σ|daily changes over 10 bars|.
  • Kaufman's Adaptive Moving Average uses ER to blend a 2-period and a 30-period smoothing constant; the average speeds up when ER is high and flattens when it is low.
  • As a filter: take trend entries only when ER exceeds roughly 0.3; take reversion entries only below roughly 0.2. The band between is no-man's-land.

Trap: ER is backward-looking by exactly its window. A 10-bar ER spikes after the trend, not before; use it to stay in or stay out, not to enter.

Chande's r² and the regression slope

Tushar Chande and Stanley Kroll, The New Technical Trader (1994).

Regress price on time over a window. The slope tells you direction; the r² tells you how much of the price variation is explained by that straight line — which is trend strength in the statistical sense. Chande's insight was that r² is a better trend filter than any oscillator because it directly measures the thing.

  • Window 14 to 20 bars. Plot r² and slope separately.
  • r² above 0.5 with a positive slope = trend to trade; r² below 0.3 = range; slope changing sign while r² is high = a sharp turn.
  • Chande's rule: r² rising through 0.5 is the entry filter; r² falling below 0.5 after having been above 0.8 is the exit filter.

Trap: Long windows produce high r² for almost any drift. Match the window to your holding period, not to what makes the chart look clean.

The Choppiness Index

E.W. Dreiss, Australian commodity trader, 1990s.

The sum of true ranges over a window divided by the total range over that window, log-scaled to 0–100. If every bar's range adds up to far more than the net range, the market has been chopping; if the sum of ranges is close to the net range, it has been trending. It is the Efficiency Ratio's cousin, built from ranges instead of closes, so it sees intrabar chop that ER misses.

  • Period 14. CHOP = 100 × log10(Σ ATR(1) / (max high − min low)) / log10(14).
  • Above 61.8: choppy — stand aside from breakouts. Below 38.2: trending — stand aside from fades. The Fibonacci thresholds were Dreiss's; they are not sacred.
  • A reading above 61.8 that begins to fall is the earliest sign a range is resolving; pair it with a squeeze (Chapter 8).

Trap: Reading Choppiness as directional. It says trend or no trend; it never says which way.

Guppy Multiple Moving Average

Daryl Guppy, Trend Trading (2004).

Two ribbons of exponential averages: a short group (3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 15) representing traders, and a long group (30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 60) representing investors. Guppy read the spacing within each ribbon, not the crossovers. A compressed ribbon means agreement about price — and the market is about to disagree.

  • Long group well separated and parallel = investors committed; a pullback in the short group into the long group that holds is the buy.
  • Short group compressing while the long group stays spread = pause within the trend, not a reversal.
  • Both groups compressing together = the trend is over; a breakout from full compression starts the next one.

Trap: Reading a single crossover between the two groups as a signal. Guppy explicitly warned against it; the information is in the width.

Wilder's ADX hook

Wilder (1978); the hook rule is the part of the book nobody read.

ADX measures the strength of the trend regardless of direction. Everyone knows "above 25 is trending." Wilder's actual rules were about the turn in ADX, and they are better.

  • ADX rising from below 20 with +DI crossing above −DI is the start of a trend. Wilder's "extreme point rule": enter on a break of the extreme (high or low) of the bar on which the DI cross occurred, not on the cross itself.
  • ADX above 40 and turning down — the hook — means the trend is losing thrust. Wilder's rule: stop adding, tighten stops. The reversal often does not come for many bars, but the easy part of the move is over.
  • ADX turning up from a hook while +DI remains above −DI is a continuation; ADX turning up while the DIs have crossed is a new trend the other way.

Trap: Shorting the hook. ADX turning down from 45 says the uptrend is decelerating, not that a downtrend has started. Traders who short there get run over by the last leg.

Raschke's Holy Grail

Raschke and Connors, Street Smarts (1995). The name is her joke about traders looking for one.

The cleanest pullback entry in the pattern literature: a strong trend, measured by ADX, pulls back to a 20-period EMA, and you buy the resumption. Nothing about it is exotic; its edge is the strictness of the trend condition, which keeps it out of the ranges where every pullback entry dies.

  • Condition: 14-period ADX above 30 and rising. The rising part is not optional.
  • Wait for price to pull back and touch the 20-period EMA.
  • Entry: a buy stop above the high of the bar that touched the EMA. Stop below the pullback's swing low.
  • Target: a retest of the prior swing high; trail from there. If the first entry is stopped out, Raschke allowed one re-entry on the next touch.

Trap: ADX above 30 but falling. That is the hook from the previous card, and the pullback you are buying is the start of the range.

Wilder's Accumulation Swing Index

Wilder (1978). The most-ignored chapter of the most-read book in technical analysis.

The Swing Index compresses each bar's open, high, low, and close, together with the previous bar's, into a single number that Wilder intended as the "real" price of the bar — weighting the close-to-close change most, and adjusting for gaps and range. The ASI is its running sum. Wilder's claim: the ASI makes a cleaner chart than price, its swing points are unambiguous, and trendlines drawn on it break before price does.

  • Compute the Swing Index per Wilder's formula (any platform has it); the limit-move parameter should be set to a large multiple of ATR for markets without limits.
  • Mark high swing points (HSP) and low swing points (LSP) on the ASI. Draw trendlines through them.
  • Wilder's confirmation rule: a price breakout is valid only if the ASI also breaks its corresponding swing point. Price up, ASI not up = false breakout.
  • An ASI trendline break with price still inside its line is the early entry, as with RSI and OBV lines.

Trap: The limit-move parameter. Wilder wrote it for commodities with daily limits, and a badly chosen value distorts every bar. Set it to something like 5 × ATR and forget it.

DiNapoli levels: displaced averages and the Double Repo

Joe DiNapoli, Trading with DiNapoli Levels (1998).

DiNapoli used simple moving averages displaced forward — plotted several bars into the future — so that the current price is compared against an average that has not yet "seen" the latest bars. Three of them: the 3×3 (3-period, displaced 3 bars), the 7×5, and the 25×5. The 3×3 is his short-term trend line; a close through it is a trend-change signal that fires far earlier than any conventional crossover. His MACD and stochastic settings were deliberately odd (MACD 8.3896 / 17.5185 / 9.0503; stochastic 8, 3, 3) and his Fibonacci targets were named.

  • Trend: price above a rising 3×3 = up; the 25×5 for the larger trend. Trade in the direction of the 25×5 using the 3×3 for timing.
  • Double Repo: after a thrust, price closes through the 3×3 twice within a few bars (a "double repenetration"). The second penetration signals the thrust has failed; trade in the direction of the penetration with a stop beyond the thrust extreme. DiNapoli's highest-confidence pattern.
  • Targets: expansions of the prior swing — COP (0.618), OP (1.0), XOP (1.618) — projected from the retracement low. Confluence of a COP with a retracement of a larger swing is a "Fibnode" and a place to take profit.

Trap: Treating a single 3×3 crossing as a signal. The 3×3 is crossed constantly; the Double Repo is the pattern, and it needs a preceding thrust to mean anything.

Bill Williams' Alligator, Awesome Oscillator, and fractals

Bill Williams, Trading Chaos (1995) and New Trading Dimensions (1998).

Under a great deal of chaos-theory decoration, a coherent trend-following kit. The Alligator is three smoothed moving averages of the median price, each displaced forward: the jaw (13, shifted 8), teeth (8, shifted 5), and lips (5, shifted 3). Intertwined lines mean the alligator is asleep — a range; separated and ordered lines mean it is feeding — a trend. A fractal is a five-bar pattern with the highest high in the middle. The Awesome Oscillator is a 5-period minus 34-period average of the median price.

  • Entry: a buy stop above an up fractal that sits above the alligator's teeth, while the lines are separating. Fractals below the teeth are ignored.
  • AO confirms: a "saucer" (two red bars then a green one, above zero) or a zero-line cross in the trend direction adds size; AO divergence at a fractal warns.
  • Exit: a close through the alligator's teeth, or the lines re-crossing.

Trap: The displacement makes the alligator look prescient on a static chart because the lines appear to lead price. They are shifted, not forecast; read them where they sit today.

Lag-reduced averages: Hull, T3, McGinley Dynamic, ZLEMA

Alan Hull (2005); Tim Tillson, TASC (January 1998); John McGinley (1990s); Ehlers and Way, TASC (2010).

Every moving average trades lag against smoothness. These four attack the lag from different directions, and each has a specific use rather than being a "better MA."

  • Hull: HMA = WMA(2 × WMA(n/2) − WMA(n), √n). Extrapolates the recent slope, so it turns almost with price. Use its turn as a trigger, never as support — it overshoots.
  • T3: a six-fold EMA with a "volume factor" (Tillson's default 0.7) that lets it hug price without the Hull's overshoot. The smoothest of the four; use as a trend line.
  • McGinley Dynamic: MD = MD₁ + (P − MD₁) / (0.6 × N × (P / MD₁)⁴). The fourth power makes it speed up when price runs away and slow down when price is near — it tracks fast markets and refuses to whipsaw in slow ones. The best of the four as a trailing trend line.
  • ZLEMA: an EMA applied to price plus (price − price lagged), which cancels most of the lag. A fast crossover pair (ZLEMA 10/30) whipsaws far less than the EMA equivalent.

Trap: Less lag means more overshoot, always. A Hull crossover system will have more, faster, and worse signals than a plain EMA system in a range.

The 52-week-high effect

Thomas George and Chuan-Yang Hwang, Journal of Finance (2004).

The only technique in this chapter that comes from a peer-reviewed paper, and it is a good one. George and Hwang found that a stock's nearness to its 52-week high — current price divided by the trailing 52-week high — predicted subsequent returns better than past returns did, and that the effect did not reverse the way momentum does. Their explanation is anchoring: traders are reluctant to buy near a salient high, so good news is under-reacted to there, and the price drifts up as the reluctance fades.

  • Nearness = price / 52-week high. Rank the universe; the top decile has historically outperformed the bottom decile over the next six to twelve months.
  • Single-asset version: a breakout to a new 52-week high, especially after a long consolidation near it, is a with-trend entry with a known behavioral mechanism behind it.
  • Combine with Chapter 7's trend filters; the effect is strongest when the broad market is also near its highs.

Trap: Evidence is from equities, cross-sectionally. On a single futures contract or currency the anchoring story is weaker and the 52-week high is just a level.

Chapter 8 — Volatility Setups

Range contraction precedes range expansion. That sentence is the entire content of a body of work by Toby Crabel, John Bollinger, and Larry Williams, and it has held up better in testing than almost anything else in pattern-based trading. The specific patterns matter, though, because "range contraction" can be measured in a dozen ways and they are not equivalent.

NR7 and ID/NR4

Toby Crabel, Day Trading with Short Term Price Patterns and Opening Range Breakout (1990). Out of print; used copies sell for several hundred dollars.

NR7: the bar with the narrowest range of the last seven. ID/NR4: an inside bar (high below the prior high, low above the prior low) that is also the narrowest of the last four. Both are precise definitions of contraction, and Crabel tested them on daily futures data with the discipline of a statistician. The finding: the bar after an NR7 or ID/NR4 has a substantially wider range than average, and the direction of the breakout tends to persist for the session.

  • Identify the pattern at the close. Place a buy stop just above its high and a sell stop just below its low for the next bar.
  • When one fills, cancel the other and use the opposite extreme as the stop. Exit at the close of the session (Crabel's version) or trail with a volatility stop (Chapter 13).
  • Crabel found ID/NR4 stronger than NR7, and NR7 following two or more consecutive narrowing days strongest of all — the "NR7 after NR7" is rare and worth waiting for.

Trap: Trading the pattern in a market that has been quiet for weeks. Contraction is relative; an NR7 inside a dead range is just a dead bar.

The Stretch and the Opening Range Breakout

Crabel (1990).

Crabel's second contribution was a volatility-scaled definition of "breakout from the open." The Stretch is the ten-day average of the smaller of (high − open) and (open − low) — the typical amount of adverse excursion from the open before the day's real move. An ORB entry is placed at the open plus or minus the Stretch.

  • Stretch = average over 10 sessions of min(high − open, open − low).
  • Buy stop at open + Stretch; sell stop at open − Stretch. First fill wins; the other side becomes the stop.
  • Crabel's finding: an ORB taken on the day after an NR7 or ID/NR4 has the strongest edge. The contraction pattern picks the day; the Stretch picks the level.
  • In a 24-hour market, define the open yourself — the London open or the New York open are the usual anchors; test which one your symbols respect.

Trap: A Stretch computed over a period that included one enormous day. Crabel used ten days precisely because it was short enough to adapt and long enough to resist a single outlier; do not "improve" it to fifty.

The Squeeze and the head fake

Bollinger, Bollinger on Bollinger Bands (2001); the TTM Squeeze variant by John Carter (2000s).

Bandwidth — (upper − lower) / middle — at its lowest level in six months is Bollinger's Squeeze. His second rule is the one that matters: the first move out of a Squeeze is frequently a head fake — a break in one direction that reverses within a few bars and then makes the real move the other way.

  • Bollinger's Squeeze: 20-period, 2σ bands; bandwidth at a 125-bar low. Wait for the bands to begin expanding; enter in the direction of the expansion, but be prepared to reverse on the head fake.
  • Carter's TTM Squeeze: bands (20, 2) inside a Keltner channel (20, 1.5 ATR) = squeeze on. When the bands exit the channel, the squeeze fires; take direction from a momentum histogram at that bar.
  • The head-fake rule in practice: if the first breakout bar closes back inside the bands, take the opposite side on the next bar's break.

Trap: Entering on the squeeze itself. A squeeze is a condition, not a signal; the trade is the release, and the release can take weeks.

Volatility breakout

Larry Williams, Long-Term Secrets to Short-Term Trading (1999); versions trace to the 1980s.

The simplest expansion entry there is: buy if price moves a fixed fraction of yesterday's range above today's open. Because the threshold is scaled to recent range, it adapts automatically, and because it requires a move before entry, it filters the dead sessions out.

  • Buy stop at open + k × (previous range), k between 0.5 and 0.7. Sell stop at open − k × range. Williams' preferred k was around 0.6 for stock indices.
  • Combine with a directional filter — Williams used the prior day's close relative to its open, or a short-term trend — and take only the side the filter favors.
  • Exit at the close, or at the first profitable open (Chapter 13).

Trap: k too small in a market with a large session-open range. The order fills on noise and the stop on the other side fills on more noise.

Standard Error Bands

Jon Andersen, TASC, September 1996.

Bollinger Bands are built around a moving average and widen with volatility. Standard Error Bands are built around a linear regression line and widen with the scatter around the trend. That inverts the reading: tight bands mean price is hugging a trend (strong), and wide bands mean price is scattered around it (weak or turning). They answer "how orderly is this move" rather than "how volatile is it."

  • 21-period linear regression of the close. Bands at ±2 standard errors of the regression, each smoothed with a 3-period average.
  • Bands narrowing while the regression slopes = an orderly trend; hold. Bands widening = the trend is losing coherence; tighten stops.
  • Price breaking outside a narrow band is a shock to the trend — treat it as the start of a new regime, not as an extreme to fade.

Trap: Reading them like Bollinger Bands. A touch of the upper Standard Error Band in a tight, sloping channel is confirmation, not exhaustion.

STARC bands

Manning Stoller, 1980s; "Stoller Average Range Channel."

A short simple average with bands at a multiple of ATR. Stoller's reading was risk-based rather than signal-based: a purchase made near the lower band has a small distance to the stop and a large distance to the upper band, so its reward-to-risk is favorable regardless of what happens next. He traded the bands as places where the odds were acceptable, not as predictions.

  • SMA(5 or 6) ± 2 × ATR(15). STARC+ is the upper band, STARC− the lower.
  • Buy near STARC− with a stop just beyond it; take profit toward the average or STARC+. Mirror at STARC+.
  • In a trend, only take the with-trend band: STARC− buys in an uptrend, STARC+ sells in a downtrend. The counter-trend band is where you exit, not enter.

Trap: Fading STARC+ in a trend. The bands are narrow (2 ATR) and a trending market rides the upper one for days.

Widner's projection bands and projection oscillator

Mel Widner, TASC, July 1995.

Donchian channels use the raw highest high and lowest low, which lag by construction. Widner's bands project each of the last n bars' highs and lows forward along the current regression slope before taking the maximum and minimum — so a rising market's band rises with it instead of waiting for a new high. The projection oscillator is %R computed inside those bands.

  • Period 14. Upper band = max over n of (high + slope × bars ago); lower band = min of (low + slope × bars ago), where slope is the n-period regression slope.
  • Projection oscillator = (close − lower) / (upper − lower) × 100. Read as a stochastic with much less lag; 80/20 extremes, and divergences.
  • Its virtue in a trend: the oscillator does not pin at 100 the way %R does, because the band moves with the trend. Pullbacks register as pullbacks.

Trap: In a sharp reversal the projection is wrong by the full slope, and the bands lag more than Donchian's for a few bars. Do not use it for the first bars after a break.

Bollinger's %b and the W-bottom

Bollinger (2001).

%b is where the close sits relative to the bands: 1 at the upper band, 0 at the lower, above 1 outside. Bollinger's use of it was to identify a specific bottom: a W in which the second low is lower in price but higher in %b, because the bands have moved down with it. Price made a new low; relative to the volatility envelope, it did not.

  • W-bottom: first low at or below the lower band (%b near or below 0). Rally. Second low, lower in price, but with %b higher than at the first low (inside the band). Buy the break of the intervening high.
  • M-top: mirror — second high higher in price, lower in %b.
  • "Walking the band": consecutive closes with %b above 0.8 is a trend, and Bollinger's rule was never to sell a walk. The first close with %b below 0.5 after a walk is the exit.

Trap: Buying the second low on the %b reading alone. The trigger is the break of the middle high; without it, the W is a downtrend with a bounce in it.

Fisher's ACD

Mark Fisher, The Logical Trader (2002). The house method at MBF Clearing, one of the largest floor operations in New York.

An opening-range framework with a scoring system attached. The opening range is the first few minutes of the session (Fisher used five to thirty depending on the market). A-levels sit a fixed distance above and below it, scaled to the market's recent range; a move through an A-level that holds is an A-up or A-down, and the day's bias follows it. C-levels are wider and apply later in the session. The pivot range from the previous session is the reference for whether the A-up is meaningful. The number line scores each day's outcome and sums over 30 days to produce a longer-term bias.

  • A-up = opening-range high + A value; A-down = opening-range low − A value. Fisher set the A value at a fraction of the average daily range, roughly a tenth for most markets.
  • An A-up that holds for a set time (a few minutes on a fast market, half an hour on a slow one) = long bias for the day, stop below the opening-range low. A failed A — price makes the A-up and then trades back through the opening range — is a reversal signal, and often the best trade of the day.
  • Pivot range: pivot = (H + L + C) / 3; second number = (H + L) / 2; range = pivot ± |pivot − second number|. An A-up above the pivot range is stronger than one below it.
  • Number line: score each day +1/+2 for A-ups and C-ups, −1/−2 for downs, sum the last 30 days. A sum at ±9 marks an extreme with a strong directional bias in Fisher's markets.

Trap: Copying Fisher's opening-range durations and A values from his examples. They were tuned per market by hand; yours must be too.

Chapter 9 — Stop Runs and Failed Breakouts

A breakout that fails is more informative than one that succeeds, because it tells you the buyers who chased it are now trapped. The patterns in this chapter are all the same pattern with different names: price exceeds an obvious level, triggers the stops and the breakout entries sitting there, and then reverses through the level. Osler's order-book research showed why — stop orders cluster just past round numbers and prior extremes — and these techniques are the trades built on it.

Turtle Soup and Turtle Soup Plus One

Raschke and Connors, Street Smarts (1995). Named for the Turtles' 20-day breakout system, which it fades.

  • Setup: today makes a new 20-bar low, and the previous 20-bar low occurred at least four bars ago (so the level is established, not fresh).
  • Entry: place a buy stop a small distance above the previous 20-bar low. If today's break reverses back through that level, you are long with the breakout traders trapped below.
  • Stop: just below today's low. Target: Raschke held for two to six bars or trailed; the pattern is a reversal of the day's excess, not a trend call.
  • Plus One: the same setup, but the entry is placed on the day after the new low. Slightly slower, noticeably fewer false triggers.

Trap: Turtle Soup in a genuine trend. When the 20-bar low breaks and does not reverse, that was the breakout, and the fade is the wrong side of the biggest move of the month. The four-bar rule and a higher-timeframe range condition exist to keep you out.

The Swing Failure Pattern

Trading-desk and forum folklore; the same logic as Turtle Soup on a single bar, and a close cousin of Sperandeo's 2B pattern.

Price wicks above a prior swing high (taking the stops and triggering the breakout buys) and then closes back below it, on the same bar. The bar's close is the tell: the market tried to hold the new high and could not.

  • Bearish SFP: bar high exceeds a prior swing high; bar close is below that swing high. Short on the close or on a break of the bar's low; stop above the wick.
  • Bullish SFP: mirror at a swing low.
  • Higher-timeframe SFPs (daily, weekly) are far more reliable than intraday ones, because the stops taken are larger.

Trap: An SFP on a bar that closes only marginally back inside. The pattern needs a decisive close; a close a few ticks back is a level being tested, not rejected.

Wyckoff springs and upthrusts

Richard Wyckoff, course materials (1930s); systematized by Hank Pruden, The Three Skills of Top Trading (2007).

A spring is a break below a trading range's support that fails and reverses. The Wyckoff addition is volume: a spring on low volume means there was no real supply below support — the break was a stop run — and the subsequent test on even lower volume confirms it. An upthrust is the mirror at resistance.

  • Range identified (Wyckoff's accumulation phases). Price breaks support. Watch volume on the break bar: low = spring; high with a close back inside = a spring with "stopping volume."
  • Entry: the test — price revisiting the spring low on lower volume and holding. Stop below the spring low.
  • The "sign of strength": a wide-range up bar on expanding volume leaving the range; Wyckoff's confirmation that markup has begun.

Trap: A spring on high volume that closes at the low. That is not a spring; it is a breakdown with participation.

Larry Williams' Smash Day

Williams (1999).

A bar that closes below the prior bar's low is a "smash" — it looks like a breakdown. Williams' observation: if the next bar trades back above the smash bar's high, the breakdown has failed and the trapped sellers fuel a reversal.

  • Down smash: close below prior bar's low. Buy stop at the smash bar's high for the next bar; stop below the smash bar's low.
  • Up smash: close above prior bar's high. Sell stop at the smash bar's low.
  • Williams' "hidden smash": a bar that closes in the bottom quarter of its range but above the prior low. Same trade, weaker.

Trap: Smash days at the start of a trend rather than the end of a move. Williams took them after extended moves, where the "smash" was the last spasm.

Volume Spread Analysis

Tom Williams, The Undeclared Secrets That Drive the Stock Market (1993); later Master the Markets.

VSA reads three things per bar — range (the "spread"), close position within the range, and volume — and infers what professional money did. It is Wyckoff with a vocabulary.

  • No demand: an up bar with a narrow range on volume lower than the previous two bars. The rally has no buyers behind it; bearish in a downtrend.
  • Stopping volume: a wide down bar on very high volume that closes off its low. Someone absorbed the selling; bullish if followed by a test.
  • Upthrust: a bar that trades above the prior high and closes near its low on high volume. Distribution.
  • Test: a narrow down bar on low volume after stopping volume. No supply left; buy.

Trap: Reading VSA bars in isolation. Every signal is conditional on the background — the same bar means different things in an uptrend and after a climax.

Sperandeo's 1-2-3 and 2B

Victor Sperandeo, Trader Vic: Methods of a Wall Street Master (1991).

Two of the most cited and least attributed patterns in trading. The 1-2-3 is a trend-change definition: (1) the trendline breaks, (2) price tests the prior extreme and fails to exceed it, (3) price breaks the intervening swing. All three must occur; two of three is a warning. The 2B is a stop-run reversal: in an uptrend, price makes a new high, fails to follow through, and drops back below the previous high. Sperandeo's 2B is the daily-bar ancestor of every "sweep" pattern.

  • 1-2-3 top: trendline broken; rally fails to make a new high; break below the low between the high and the failed rally. Sell on (3), stop above (2).
  • 2B top: new high, then a close (or a trade, on short timeframes) back below the prior high. Sell there, stop above the new high. Sperandeo's timing rule: on daily bars the 2B should resolve within a day or two; on intermediate swings, within three to five days.
  • The 2B works because the breakout buyers and the stops above the old high are both trapped by the same move.

Trap: A 2B that takes a week to fail. If price holds above the old high for many bars, it is a breakout consolidating, and the eventual dip through the level is a pullback, not a 2B.

The Ross Hook and the Traders Trick Entry

Joe Ross, Trading by the Book (1991) and Trading the Ross Hook (1996).

After a breakout — from a 1-2-3, a ledge, or a trading range — the first bar that fails to make a new high in the direction of the breakout is a Ross Hook. Ross's observation was that the breakout of the hook is the most reliable continuation entry, and that everyone knows it, so the stop orders sit just above the hook and the fill is bad. The Traders Trick Entry enters during the correction, before the hook breaks, so that the crowd's breakout entry becomes your first profit.

  • Identify the hook: the first bar after a breakout whose high is below the prior bar's high (for an uptrend).
  • The correction that follows must be short — Ross allowed no more than three bars. Longer, and the setup is void.
  • TTE: on any correction bar, place a buy stop above that bar's high. Stop below the correction's low. When the hook itself breaks, the crowd's entry runs your position into profit; take partial there.

Trap: Taking the TTE on the fourth correction bar. Ross's three-bar limit is the whole filter; a longer correction is a range forming, and the hook breakout fails.

Liquidity sweeps, order blocks, and fair value gaps

Michael Huddleston ("ICT"), free online lectures (2010s onward), originally for forex. The largest body of trading technique that has never been in a book — and never formally tested.

Strip the vocabulary and the framework is three mechanical claims: (1) price is drawn to liquidity — clusters of stops above equal highs and below equal lows — and sweeps it before reversing; (2) the last opposing candle before an impulsive move (an order block) marks where the institution accumulated, and price returns to it; (3) a three-candle move that leaves a gap between candle one's high and candle three's low (a fair value gap) is an imbalance that price tends to revisit. Each is testable, and each is a restatement of Osler's stop clustering, the retest, and gap-fill logic.

  • Sweep: equal highs (within a tick or two) mark resting buy-stops. A wick through them followed by a close back below, then a break of the most recent swing low (a "market structure shift"), is the short. Identical to an SFP with a confirmation step.
  • Order block: after the structure shift, mark the last up-candle before the impulsive drop. The retest of that candle's range is the entry; stop above it.
  • Fair value gap: mark the three-candle gap on the impulse. Price returning to fill half or all of it is the alternative entry.
  • Huddleston's timing overlay — "kill zones" around the London and New York opens, and a "Judas swing" false move at the open — is the session-anchor logic of Chapter 8 in different words.

Trap: Hindsight. Every chart has equal highs, opposing candles, and three-candle gaps somewhere, and the community's examples are selected after the fact. Before trading any of it, measure the fill rate of fair value gaps and the retest rate of order blocks on your own data, with a null built from random levels.

Chapter 10 — Volume and Order-Flow Indicators

Anchored VWAP

Brian Shannon, Maximum Trading Gains with Anchored VWAP (2023); the technique circulated in his work for a decade before.

A session VWAP resets every day, which makes it meaningless for anything longer. An anchored VWAP starts at a moment you choose — a swing low, a swing high, an earnings release, an IPO, a year boundary — and represents the average price paid by everyone who has traded since that moment. Price above the AVWAP from a low means the average buyer since the low is in profit; price falling through it means they are not, and their behavior changes.

  • Anchor at the most significant recent swing low and swing high. Price between them is contested; price above the high-anchored AVWAP is in a position where even the top-tickers are back in profit.
  • The pinch: when the AVWAP from a high and the AVWAP from a low converge, the average buyer and average seller are at the same price. Breakouts from a pinch are directional and fast.
  • Pullbacks to a rising AVWAP from a major low are the swing-trade buy; the first close below it after a long uptrend is the swing-trade exit.

Trap: Anchoring to arbitrary points. The anchor has to be a moment when a large group of participants entered; a random Tuesday is not one.

VWAP standard-deviation bands

Floor and institutional desks; formalized in Brett Steenbarger's and others' intraday work (2000s).

Bands at one and two standard deviations of price around the session (or anchored) VWAP. Unlike Bollinger Bands, which are centered on a moving average, these are centered on the actual volume-weighted average price, so a touch of the second band means the market is two deviations from where the average participant transacted.

  • Intraday reversion: fade the second band back toward VWAP when the session is range-bound (Choppiness above 61.8 or Efficiency Ratio low).
  • Intraday trend: price holding above the first band all session is a trend day; do not fade it.
  • The first band acts as support on trend days — buy the pullback to +1σ, stop below VWAP.

Trap: Fading the second band on a trend day. The bands expand with the trend and the fade is a fade of the day's entire direction.

Volume Profile: POC, value area, naked POC

Peter Steidlmayer's Market Profile (CBOT, 1980s); Jim Dalton, Mind Over Markets (1990).

A histogram of volume by price instead of by time. The point of control (POC) is the price with the most volume; the value area is the range containing 70% of the volume around it. High-volume nodes are prices the market agreed on; low-volume nodes are prices it rejected — and price moves fast through rejected zones and slow through accepted ones.

  • Session profile: trade rotations inside the value area; treat its edges as the reference levels.
  • Naked POC: a prior session's POC that price has not revisited. It acts as a magnet — a large fraction of naked POCs get tested within a few sessions.
  • Low-volume nodes between two high-volume nodes are the fast lanes; a breakout through one usually runs to the next high-volume node before pausing.
  • Composite profiles over weeks identify the big value areas that daily traders cannot see.

Trap: A profile built from a single venue's volume in a market where volume is spread across a dozen. Aggregate first, or use the venue that leads.

The 80% rule

Dalton (1990), from Market Profile floor practice.

If a session opens outside the previous session's value area and then re-enters it, and holds inside for two consecutive half-hour periods, there is roughly an 80% chance (Dalton's figure, from floor observation) that price traverses the entire value area to the opposite side. The logic: the open outside value was a failed auction; the return to value means the market has accepted the old range, and it rotates through it.

  • Requires: open outside prior value area; price re-enters; two consecutive 30-minute bars close inside.
  • Entry on the second close inside; target the far edge of the value area; stop back outside the near edge.
  • For 24-hour markets, define the "session" by your chosen anchor (Chapter 8) and use the prior session's profile as the value area.

Trap: Applying it after the first bar back inside. The two-period acceptance is the whole rule; one bar inside is often a probe before the trend resumes.

Cumulative Volume Delta: absorption and exhaustion

Order-flow desks and footprint-chart practitioners (2010s); Bookmap and Sierra Chart communities.

Delta is the volume executed at the ask (aggressive buys) minus the volume executed at the bid (aggressive sells), per bar. CVD is its running total. It tells you who is initiating. Price tells you who is winning. When they disagree, something is being absorbed.

  • Absorption: CVD rising sharply while price does not advance. Aggressive buyers are hitting a wall of passive sellers. Bearish — when the aggressors give up, there is nothing holding price.
  • Exhaustion: a very large delta spike at a new extreme followed by no follow-through. The last aggressive participants have entered. Reversal setup, especially with an SFP (Chapter 9).
  • Divergence: price higher high, CVD lower high. The second high was made with fewer aggressors. Weakness.
  • Build CVD from spot trades on a liquid venue, where the taker side is reported per trade.

Trap: CVD from a venue where you cannot tell taker side reliably. Tick-rule classification (uptick = buy) is a poor substitute in a market that prints inside the spread.

OBV trendlines

Joseph Granville, Granville's New Key to Stock Market Profits (1963).

On-Balance Volume adds the bar's volume on up-closes and subtracts it on down-closes. Granville's actual technique was not divergence; it was drawing trendlines and support/resistance on OBV itself and trading their breaks, on the theory that volume leads price. He was frequently right and frequently early.

  • Draw trendlines on OBV as on price. An OBV trendline break with price still inside its own line is the signal; expect price to follow within a few bars.
  • OBV making a new high while price is still below its prior high is Granville's strongest buy — accumulation completed before the breakout.
  • Smooth OBV with a short moving average on thin symbols to prevent one block from dominating.

Trap: OBV's level is path-dependent and arbitrary; only its shape and slope mean anything. Never compare OBV values across symbols or across a data gap.

Weis Wave

David Weis, Trades About to Happen (2013); Wyckoff's "wave volume" made mechanical.

Bar volume is noise. Weis accumulated volume across each swing — every up-wave and down-wave defined by a zigzag — and compared the totals. Effort (wave volume) versus result (wave length) is Wyckoff's central idea, and the Weis Wave is the only indicator that measures it directly.

  • Define waves with a zigzag of a chosen reversal size (an ATR multiple). Sum the volume within each wave; plot as a histogram, up-waves and down-waves distinguished.
  • Shrinking volume on successive up-waves in a rally: demand is fading. Large volume on a down-wave that travels a short distance: absorption — buyers are taking the supply.
  • The largest wave volume of the move at a new extreme with little price progress is the climax. Expect a test on lower wave volume.

Trap: A zigzag threshold that is too small produces a wave for every wiggle and the volume comparison means nothing. Size the reversal to your trading timeframe's swings.

Market Profile: initial balance, day types, and unfinished business

Steidlmayer; Dalton, Mind Over Markets (1990) and Markets in Profile (2007).

Beyond the POC and value area (previous cards), the profile carries a set of structural tells about who is participating and what is left undone.

  • Initial balance: the range of the first hour. A day that extends beyond it has "other-timeframe" participation; the size and direction of the extension classify the day.
  • Day types: normal (wide IB, little extension), trend (narrow IB, one-directional extension all day — do not fade), neutral (extension both ways — fade the edges), double-distribution (two value areas separated by single prints — trade the gap between them).
  • P and b shapes: a profile shaped like a P is short covering (volume concentrated high after a rally); a b is long liquidation. Both are one-sided auctions that tend to be followed by a return toward the thin side.
  • Single prints: a stretch of the profile only one TPO wide — price moved through it fast. It is likely to be revisited. Poor highs and lows: a flat extreme with several TPOs at the same price — an unfinished auction, likely to be repaired. Excess: a single-print tail at the extreme — a finished auction, likely to hold.

Trap: Trading a poor high as a target on the same day it forms. Repairs happen, but on their own schedule; the poor high is a level to carry forward, not a promise.

Footprint patterns: stacked imbalances and unfinished auctions

Order-flow practitioners on footprint charts (2010s).

A footprint bar shows volume traded at the bid and at the ask, per price level. Two patterns have earned their reputation.

  • Stacked imbalances: at three or more consecutive price levels, ask volume exceeds the diagonally adjacent bid volume by 300% or more (or the reverse). Aggressive participants stepped in with size across several ticks. The zone acts as support on a retest; if it fails, those participants are trapped and the move accelerates.
  • Unfinished auction: the bar's high (or low) has volume on both sides — no zero print at the extreme. The auction did not complete; price tends to return to that level. A finished auction has a zero on the ask at the high.
  • Delta at the extreme: a bar closing at its high with negative delta — aggressive sellers, and price rose anyway — is absorption by passive buyers. Bullish. The mirror is bearish.

Trap: The imbalance threshold. 300% at a level with 12 contracts is noise; at 1,200 it is a participant. Require a minimum absolute size.

Reading the order-book heatmap

Bookmap and Jigsaw communities (2010s).

A heatmap plots resting limit-order size at each price over time. It is the only view that shows intent — what participants are willing to do — rather than what they did. Most of that intent is theater, which is what makes it readable.

  • Walls that appear as price approaches and vanish as it arrives are spoofs; the tell is the pull. Walls that stay and absorb are real, and price usually rejects them on first contact.
  • Icebergs: executed volume at a level far exceeds what was displayed, and the displayed size refreshes. Someone is working a large order passively. Price tends to hold that level until the iceberg is done, then move fast.
  • Vacuum: a stretch of price with almost no resting orders. When price enters it, it moves through fast — the low-volume node of the profile, seen from the book side.

Trap: Trading against a wall because it "must" hold. Walls are pulled in milliseconds. Trade the pull (a fade of the level fails) or the absorption (executed volume with no movement), never the display.

Point and Figure counts

Victor de Villiers (1933); A.W. Cohen (1947); Thomas Dorsey, Point and Figure Charting (1995).

Point and Figure removes time entirely and records only reversals of a chosen size. Its unique contribution is the count — a price target derived from the size of the base, on the principle that the energy stored in a consolidation is proportional to its width.

  • Box size: 1–3% of price, or an ATR fraction. Reversal: three boxes.
  • Vertical count: after a bottom, count the boxes in the first up-column. Target = low + (boxes × 3 × box size).
  • Horizontal count: measure the width of the base in columns. Target = low + (columns × 3 × box size). The horizontal count is the one Cohen trusted for large moves.
  • A count is activated on the breakout above the base and cancelled if price breaks the base's low.

Trap: Counting a base that is not one. The horizontal count needs a clean, bounded consolidation; a sloping mess of columns produces a number with no meaning.

Chapter 11 — DeMark Counts and Lines

Tom DeMark's indicators were built for institutional clients and published in The New Science of Technical Analysis (1994) and DeMark on Day Trading Options (1999), both dense and expensive. TD Sequential in particular has spread widely through social-media chart analysis, usually with the rules garbled. These are the actual rules.

TD Sequential — Setup

DeMark (1994).

A trend-exhaustion count. A buy setup is nine consecutive bars, each closing below the close four bars earlier. A sell setup is nine consecutive closes above the close four bars earlier. The count measures persistence of directional closing, not magnitude, and completes when the persistence has run long enough to be unusual.

  • Count begins after a "price flip": a close below the close four bars earlier that follows a close above the close four bars earlier (for a buy setup).
  • Nine consecutive qualifying closes complete the setup. Interrupted counts restart.
  • Perfection: a buy setup is perfected when the low of bar 8 or bar 9 is below the lows of bars 6 and 7. Unperfected setups often extend.
  • A completed 9 is a pause-or-reverse warning, not an entry. Most 9s produce a one-to-four-bar reaction. Trade the reaction; do not assume the trend has ended.

Trap: Treating every 9 as a reversal. DeMark's statistics show the 9 is a reaction signal; the reversal signal is the 13.

TDST and Countdown

DeMark (1994).

TDST (TD Setup Trend) is the extreme of the completed setup: the highest high of a buy setup's nine bars is TDST resistance; the lowest low of a sell setup is TDST support. A close beyond TDST means the trend has continued through exhaustion — and cancels the setup's reversal implication. Countdown begins after a completed setup and is the real exhaustion count.

  • Buy countdown: after a buy setup completes, count bars that close at or below the low two bars earlier. Bars need not be consecutive. Thirteen completes it.
  • Countdown 13 is DeMark's reversal signal. Enter on a subsequent price flip (close above the close four bars earlier) or on a TD Sequential "9-13-9" — a new setup in the opposite direction.
  • Cancellation: a close above TDST resistance during a buy countdown cancels it. A new sell setup completing also cancels it.
  • TD Combo: the same count starting from bar 1 of the setup with stricter conditions (close below prior close, low below prior low, close below low two bars earlier). Faster, rarer.

Trap: Counting Countdown as consecutive bars. It is not; the 13 can take fifty bars to arrive, and that is when it matters most.

TD Lines

DeMark (1994).

Conventional trendlines are subjective. TD Lines are drawn through TD Points — a TD Point Low is a low with a higher low on each side (level 1); level 2 requires two higher lows on each side. A TD Demand Line connects the two most recent TD Point Lows, drawn from right to left, so it always uses the most recent structure. TD Supply Lines connect TD Point Highs.

  • Draw only through the two most recent TD Points of the chosen level. When a new TD Point forms, redraw.
  • Breakout qualifiers: DeMark accepted an upside break of a supply line only if, for example, the previous bar's close was a down close (the break comes from weakness, trapping shorts), or the open gapped above the line and above the prior close. Unqualified breaks are ignored.
  • Price objective: the distance from the line to the lowest low beneath it (for a supply-line break), projected from the breakout point.

Trap: Drawing TD Lines through level-1 points on a noisy timeframe. Level 2 or higher on daily bars is where the objectivity actually reduces false breaks.

TD REI and DeMarker

DeMark (1994).

Two DeMark oscillators built from bar-to-bar range comparisons rather than closes. The Range Expansion Index sums the changes in highs and lows over five bars, with a condition that suppresses the reading in a fast trend so that it does not register overbought while price is running. The DeMarker compares the sum of upward high-to-high moves against the total of up and down moves over 13 bars. DeMark's distinctive rule for both is about duration.

  • REI: readings above roughly +45 are overbought, below −45 oversold. A reading that stays past the threshold for six bars or more is a trend, and the signal is the exit from the zone, not the entry into it.
  • DeMarker: 13 periods; 0.30 and 0.70. Same duration rule: brief extremes reverse; prolonged extremes trend.
  • Both are meant to be read with TD Sequential — an REI extreme coinciding with a setup 9 is the confluence DeMark looked for.

Trap: Fading a prolonged reading. DeMark's whole point with these two was that an oscillator stuck at an extreme is the strongest trend signal there is.

Chapter 12 — Median Lines and Geometry

Andrews' Pitchfork and Hagopian's rule

Alan Andrews, "Action-Reaction" course (1960s–70s); documented by Patrick Mikula, The Best Trendline Methods of Alan Andrews (2002).

Three pivots: A (a significant extreme), B and C (the next swing high and low, or low and high). The median line runs from A through the midpoint of BC; parallels through B and C form the fork. Andrews' claim was that price returns to the median line about 80% of the time — which makes the median line the target, and the failure to reach it the more interesting signal.

  • Draw A-B-C on the timeframe of the move you are trading. The median line is the expected path of the trend's center.
  • Price reaching the median line: expect a reaction. Price reaching the upper parallel: expect a larger reaction or a break.
  • Hagopian's rule (George Hagopian, Andrews' student): if price fails to reach the median line and reverses, it will travel farther in the reverse direction than it moved before the failure. The failure to reach is a trade.
  • Warning lines: parallels at one and two fork-widths beyond the outer parallels, for markets that overshoot the fork.
  • Schiff variant: shift A halfway toward B (in price) for steep moves; modified Schiff shifts it halfway in both price and time.

Trap: Choosing pivots to make the fork fit. The pivots are the last three significant swings, chosen before you look at whether the fork "works."

Fibonacci confluence clusters

Desk practice; systematized by Robert Fischer, Fibonacci Applications and Strategies for Traders (1993), and Carolyn Boroden's work (2000s).

A single retracement level is a coin flip. A price where a retracement of one swing, an extension of another, and a prior structural level all fall within a small band is not — because participants using each of the three methods converge there. The technique is the cluster, not the ratio.

  • Compute 0.382/0.5/0.618/0.786 retracements of the last two or three swings, and 1.272/1.618 extensions of the last completed swing. Overlay prior swing highs and lows and the AVWAPs (Chapter 10).
  • A cluster is three or more levels within roughly half an ATR. Trade the cluster with a stop beyond the far edge of the band.
  • Boroden's timing addition: project Fibonacci ratios of the duration of prior swings forward in time; a price cluster arriving at a time cluster is the highest-confidence version.

Trap: Enough swings produce a level everywhere. Limit the swings to the last two or three significant ones on your trading timeframe, and require a structural level in the cluster, not just ratios.

Measured moves and AB=CD

Old chartist practice; the AB=CD form from H.M. Gartley, Profits in the Stock Market (1935), revived by Scott Carney.

Corrective swings tend to reproduce the size of the prior impulse. AB=CD: a swing AB, a retracement BC, and a projected swing CD equal in length to AB (or 1.272 or 1.618 times it). The pattern gives a target for the trend and a reversal zone at its end.

  • Identify AB (impulse) and BC (retracement of 0.382–0.786). Project CD = AB from C.
  • Completion zone: where CD = AB coincides with a cluster (previous card). That is the reversal zone; Gartley's version adds a 0.786 retracement of the larger XA leg at D.
  • Time symmetry: Gartley expected CD to take roughly as long as AB. A CD that arrives much faster tends to overshoot to 1.272 or 1.618.

Trap: Trading the completion zone without a reversal trigger. The zone is where to look for an SFP or a VSA bar; it is not the entry by itself.

Floor pivots and the Camarilla equation

Floor pivots: CBOT floor tradition. Camarilla: Nick Scott (1989).

Both derive intraday levels from the prior session's high, low, and close. Floor pivots are the ones every platform draws. The Camarilla levels are less known and were built for a different purpose: the inner levels are for fading, the outer levels for breakouts.

  • Floor: P = (H + L + C) / 3; R1 = 2P − L; S1 = 2P − H; R2 = P + (H − L); S2 = P − (H − L). Trade rotations between S1 and R1 on range days; a break of R2 or S2 is a trend day.
  • Camarilla: H3 = C + (H − L) × 1.1 / 4; H4 = C + (H − L) × 1.1 / 2; L3 and L4 mirror. Scott's rules: sell at H3 with a stop above H4; buy at L3 with a stop below L4. If H4 breaks, go long the breakout; if L4 breaks, short it.
  • The 1.1 factor and the 4 and 2 divisors are Scott's; they place H3/L3 at about 27% of the prior range from the close and H4/L4 at about 55%.

Trap: Fading H3 on a day that opens above it. Scott's levels assume the open is inside the H3–L3 band; an open outside it is already a breakout.

The harmonic zoo: Bat, Butterfly, Crab

Scott Carney, The Harmonic Trader (1999) and Harmonic Trading (2004–2010), extending Gartley.

Carney's contribution was to notice that the Gartley's ratios were one case of a family, and to catalogue the others by where point D — the completion — falls relative to the XA leg. Each pattern is a five-point structure (X-A-B-C-D) with a specific B retracement and a specific D completion.

  • Bat: B retraces 0.382–0.50 of XA; D completes at 0.886 of XA. The shallowest B; D lands just short of X.
  • Butterfly: B at 0.786 of XA; D extends beyond X to 1.27 (up to 1.618) of XA. A failed-breakout structure.
  • Crab: B at 0.382–0.618; D at 1.618 of XA — the most extreme extension, and Carney's highest-reward pattern because the reversal zone is so far from the structure.
  • All complete at a cluster (Chapter 12's confluence card) and all require a reversal trigger at D — an SFP, a VSA bar, or a divergence — before entry.

Trap: Ratio-fitting. With tolerances of a few percent on four ratios, some pattern matches almost any five swings. Carney's own rule was that the B ratio must be precise; if B is wrong, the pattern is not there.

Chapter 13 — Exits Nobody Talks About

Chuck LeBeau spent his career arguing that exits matter more than entries and that almost nobody studies them. He was right on both counts. These are the exits from his work and from Cynthia Kase's, plus Larry Williams' single most useful contribution.

The Chandelier exit

Chuck LeBeau, Technical Traders Guide to Computer Analysis of the Futures Market (1992) and later newsletters.

A trailing stop hung from the highest high since entry, at a multiple of ATR below it. Because it hangs from the extreme rather than from the close, it does not tighten on a single wide up-bar and it never moves down.

  • Stop = highest high since entry − 3 × ATR(22). LeBeau's default; he tested 2.5 to 4.
  • Recalculate each bar; the stop only ratchets upward.
  • For a mean-reversion trade, do not use it; for trend trades it is the best general-purpose trailing exit in the literature.

Trap: A multiple below 2.5 in a volatile market. Normal pullbacks in a healthy trend run two to three ATRs, and a tight chandelier exits on every one.

The Yo-Yo exit

LeBeau.

The Chandelier's blind spot: a sudden reversal from a high that does not reach the stop for many bars while the trend is clearly over. The Yo-Yo hangs the stop from the most recent close instead of the highest high, so a sharp adverse bar exits immediately.

  • Stop = previous close − 2 × ATR(10) (for longs). Recomputed each bar from that bar's close; it can move down as well as up.
  • Run it alongside the Chandelier and exit on whichever triggers first. The Chandelier catches the slow bleed; the Yo-Yo catches the shock.

Trap: Using the Yo-Yo alone. Because it can move down, it is not a trailing stop and will let a slow reversal run.

Kase DevStop

Cynthia Kase, Trading With the Odds (1996).

Kase's observation: the adverse excursion of a trend, measured over two-bar ranges, is roughly normally distributed once you correct for skew. So the stop should sit at one, two, or three standard deviations of that excursion below the trend — with the number of deviations set by how much you are willing to be shaken out.

  • Compute the two-bar true range over a 30-bar window; take its mean and standard deviation. Stops at mean + 1σ, + 2σ, + 3σ below the highest close (longs).
  • Kase's warning line at 1σ: reduce size. The 2σ stop for normal exits; the 3σ stop as the never-cross line.
  • The skew correction (Kase applied a factor for the log-normal shape) is what makes the 3σ line rarely hit by noise.

Trap: Placing full size at the 1σ line. Kase designed the ladder for scaling out, not for a single exit.

The bailout exit

Larry Williams (1999).

For short-term pattern trades (Chapters 8, 9, 14), Williams' rule was to exit at the first profitable open — the first session that opens above your entry. It sounds too simple. It works because short-term patterns capture a one-to-two-bar edge, and holding past the first profitable open is holding for a different, weaker edge.

  • Enter on the pattern. At each subsequent session open, if the trade is profitable, exit at the open.
  • If not yet profitable, hold with the pattern's stop. Williams also used a time stop of a few bars.
  • In a 24-hour market, define the "open" as your session anchor (Chapter 8).

Trap: Using the bailout on a trend entry. It will exit every trend trade on bar two.

Parabolic SAR tuning and Wilder's Volatility System

Wilder (1978).

The Parabolic SAR is a stop that accelerates: it starts a set distance from the entry extreme and moves toward price faster the longer the trend runs, on the theory that time in a trend is borrowed. The defaults (acceleration 0.02, step 0.02, maximum 0.20) are Wilder's for commodities in the 1970s. Wilder's less-known Volatility System is simpler and, in his own tests, better.

  • SAR tuning: lower the maximum (0.10) for swing trades that should survive pullbacks; raise the initial step (0.03–0.04) for markets that trend fast and reverse fast. The maximum is the parameter that matters.
  • Volatility System: ARC = 3 × ATR(7) (Wilder tested 2.8–3.1). The stop is the most extreme close in your favor since entry, minus the ARC (for longs). Reverse when hit.
  • Wilder ran both always-in — the stop was also the reversal entry. Modern use is to take the stop and re-enter on a separate signal.

Trap: Parabolic SAR in a range. It reverses every few bars by design and will lose on nearly all of them; it is a trend-only exit.

Maximum Adverse Excursion

John Sweeney, Maximum Adverse Excursion: Analyzing Price Fluctuations for Trading Management (1996).

The stop level should come from your own trades, not from the chart. Sweeney's method: for every closed trade, record the worst point it reached against you before it closed (the MAE). Plot the MAEs of winners and losers separately. Winners cluster at small MAEs — they rarely go far against you before working. Beyond some MAE, almost no winners exist. That is your stop, and it was derived from evidence rather than from a swing low.

  • Log MAE (and its mirror, maximum favorable excursion) in ATR units for every trade. Needs at least a hundred trades to be meaningful.
  • Find the MAE beyond which fewer than, say, 10% of winners went. Place the stop there. Trades exceeding it were overwhelmingly going to lose anyway.
  • MFE gives the target the same way: the level most winners reached before giving back. Set a scale-out there.
  • Rerun the analysis per setup; the Turtle Soup has a different MAE profile from the Holy Grail.

Trap: Mixing regimes. An MAE distribution built in a quiet year sets stops that get hit by noise in a volatile one. Express it in ATR units and rebuild it periodically.

Chapter 14 — Short-Term Rhythm Methods

These are the methods built around a recurring one-to-three-bar rhythm: the tendency of a session that extends hard in one direction to be followed by a session that gives some of it back, and the tendency of that rhythm to persist for a few cycles. The oldest is George Douglass Taylor's, from a course he mimeographed in 1950. Most of the others descend from it.

The Taylor Trading Technique

George Douglass Taylor, The Taylor Trading Technique (1950); reinterpreted by Raschke and by George Angell.

Taylor's premise was that markets rotate through a three-session rhythm — a Buy day, a Sell day, and a Sell-Short day — driven by the way large operators accumulate on weakness and distribute on strength. The count is kept in a "book" and re-anchored to the rhythm of lows. The method is dense; the usable core is this:

  • Buy day: expect a low early in the session, ideally after price violates the prior session's low and fails to follow through. Buy that failure. Objective: a rally toward the prior high.
  • Sell day: sell the long into strength, ideally above the Buy day's high. Do not initiate shorts.
  • Sell-Short day: expect a high early, ideally after price exceeds the Sell day's high and fails. Short the failure. Cover on a decline toward the Buy day's low.
  • Recount when the rhythm breaks: the next session that makes a low first, then rallies, is a new Buy day.

Trap: Forcing the count. Taylor's own book is full of exceptions; the method is a rhythm to read, not a calendar to obey, and it fails outright in strong trends.

Momentum Pinball

Raschke and Connors, Street Smarts (1995).

A Taylor-style two-day pattern with a mechanical trigger. The indicator is a 3-period RSI of the one-day rate of change — Raschke called it the LBR/RSI — which is deliberately hypersensitive. An extreme reading identifies a session whose excess is likely to be given back the following session.

  • Compute the one-bar rate of change; compute a 3-period RSI of that series.
  • Reading below 30 at the close: buy setup for the next session. Buy a break above the first sub-period's high (Raschke used the first hour on daily bars); stop below that sub-period's low.
  • Reading above 70: sell setup, mirror.
  • Exit at the close of the entry session, or hold overnight and exit at the first profitable open (Chapter 13) if the session closed strongly in your favor.

Trap: Trading the setup without the trigger. The reading identifies the session; the break of the first sub-period is what confirms the excess is reversing rather than continuing.

The 80-20s

Raschke and Connors (1995), building on Taylor and on Steve Moore's statistical work.

A session that opens in the top 20% of its range and closes in the bottom 20% (or the reverse) has traveled nearly its whole range in one direction. Moore's statistics showed that the next session frequently reverses part of that move — but only after first extending it, which is the trap for early faders and the setup for this trade.

  • Setup bar: open in the top 20% of the range, close in the bottom 20%, and the range should be larger than average.
  • Next session: wait for price to trade a small amount below the setup bar's low. Then place a buy stop at the setup bar's low. You are buying the failure of the continuation.
  • Stop below the new session's low. Exit at the close.
  • Mirror for the sell side: open in the bottom 20%, close in the top 20%.

Trap: Buying the close of the setup bar. The edge is in the failed extension the next session, not in the setup bar's exhaustion.

Greatest Swing Value

Larry Williams (1999).

Williams' answer to "how far above the open is a real breakout": look at the recent sessions that closed down, and measure how far they rallied above their open before failing. The average of those failed rallies is the noise. A move beyond it, by a margin, is signal.

  • Buy swing value: for each of the last four sessions that closed below their open, take (high − open). Average them.
  • Buy stop for the next session at open + 1.8 × buy swing value. Williams' multiplier; he tested 1.5 to 2.
  • Sell swing value: on sessions that closed above the open, (open − low). Sell stop at open − 1.8 × sell swing value.
  • Filter with a trend or day-of-week bias and exit at the bailout.

Trap: Using all sessions instead of only the down-closing ones for the buy side. The point is to measure how far failed rallies go; including the successful ones inflates the threshold.

Larry Williams' Oops!

Williams, How I Made One Million Dollars Last Year Trading Commodities (1973); restated in 1999.

A gap-reversal pattern for markets with a session gap. Price opens below yesterday's low — the public sees a breakdown and sells the open. If price then trades back above yesterday's low, everyone who sold the open is wrong at once, and the covering carries price. Williams named it for the sound they make.

  • Buy Oops: open below the prior session's low. Place a buy stop at the prior low. If filled, stop below the session's low; exit at the close or the first profitable open.
  • Sell Oops: open above the prior high; sell stop at the prior high.
  • Williams' filter: the gap should be against the direction of the prior day's close, and the pattern is stronger on specific days of the week per market (next card).

Trap: Large gaps. A small gap through yesterday's low is a stop run; a large one is news, and the Oops entry gets hit and run over.

Trading day of week and day of month

Williams, Long-Term Secrets to Short-Term Trading (1999); the "TDW" and "TDM" filters.

Williams never traded a pattern without first asking which days it worked on. His tables showed that short-term patterns in stock index futures, bonds, and currencies had markedly different results by day of week — some patterns only paid on two of the five — and by position in the month, with the first few and last few trading days behaving differently from the middle. The technique is not a trade; it is a filter applied to every other card in this guide.

  • For any pattern, tabulate results by day of week. If the edge is concentrated in two days, trade only those days. A pattern that works uniformly across the week is rarer than one that does not.
  • Repeat by trading day of month (1st through last). Month-end and month-start effects persist because of institutional flows that do not care about your pattern.
  • Combine: Williams' best stock-index results came from a specific pattern on a specific weekday in a specific part of the month.

Trap: Five weekdays times twenty-two month-days is 110 cells, and some will look good by chance. Require the effect to persist across separate years, and keep an honest count of the cells you looked at.

Chapter 15 — Breadth, Sentiment, and Positioning

Indicators built from the market rather than from one chart. Breadth measures how many issues participate in a move; sentiment measures what the crowd is paying for; positioning measures what the commercials and the large speculators actually hold. All three are slow, all three are equity- or futures-specific, and all three catch turns that no single-instrument indicator can see.

Zweig Breadth Thrust

Martin Zweig, Winning on Wall Street (1986).

A 10-day exponential average of the daily ratio of advancing issues to advancing plus declining issues. A thrust occurs when the average goes from below 0.40 (fewer than 40% of issues rising — a washed-out market) to above 0.615 within ten trading days. It is rare — a few dozen signals since the 1940s — and it has one of the best forward-return records of any published indicator.

  • Compute the ratio daily on NYSE (or the relevant exchange) advances and declines; 10-day EMA.
  • Signal: the EMA crosses from below 0.40 to above 0.615 within ten sessions. The window is strict; a slower recovery does not count.
  • Forward returns after a thrust have historically been strongly positive over six to twelve months. Zweig's rule was to be fully invested after one.

Trap: Thrusts computed on an index with few constituents, or on a sector. Zweig's statistics are for the broad market.

McClellan Oscillator and Summation Index

Sherman and Marian McClellan (1969); Tom McClellan's later work.

The difference between a 19-day and a 39-day EMA of net advances (advances minus declines), usually ratio-adjusted for the number of issues. It is a breadth MACD. The Summation Index is its running total and measures the market's longer-term breadth trend.

  • Oscillator above +100: overbought breadth — but also, after a decline, a breadth thrust that marks the start of a new advance rather than its end. Below −100: oversold; a spike below −150 has historically marked washouts.
  • Divergence: the index at a new high with the oscillator at a lower high means fewer stocks are carrying the advance. The classic top warning.
  • Summation Index: crossing above zero confirms an uptrend in breadth; a Summation Index above +1,000 (ratio-adjusted) is a strong, broad market.

Trap: Reading +100 as a sell when it comes off a low. Context decides: the same reading is a thrust after a washout and an extreme after a long rally.

The Coppock Curve

Edwin Coppock, Barron's (October 1962); commissioned by the Episcopal Church to identify long-term buying opportunities.

Coppock reasoned that bear markets are like bereavements and that the average mourning period is eleven to fourteen months. So: the sum of an 11-month and a 14-month rate of change, smoothed with a 10-month weighted moving average. A buy signal is the curve turning up from below zero. It was designed to give only buy signals, and it has caught most major bottoms since it was published, usually a few months late.

  • Monthly closes. Coppock = 10-month WMA of (ROC(14) + ROC(11)).
  • Buy when the curve turns up from a negative value. Ignore turns above zero.
  • Variants on weekly and daily data exist; the monthly is the one with the track record.

Trap: Using it for exits. Coppock did not, and the curve's turn down from a high has no reliable record.

The Hindenburg Omen

Jim Miekka (1995), building on Norman Fosback's split-market work.

A crash-warning pattern with a memorable name and a poor record, included because it is asked about constantly and the underlying idea — a "split market" in which many issues make new highs and many make new lows on the same day — is sound even if the trigger is not.

  • Conditions (one version): NYSE new 52-week highs and new lows each exceed 2.2% of issues; new highs are no more than twice new lows; the 50-day average of the index is rising; the McClellan Oscillator is negative. Two occurrences within 36 days confirm.
  • Historical record: nearly every crash was preceded by an Omen; the great majority of Omens were followed by nothing. It is a necessary-not-sufficient condition.
  • Use the split-market reading as a risk gauge — reduce size, tighten stops — and never as a short signal.

Trap: Acting on it. Its false-positive rate would bankrupt anyone who shorted on every signal.

The COT Index

Larry Williams, Trade Stocks and Commodities with the Insiders (2005); Steve Briese, The Commitments of Traders Bible (2008).

The CFTC publishes weekly positions of commercial hedgers, large speculators, and small traders in every US futures market. Commercials are the producers and users of the commodity; they are net short most of the time as a hedge, and when their net position becomes unusually long, they are seeing something. The COT Index normalizes their net position against its own history.

  • COT Index = (current net − lowest net over the lookback) / (highest net − lowest net) × 100. Williams used 26 weeks; Briese used up to three years.
  • Commercial index above 80 = commercials unusually long = bullish setup; below 20 = bearish. The large-speculator index reads the opposite way.
  • It is a setup, not a trigger. Williams paired it with a trend-following entry on the price chart; the COT reading told him which direction to take breakouts in.

Trap: Timing. Commercials are early by weeks or months, and an extreme reading can persist through a large continuation of the move they are positioned against.

Put/call extremes and the VIX term structure

Options-desk practice; Larry McMillan and Jason Goepfert (SentimenTrader) on the put/call ratio; the term-structure reading from volatility traders (2000s).

Options prices are the crowd's bill for protection and for speculation. Two readings have earned a place.

  • Equity-only put/call ratio, 10-day average: readings at the top of its yearly range (heavy put buying) mark fear and have preceded rallies; readings at the bottom mark complacency. Use percentiles of the last year, not fixed numbers — the ratio's range drifts.
  • VIX term structure: the ratio of the 30-day VIX to the 3-month VIX (VIX3M). Above 1 — backwardation — means near-term fear exceeds longer-term fear; historically these readings cluster at panics and resolve upward for equities. Deep contango (ratio well below 0.9) marks complacency.
  • The VIX spike rule: a VIX that has fallen 20% or more from a recent spike high has, historically, marked the point at which the panic is over. A trigger for the fear readings above.

Trap: Fixed thresholds on the put/call ratio. Structural changes in options volume (index products, weekly expiries, retail flows) move its baseline; a reading that was extreme in 2010 is ordinary now.

Chapter 16 — Confluence and Timeframes

Elder's Triple Screen

Alexander Elder, Trading for a Living (1993).

The rule that organizes everything else in this guide. Decide the trend on a timeframe one order of magnitude above your trading timeframe; look for a counter-trend pullback on your trading timeframe with an oscillator; enter on a timeframe below it with a breakout trigger. Each screen filters the one below.

  • Screen 1 (weekly, if you trade daily): trend direction from a slow indicator — Elder used the MACD histogram's slope. Only trade in that direction.
  • Screen 2 (daily): an oscillator pullback against the weekly trend — a stochastic or Force Index dip in an uptrend.
  • Screen 3 (intraday or the next daily bar): a trailing buy stop above the previous bar's high, moved down each bar until filled.

Trap: Collapsing the screens. The technique's entire value is that no single timeframe is allowed to decide alone.

The two-family rule

Desk practice; a synthesis rather than an attribution.

Indicators come in families that measure the same thing: momentum (RSI, stochastic, MACD, TSI, Schaff all measure the rate of change of closes), volume (OBV, CVD, MFI, profile), structure (trendlines, TD Lines, pitchforks, clusters, AVWAP), and volatility (bands, ATR, Choppiness, NR patterns). Confluence within a family is not confluence — an RSI divergence confirmed by a stochastic divergence is one signal read twice.

  • Require agreement from at least two different families before entry. Momentum plus volume. Structure plus volatility.
  • Structure family for the level, volatility family for the timing, momentum or volume family for the confirmation, is the most robust three-family template.
  • Count families, not indicators, when you assess a setup's strength.

Trap: Five momentum oscillators on one chart all saying "oversold." That is one opinion, loudly.

Timeframe-tuned settings

Ehlers; Hurst; common practice at cycle-oriented desks.

The dominant cycle differs by timeframe, so an indicator's correct length differs by timeframe too — and it is rarely the default. A 14-period RSI on a 4-hour chart and a 14-period RSI on a daily chart are measuring different fractions of their respective cycles, and one of them is mistuned.

  • Measure the dominant cycle on each timeframe you use (Chapter 6, DPO card). Set oscillators to half of it.
  • Set moving averages to the full cycle or a whole multiple of it, so that a single cycle averages out.
  • Keep a table of tuned settings per symbol per timeframe and re-measure monthly.

Trap: Tuning to a cycle that does not exist on that timeframe. When the measurement is unstable across windows, use the default and move on.

Chapter 17 — The Grimoire Rules

  1. Read RSI by its range, not by 70/30. Bull range 40–80, bear range 20–60. The range shift is the trend change. Trade the reversals with the trend and the divergences against it.
  2. Draw trendlines on the indicator. RSI, OBV, and the 3-10 all break their lines before price breaks its own.
  3. Pinned means trending. A stochastic above 80, a Laguerre RSI at 1, a Schaff at 100 — none of these are overbought. Bernstein bought them.
  4. Tune the length to the cycle. Oscillator period equals half the dominant cycle; moving-average period equals the full cycle. Remeasure.
  5. Filter every setup with a trendiness measure — Efficiency Ratio, r², or Choppiness — that is not a moving-average slope.
  6. Contraction picks the day; the Stretch picks the level. NR7 and ID/NR4 into an opening-range breakout is the most-tested pattern in this guide.
  7. A failed breakout is a trade. Turtle Soup, SFP, spring, smash day, upthrust — one pattern, five names. Wait for the close back through the level.
  8. Anchor the VWAP to a moment that mattered. The pinch and the pullback to a rising AVWAP are the two swing trades it gives you.
  9. Delta tells you who initiated; price tells you who won. When they disagree, someone is absorbing.
  10. A DeMark 9 is a reaction; the 13 is the reversal. TDST cancels both.
  11. Trade the failure to reach the median line. Hagopian's rule is the pitchfork's best signal.
  12. Hang trend exits from the high; hang shock exits from the close. Chandelier and Yo-Yo together. Bail out short-term patterns at the first profitable open.
  13. Two families or no trade. Momentum, volume, structure, volatility. Confluence inside one family is one signal repeated.
  14. Set stops from your MAE distribution, not from the chart. Sweeney's method turns your own trade log into the stop level.
  15. A 2B, a sweep, and an Oops are the same trade. The public breaks a level; the level holds; the public is trapped. Sperandeo, Huddleston, and Williams named it three times.
  16. Breadth thrusts are regime changes. Zweig and McClellan readings off a washout are starts, not extremes.
  17. Every card is a hypothesis with a pedigree. The settings were the originator's, on the originator's market. Test on yours, with an honest count of what you tried.
