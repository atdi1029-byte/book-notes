## Foreword (Christopher Ebert)

N001 [SHOULD KNOW] Trading is a SKILL, not an inborn talent. No one is born a good trader — proficiency comes through learning and practice. The sheer number of traders (100M+) creates the illusion that trading is easy, but 95% wipe out their accounts. Even surpassing the average trader means likely failure — even the best traders in history (including Darvas) initially suffered devastating losses.

N002 [SHOULD KNOW] The Darvas method is one of the simplest trading strategies. Steve Burns verified it still works in modern markets — technology changed but market behavior is "practically the same." Burns built accounts over $250K from 2003-2007 using Darvas principles, then went to cash before the 2008 crash.

## Introduction (Steve Burns)

N003 [SHOULD KNOW] Darvas turned $25,000 into over $2 million in just 18 months, trading stocks over 40 years ago. His book is recommended reading by William J. O'Neil, founder of Investor's Business Daily.

N004 [NICE TO KNOW] Burns considers Darvas "one of the first modern day trend traders to explain his system publicly." Burns adds commentary on risk management, trading psychology, and the trend trading method so readers don't have to extract principles from the narrative alone.

## PART ONE: THE GAMBLER

## Chapter 1: Canadian Period (pp. 15-31)

N005 [MUST KNOW] Darvas was a professional dancer performing in nightclubs. His entry into stocks was pure accident — twin brothers Al and Harry Smith offered to pay him in stock (6,000 shares of BRILUND, a Canadian mining firm at $0.50/share) instead of money for a Toronto nightclub appearance. He bought the stock for $3,000 as a gesture when he couldn't keep the date. Two months later BRILUND was at $1.90 — he sold for ~$8,000 profit. This lucky win hooked him.

N006 [MUST KNOW] The "gambler phase" — Darvas knew nothing about stocks. He asked everyone he met for tips (nightclub patrons, rich people). "Whatever I was told to buy, I bought." He followed hunches, rumors, god-sent names, uranium-finds, oil strikes. He owned 25-30 stocks at once, all in small parcels. He was "the perfect pattern of the optimistic, clueless small operator."

N007 [MUST KNOW] The "pet stocks" trap — Darvas developed emotional attachments to certain stocks for irrational reasons (a friend recommended them, or he'd once made money on them). He treated them like family members, praised them, refused to see they were losing money. His pet stocks caused his heaviest losses.

N008 [MUST KNOW] The KAYRAND MINES lesson — bought 10,000 shares at $0.10, sold next day at $0.11 thinking he'd made $100 profit. Broker informed him commissions were $50 to buy and $50 to sell, plus transfer taxes. He'd actually taken a loss. He was so novice he didn't understand transaction costs.

N009 [SHOULD KNOW] The EASTERN MALARTIC lesson — a "statistically-minded broker" showed him a dividend check from KERR-ADDISON (famous gold company) worth 5x the original stock price. Broker recommended EASTERN MALARTIC based on production figures and financial analysis. Darvas bought 1,000 shares at $2.90, watched it go to $2.70, then $2.60, then $2.41 — sold at a loss. Early lesson that fundamental analysis from "experts" doesn't guarantee profits.

N010 [MUST KNOW] After 7 months of trading in Canada, Darvas reviewed his books and found he'd lost almost $3,000. His profit-and-loss accounting was "the whole sad story of defeat in microcosm": OLD SMOKY GAS & OILS bought at 19¢/sold at 10¢, KAYRAND bought at 12¢/sold at 8¢, REXSPAR bought at $1.30/sold at $1.10. He was still net positive only because of the initial BRILUND windfall. This was his wake-up call.

N011 [MUST KNOW] The Canadian advisory service scam — Darvas subscribed to financial advisory services for tips. They used manipulative language: "Buy this stock now before it is too late!" "The plutocrats of Wall Street have been trying to acquire all the stock... but we are only interested in the participation of investors of moderate means. People like you..." Every stock they recommended invariably dropped immediately after he bought. He later realized: when tipsters advise small operators to buy, the professionals who bought on inside information much earlier are selling. "They are not firstest with the mostest, but lastest with the leastest."

N012 [SHOULD KNOW] The CALDER BOUSQUET accident — bought 5,000 shares at 18¢ ($900) purely because he liked the name. Flew to Madrid for a dancing engagement. When he returned a month later it had doubled to 36¢. He sold for $900 profit. "It was doubly blind luck" — he would have sold at 22¢ if he'd been watching it, but being away in Spain with no access to Canadian stock quotes saved him from selling too soon. First accidental lesson in letting profits run.

N013 [MUST KNOW] By end of 1953, Darvas's $11,000 was down to $5,800. He transitioned from Canada to Wall Street via a friend's recommendation of broker Lou Keller. He added savings from his dancing career to bring his stake to $10,000.

## PART TWO: THE FUNDAMENTALIST

## Chapter 2: Entering Wall Street (pp. 33-55)

N014 [MUST KNOW] Wall Street gave Darvas false confidence. Broker Lou Keller suggested "safe stocks" with fundamental reasons (dividend increases, stock-splits, improved earnings). In the middle of the biggest bull market ever, it was hard NOT to make money. Three early trades — COLUMBIA PICTURES, NORTH AMERICAN AVIATION, KIMBERLY-CLARK — each netted ~$400 profit. $1,333.38 total in just a few weeks. He felt he was "in control."

N015 [MUST KNOW] Overtrading and churning — Darvas telephoned his broker "sometimes twenty times a day." If he didn't conduct at least one transaction daily he didn't feel he was "fulfilling my role in the market." He "reached out for fresh stocks like a child for new toys." In July 1954, a batch of 10 trades (AMERICAN BROADCASTING-PARAMOUNT, NEW YORK CENTRAL, GENERAL REFRACTORIES, AMERICAN AIRLINES) produced a net profit of only $1.89. The broker earned $236.65 in commissions on those same trades.

N016 [MUST KNOW] The "You cannot go broke taking a profit" trap — Darvas adopted this Wall Street saying. Case study: KAISER ALUMINUM. Bought at 63⅜, sold at 75 for $1,074.45 profit. Then jumped into BOEING (lost $403.25), MAGMA COPPER (lost $1,016.80), back into KAISER at 82 (sold 5 minutes later at 81¾ for a $115.61 loss). Total circular KAISER operation: net LOSS of $461.21. If he'd held KAISER from his original 63⅜ purchase to its ultimate sale at 81¾, he'd have PROFITED $1,748.75.

N017 [MUST KNOW] The RAYONIER case — same pattern. From Nov 1954 to Mar 1955, constantly jumping in and out as stock went from ~50 to ~100. Three trades netted $1,238.12 total. Then switched to MANATI SUGAR (lost $1,043.10). Net RAYONIER-MANATI profit: only $195.02. If he'd held his original November RAYONIER purchase and sold in April at 80, profit would have been $2,612.48.

N018 [SHOULD KNOW] The "Buy Cheap, Sell Dear" trap — led Darvas to the over-the-counter market searching for bargains. Bought illiquid stocks (PACIFIC AIRMOTIVE, COLLINS RADIO, GULF SULPHUR, DOMAN HELICOPTER, etc.). Discovered these stocks "stuck to my fingers like tar" — when he wanted to sell at 42, the bid was only 38. No specialists, no continuous market, no transparent pricing. He quickly abandoned OTC.

N019 [MUST KNOW] The insider-following attempt — Darvas read in The Wall Street Journal that SEC required officers/directors to report their stock transactions. He tried to follow insiders' buys and sells. It didn't work: by the time he found out about insider transactions, it was always too late. Also, insiders are human too — "they often bought too late or sold too soon." Key insight: "They might know all about their company but they did not know about the attitude of the market in which their stock was sold."

N020 [MUST KNOW] Darvas's first 7 rules emerging from the fundamentalist period:
1. Don't follow advisory services — they're not infallible
2. Be cautious with brokers' advice — they can be wrong
3. Ignore Wall Street sayings, no matter how ancient and revered
4. Don't trade OTC — only listed stocks with liquidity
5. Don't listen to rumors
6. The fundamental approach works better than gambling — study it
7. Hold one rising stock longer rather than juggle a dozen for short periods

N021 [MUST KNOW] The VIRGINIAN RAILWAY discovery — Darvas owned 100 shares bought at 29¾ in Aug 1954 but had completely forgotten about it because he was too busy day-trading other stocks. When he finally noticed it 11 months later, this quiet, dividend-paying stock had risen to 43½. It had made him $1,303.68 without any effort or anxiety. This was the seed of his insight: the stocks he forgot about outperformed the ones he actively traded.

## Chapter 3: My First Crisis (pp. 57-69)

N022 [MUST KNOW] Industry group analysis — Darvas learned stocks move in groups by industry. He started looking for: (a) the strongest industry group, then (b) the strongest company within that group. He'd read stock tables by industry group, not alphabetically. When one stock outperformed, he'd check its "brothers" in the same industry.

N023 [MUST KNOW] The fundamentalist rating system — Darvas discovered stock-rating services (like Standard & Poor's). Ratings from AAA (safest) through D (no apparent value). He set up comparison tables analyzing five steel companies: Bethlehem Steel, Inland Steel, U.S. Steel, Jones & Laughlin, Republic Steel. He compared earnings per share, estimated earnings, dividends across years. His analysis pointed clearly to JONES & LAUGHLIN — strong industry, B rating, ~6% dividend, best P/E ratio.

N024 [MUST KNOW] The JONES & LAUGHLIN disaster — Darvas went all-in. He mortgaged his Las Vegas property, borrowed against his insurance policy, took an advance from the Latin Quarter nightclub. On Sept 23, 1955, bought 1,000 shares at 52¼ on 70% margin. Cost: $52,652.30. Deposited $36,856.61 cash. On Sept 26, the stock began dropping. He was paralyzed, refused to sell because his research said it was worth $75. On Oct 10 it hit 44. His net loss was $9,069.18.

N025 [MUST KNOW] The crisis aftermath — "Gambling, tips, information, research, investigation, whatever method I tried to be successful in the stock market, had not worked out." He faced potential bankruptcy — might lose his Las Vegas property. Black despair. But then, desperately studying stock tables, he noticed TEXAS GULF PRODUCING — a stock rising purely on price action, with no fundamental reason he knew of. This was the seed of the transition to the technician phase.

N026 [MUST KNOW] TEXAS GULF PRODUCING — the pivotal trade. Bought 1,000 shares at prices between 37⅛ and 37½, total cost $37,586.26. Watched it rise anxiously. At 40 he had a "compelling temptation to sell" but for the first time in his career he refused to take a quick profit — he had the $9,000 J&L loss to recoup. Held for 5 weeks watching it obsessively (calling broker every hour). Sold at 43¼ for $42,840.43. Didn't recover the full $9,000 but recovered more than half. Key realization: "The stock that saved me from disaster was one about which I knew nothing. I picked it for one reason only — it seemed to be rising."

N027 [MUST KNOW] The fundamental-vs-technical epiphany — after TEXAS GULF PRODUCING, Darvas asked: "What was the value of examining company reports, studying the industry outlook, the ratings and the price-earnings ratios? The stock that saved me from disaster was one about which I knew nothing." The J&L crisis "was not wasted. It led me toward the glimmering of my theory."

## PART THREE: THE TECHNICIAN

## Chapter 4: Developing the Box Theory (pp. 71-93)

N028 [MUST KNOW] The M & M WOOD WORKING confirmation — Darvas noticed this stock rising from 15 to 23⅝ in Dec 1955, with increasing volume after a 5-week lull. No financial information service could tell him anything about it. He bought 500 shares at 26⅝, held patiently, sold at 33⅛ for $2,866.62 profit. Later learned it had been rising due to a secretly negotiated merger — another company was taking it over for $35/share. He'd sold just 2 points under the high. Key insight: "My buying, based purely on the stock's behavior, enabled me to profit from a proposed merger without knowing anything about it. I was an insider without actually being one."

N029 [MUST KNOW] The "tempestuous beauty" analogy — how to detect unusual stock activity: "If a tempestuous beauty were to jump on a table and do a wild dance, no one would be particularly astonished... But if a dignified matron were suddenly to do the same, this would be unusual and people would immediately say, 'There is something strange here — something has happened.'" Applied to stocks: if a usually inactive stock suddenly became active AND advanced in price, assume insiders with good information are behind it. By buying, "I would become their silent partner."

N030 [MUST KNOW] The PITTSBURGH METALLURGICAL lesson — Darvas bought 200 shares at 67 ($13,483.40) because it was "fast-moving, dynamic." It immediately dropped. Sold 10 days later at 57¾ for a $2,023.32 loss. Examining its history afterward, he discovered he'd bought at the TOP of an 18-point rise — "almost at the very point that I put money in it, it started to drift downward." He had bought "the right stock at the wrong time."

N031 [MUST KNOW] THE BOX THEORY — Darvas's core discovery. After studying hundreds of charts and stock tables, he realized: "Stock movements were not completely haphazard. Stocks did not fly like balloons in any direction. As if attracted by a magnet, they had a defined upward or downward trend, which, once established, tended to continue. Within this trend stocks moved in a series of frames, or what I began to call 'boxes'. They would oscillate fairly consistently between a low and a high point."

N032 [MUST KNOW] How the Box Theory works in practice:
- Boxes stack like a pyramid, one on top of another
- Stock bounces between top and bottom of its current box — this is NORMAL
- No bouncing = not a lively stock = probably won't rise dynamically
- Buy when stock moves into a HIGHER box (breaks above the ceiling)
- If stock falls below the FLOOR of its current box, it's falling to a lower box = SELL immediately
- Example: stock in 45/50 box. Reads: 45-47-49-50-45-47. Fine, it's bouncing normally. If it breaks to 48-52-50-55-51-50-53-52, it's establishing a new 50/55 box. Buy signal.
- If it falls to 44½ while supposedly in the 45/50 box — SELL, it's "all wrong"
- Box sizes vary: some stocks move in 10% frames, others 15-20%

N033 [MUST KNOW] The dancer-spring analogy: "Before a dancer leaps into the air he goes into a crouch to set himself for the spring." Stocks do the same — a stock that reacts from 50 to 45 after reaching 50 is "like a dancer crouching, ready for the spring-up." The 45 position also shakes out weak stockholders who mistake the reaction for a drop, enabling faster advance.

N034 [MUST KNOW] The LOUISIANA LAND & EXPLORATION timing problem — Darvas watched it form pyramiding boxes, identified the top of the last box at 59¾. Told broker to call when it reached 61 (the door to the new box). Broker took 2 hours to reach him, stock was already at 63. He bought at 65 — at the TOP of the new box. This led to discovering the "on-stop" buy order: an automatic order that executes when stock reaches a specified price. This solved the timing problem.

N035 [MUST KNOW] Three successful box trades: ALLEGHENY LUDLUM STEEL (bought at 45¾ going into 45/50 box, sold 3 weeks later at 51), DRESSER INDUSTRIES (bought at 84 entering 84/92 box, sold at 86½ — didn't progress right), COOPER-BESSEMER (bought at 40¾ bottom of 40/45 box, sold at 45½). Total profit: $2,442.36.

N036 [MUST KNOW] The NORTH AMERICAN AVIATION disaster — Darvas bought 500 shares at 94⅜ thinking it was about to establish a new box over 100. It immediately fell back. His pride wouldn't let him sell: "This stock cannot go down any further." He stubbornly held on. By week's end, all profit from his three successful box trades was gone. This taught him the critical realization:
1. There is no sure thing in the market — expect to be wrong half the time
2. Accept this and subdue your pride and ego
3. Become an impartial diagnostician, not identified with any theory or stock
4. Cannot merely take chances — must reduce risks as far as humanly possible

N037 [MUST KNOW] The stop-loss invention — Darvas's "quick-loss weapon." He would give "on-stop" buy orders at a set price AND simultaneously place an automatic stop-loss sell order below. If a stock went below where it "should" be, he'd be sold out automatically with a small loss. He'd "never sleep with a loss" — if stocks went below their proper price, he wouldn't own them at bedtime. He knew he'd be "stopped out for the sake of a point" many times, only to see the stock climb immediately after, but "this was not so important as stopping the big losses."

N038 [MUST KNOW] The trailing stop-loss and the Broadway analogy — Darvas couldn't train himself not to sell rising stocks too quickly out of fear, so he adopted a trailing stop-loss that moved up with the stock price, far enough away that "a meaningless swing would not touch it off" but close enough that a real reversal would trigger a sale. For WHEN to sell: "I would be a fool to sell a stock as long as it keeps advancing. When to sell then? When the boxes started to go into reverse! When the pyramids started to tumble downwards, that was the time to close the show and sell out." He compared it to a Broadway show: you don't close "My Fair Lady" after 200 performances just because you guessed that's when it would end — you close when seats are empty.

N039 [MUST KNOW] Darvas's complete system crystallized — four objectives and four weapons:
OBJECTIVES: 1. Right stocks, 2. Right timing, 3. Small losses, 4. Big profits
WEAPONS: 1. Price and volume, 2. Box theory, 3. Automatic buy-order, 4. Stop-loss sell-order
Basic strategy: "I would just jog along with an upward trend, trailing my stop-loss insurance behind me. As the trend continued, I would buy more. When the trend reversed? I would run like a thief."

N040 [MUST KNOW] The emotional discipline requirement — "I must not fall in love with them when they rose and I must not get angry when they fell... there are no such animals as good or bad stocks. There are only rising and falling stocks — and I should hold the rising ones and sell those that fall." He knew this required bringing "fear, hope and greed — under complete control."

## Chapter 5: Cables Round the World (pp. 95-125)

N041 [MUST KNOW] The cable system — Darvas signed up for a 2-year world dancing tour just as he developed his system. He and his broker agreed to communicate via cables (telegrams). Tools: (1) Barron's weekly magazine airmailed to him globally — arrived by Thursday, 4 days behind Wall Street; (2) Daily telegrams with closing prices + daily high/low of his stocks. The cables used a coded format: "B 32½ (34½-32⅜) L 57 (58⅝-57) U 89½ (91½-89) A 120¼ (121/2-120¼) F 132¼ (134⅞-132¼)"

N042 [MUST KNOW] The advantage of distance — initially Darvas was afraid of being far from Wall Street. But he gradually realized isolation was his greatest asset: "No phone calls, no confusion, no contradictory rumors — these factors combined gave me a much more detached view." He handled only 5-8 stocks at a time, separating them from "the confusing, jungle-like movement" of hundreds. The poker analogy: "I could not hear what people said, but I could see what they did. It was like a poker game in which I could not hear the betting, but I could see all the cards."

N043 [MUST KNOW] Paper trading doesn't work — Darvas tried practicing on paper first but found it "quite different from actual investing. It was like playing cards without any dollars in the pot. It had as much savor and excitement as bridge at an old ladies' home." With no money at risk, controlling emotions was easy. With $10,000 invested, "my emotions came floating quickly up to the surface."

N044 [MUST KNOW] The Dow Jones discovery — Darvas's stocks sometimes made inexplicable moves that didn't match their behavior. Searching Barron's for answers, he discovered these moves coincided with violent moves in the general market. He'd been watching only his own stocks, ignoring the overall market — "like trying to direct a battle by only looking at one section of the battlefield." He added the Dow-Jones Industrial Average closing price to his daily cables. But he learned NOT to apply the DJIA mechanically — it simply mirrors 30 stocks, not a fortune-telling tool. The relationship was "much more like an art" — he used it only to determine "whether I was in a strong or a weak market."

N045 [MUST KNOW] The cause-of-error tables — Darvas kept a journal for EVERY trade: wrote down reason for buying, reason for selling, and if a loss, the cause of error. Example errors from his table: BLAND CREEK COAL (bought too late), JOY MANUFACTURING (stop-loss too close), EASTERN GAS & FUEL (overlooked weak general market), ALCOA (bought on decline), COOPER-BESSEMER (wrong timing). This became "one of the most important of all my qualifications" — like learning to drive, you can't learn it just from books.

N046 [MUST KNOW] Stocks have "characters" like people — "Some of them are calm, slow, conservative. Others are jumpy, nervous, tense." Some were consistent and predictable ("like dependable friends"), others consistently hurt him. If a stock "slapped me twice I would refuse to touch them anymore."

N047 [MUST KNOW] The diagnostician mindset — "I was becoming a diagnostician but could not be a prophet." He could only say a stock was healthy "now, today, at this hour" — couldn't guarantee it wouldn't "catch a cold tomorrow." His educated guesses were often wrong, but this no longer upset him. Emotional detachment achieved: "I was no longer proud if the stock went up, nor did I feel wounded if it fell." Key insight: "The value of a stock is its quoted price. This in turn is entirely dependent on supply and demand... there is no such thing as a $50 stock. If a $50 stock went to $49 — it was now a $49 stock."

N048 [SHOULD KNOW] Don't hold for tax reasons — Darvas rejected the practice of holding stocks for 6 months to get long-term capital gains rates. "I might lose money by holding on to a falling stock just for tax reasons." He would "do the right thing first — follow what a stock's behavior commands and care about taxes later."

N049 [MUST KNOW] The COOPER BESSEMER persistence trade — Darvas bought this stock three times. First two trades ended in losses. The third (Jan-Apr 1957) was bought at 57, sold at 70¾ — a sizable profit of $2,575.55. Other successful stocks: DRESSER INDUSTRIES, REYNOLDS METALS.

N050 [MUST KNOW] The 1957 bear market — stop-losses saved him. In summer 1957 from Singapore, a "staggering series of events" unfolded. He got stopped out of BALTIMORE & OHIO, DOBECKMUN, DAYSTROM, FOSTER WHEELER, AEROQUIP — one by one, each stock sagged through the bottom of its box. By Aug 26, 1957, he held not a single stock. His stop-losses had "automatically released me well before the bad times came." He compared it to Hitler's invasion of Stalingrad — nobody recognized the turning point while it was happening. The stop-losses made recognizing the turning point "unnecessary."

N051 [MUST KNOW] The vindication table — Darvas later studied what happened to his 1957 stocks: every single one dropped dramatically. BALTIMORE & OHIO from 55 to 22⅝, DAYSTROM from 42¼ to 30, FOSTER WHEELER from 59½ to 25⅛, AEROQUIP from 27½ to 16⅞. Without stop-losses he would have lost ~50% and been "like a man in a cage, locked in with my holdings." The hold-and-hope "conservative investor" strategy was exposed as the real gambling.

N052 [SHOULD KNOW] End of technician phase — by Sept 1957, Darvas received his monthly statement and found he'd made up the money lost on JONES & LAUGHLIN. His original $37,000 capital was "almost intact." But after commissions and taxes from years of trading, he had a net loss of $889. He'd come out of "the greatest bull market in history with a lot of experience, a great amount of knowledge, much more confidence — and a net loss of $889."

## PART FOUR: THE TECHNO-FUNDAMENTALIST

## Chapter 6: During the Baby-Bear Market (pp. 127-139)

N053 [MUST KNOW] The summer camp / hospital analogy — Darvas compared bull and bear markets: "The bull market I saw as a sunny summer camp filled with powerful athletes. But I had to remember that some stocks were stronger than others. The bear market? The summer camp had changed to a hospital. The great majority of stocks were sick — but some were sicker than others." A stock that fell from 100 to 40 would not climb back for a long time — "like an athlete with a badly injured leg who would need a long period of recuperation."

N054 [MUST KNOW] The George Bernard Shaw analogy — Darvas accepted the market as it was, not as he wanted it to be. He quoted Shaw's story: at an opening night, after the curtain fell, everyone cheered except one man who booed. Shaw approached him: "Don't you like my play?" "No, I don't." Shaw replied: "Neither do I, but what can the two of us do against all that crowd?" Darvas firmly refused to trade during the bear market, telling his broker "This is a market for the birds."

N055 [MUST KNOW] Finding stocks that resist the decline — During the bear market, Darvas spent weeks watching from the sidelines as a "runner limbering up for the race." He studied Barron's weekly, looking for stocks that resisted the downtrend: "I reasoned that if they could swim against the stream, they were the ones that would advance most rapidly when the current changed." On closer examination, these resistant stocks were companies with sharply upward earning trends. Key insight: "Capital was flowing into these stocks, even in a bad market."

N056 [MUST KNOW] The TECHNO-FUNDAMENTALIST theory born — Darvas married his technical approach to fundamentals: "I would select stocks on their technical action in the market, but I would only buy them when I could give improving earning power as my fundamental reason for doing so." This is the method he used for the rest of his career.

N057 [MUST KNOW] The "20-year view" stock selection — Darvas took a forward-looking approach: he searched for stocks "tied up with the future" where "revolutionary new products would sharply improve the company's earnings." He identified infant industries of the late 1950s: electronics, missiles, rocket fuels. Historical parallel: before automobiles, smart operators went into railroads because they'd supersede covered wagons. Later, shrewd investors moved into GM and CHRYSLER.

N058 [MUST KNOW] The fashion analogy for stocks — "There are definite fashions in stocks just as there are in women's clothes." Forward-looking investors ride the fashion while it persists, then exit when it fades. He had to watch eagerly for fashion changes, or risk "still holding a long-skirt stock when the new stocks were showing their knees."

N059 [MUST KNOW] Higher-priced stocks are more efficient — Darvas calculated that with $10,000 to invest, commissions on a $10 stock (1,000 shares) were $300 round-trip, while commissions on a $100 stock (100 shares) were only $90. Mistakes were much less costly with higher-priced stocks.

N060 [MUST KNOW] The Rothschild analogy — Darvas recalled how Rothschild had an agent at Waterloo who, as soon as victory was certain, rushed to London so Rothschild could buy British government shares before anyone else heard the news. "Communications are much quicker but the ancient art remains the same — to get in faster than the other fellow."

N061 [MUST KNOW] Darvas's five-year education summarized: "My Canadian period taught me not to gamble; my fundamentalist period taught me about industry groups and their earning trends; my technical period taught me how to interpret price-action and the technical position of stocks — and now I reinforced myself by piecing them all together."

N062 [MUST KNOW] The five future leaders identified — In Nov 1957, during the depths of the baby-bear market, these stocks were sleeping: UNIVERSAL PRODUCTS at 20, THIOKOL CHEMICAL at 64, TEXAS INSTRUMENTS at 23, ZENITH RADIO at 116, FAIRCHILD CAMERA at 19. "These stocks were not dead. They were only sleeping the promising sleep of the unborn... They were going to make me $2,000,000."

## Chapter 7: The Theory Starts to Work (pp. 141-163)

N063 [MUST KNOW] LORILLARD — Darvas's first techno-fundamentalist trade. Performing at the "Arc En Ciel" in Saigon in Nov 1957, he noticed LORILLARD in Barron's — rising from 17 to establish itself in a 24/27 box while the rest of the market sank. Volume was 126,700 shares vs its usual 10,000. Technical signal: strength in a bear market + unusual volume. Fundamental reason: manufacturer of "Kent" and "Old Gold" filter-tip cigarettes during the filter-tip craze sweeping America.

N064 [MUST KNOW] LORILLARD execution — Cabled from Bangkok: "BUY 200 LORILLARD 27½ ON STOP WITH 26 STOPLOSS." Bought at 27½. Stopped out at 26 on Nov 26th — but the stock bounced right back, so he re-entered at 28¾ with stop at 26. This time it worked perfectly. Added 400 shares at 35 and 36½ while flying Bangkok to Japan. Stock pyramided boxes beautifully: 27/32, 31/35, then pushed to 44⅜ by Feb 17, 1958. A cancer scare about filter-tips dropped it from 44 to 36¾ in one day — but his stop-loss at 36 wasn't touched. He added 400 more at 38⅝. By late March, volume hit 316,600 as it entered the 50/54 box. An advisory service was urging clients to SHORT Lorillard — they were completely wrong.

N065 [MUST KNOW] LORILLARD final position: 200 at 28¼ ($5,808.76) + 200 at 35 ($7,065.00) + 200 at 36½ ($7,366.50) + 400 at 38⅝ ($15,587.24) = 1,000 shares for $35,827.50. Last three purchases on 50% margin, preserving capital for DINERS' CLUB.

N066 [MUST KNOW] DINERS' CLUB — near-monopoly in the expanding credit-card field (a pioneer). Just split 2-for-1, volume swelled to 23,400 (unusually high). Bought 500 at 24½ on stop with 21⅝ stop-loss. Added 500 at 26⅛. Total: 1,000 shares for $25,520.80. Pattern evolved: 28/30, then 32/36 box with 52,600 weekly volume. Trailed stop to 27, then 31. Stock hesitated near 36 — Darvas raised stop to unusually narrow 36⅜. In fourth week of April, DINERS' CLUB broke through its box floor. Sold at $35,848.85. Profit: $10,328.05.

N067 [MUST KNOW] Six weeks later, American Express announced plans to launch a rival to DINERS' CLUB — this explained why insiders had been selling near 36. "Some people had known this before the announcement and were selling out. Without knowing about it, I was their partner." The technical side had warned Darvas to exit before the news broke.

N068 [MUST KNOW] E.L. BRUCE — the spectacular trade. Small Memphis firm on the American Stock Exchange making hardwood flooring. Didn't fit Darvas's fundamental requirements at all, but the technical pattern was "so compelling that I could not take my eyes off it." Usually traded <5,000 shares/week. In April 1958, volume exploded to 19,100, then 41,500, 54,200, 76,500 — price jumping 5-8 points weekly without downward reaction. Went from 18 to 50 in weeks.

N069 [MUST KNOW] Darvas decided: "Fundamentals or no fundamentals, if it went over 50, I would buy it, and I would buy a lot of it." After selling LORILLARD at 57⅜ ($56,880.45, profit $21,052.95), he had nearly doubled his capital. Opened accounts with TWO additional brokerage firms to avoid detection. Bought 2,500 shares of BRUCE at 50¾ to 53⅝ — total investment: $130,687.55 on margin.

N070 [MUST KNOW] BRUCE soared past 60, then 77 by June 13. Then his broker called from New York to Calcutta: "They have suspended trading in BRUCE on the American Stock Exchange." Terror — over $60,000 invested. But the news was GOOD: a takeover battle. Edward Gilbert was trying to oust the Bruce family, buying up the 314,600 shares outstanding. Short sellers who'd bet it would fall to $30 based on book value were caught in a massive squeeze — 275,000 shares traded in 10 weeks.

N071 [MUST KNOW] The $100 decision — broker asked if Darvas wanted to sell at the OTC price of $100. He made "one of the most momentous decisions of my life": "No, I will not sell at 100. I have no reason to sell an advancing stock. I will hold onto it." He gradually sold blocks of 100-200 shares OTC over the next weeks at an average price of 171. Total profit on E.L. BRUCE: $295,305.45. "This was my first really big killing in the market."

N072 [SHOULD KNOW] Everyone in Calcutta asked: "Who gave you the tip?" Nobody believed he'd done it himself through price/volume analysis. They assumed Edward Gilbert had tipped him off.

## Chapter 8: My First Half-Million (pp. 165-179)

N073 [MUST KNOW] Post-BRUCE discipline — after making $325,000 in nine months, Darvas became MORE cautious, not less. "So many operators have made big money in nine months and lost it in nine weeks." First step: withdrew half his profits from the market. Watched warily for new opportunities.

N074 [SHOULD KNOW] Small losses after BRUCE: MOLYBDENUM (bought 27, stopped out 26½, loss $482.47), HAVEG INDUSTRIES (bought 31⅜, sold 30½, loss $804.01). These small losses proved the system was working — protecting capital.

N075 [MUST KNOW] LORILLARD re-entry mistake — Darvas went back to LORILLARD out of "sentimental attachment" — it had been his first big winner. "It became my American pet." He bought it three more times, lost each time: 1,000 at 70.5 (loss $3,590.76), 500 at 69⅛ (loss $1,169.63), 1,000 at 67¾ (loss $1,712.14). Total LORILLARD losses: $6,472.53. The stock had become "a rather weary, slow-moving elderly gentleman" — no longer a Darvas stock. After the third loss he finally broke the sentimental attachment.

N076 [MUST KNOW] The half-million P&L summary:
PROFITS: LORILLARD $23,052.95, DINERS' CLUB $10,328.05, E.L. BRUCE $295,305.45
LOSSES: LORILLARD $6,472.53, MOLYBDENUM $482.47, HAVEG INDUSTRIES $804.01
Net: $326,686.45 profit → $318,927.44 overall profit. Total losses only $7,759.01.

N077 [MUST KNOW] UNIVERSAL PRODUCTS (later UNIVERSAL CONTROLS) — electronics company spotted in Barron's while the general market was strengthening. Quoted around 35, bouncing between 35⅞ and 33½. Pilot buy: 300 shares at 35¼ ($10,644.93) with stop-loss at 32½. Stock firmed up in Kashmir — added 1,200 at 36½ ($44,083.56) with stop at 33. Then 1,500 at 40 ($60,585.00) from Karachi. Total: 3,000 shares for $115,313.49 (pre-split) = 6,000 shares after 2-for-1 split.

N078 [MUST KNOW] The secretary's father anecdote — Darvas told his secretary to buy UNIVERSAL CONTROLS at 31¾ with a stop at 30. The secretary's father, "an old-fashioned, pure fundamentalist," insisted on examining the company books first. While he examined them, the stock went to 50. Lesson: price action doesn't wait for fundamental analysis.

N079 [MUST KNOW] THIOKOL CHEMICAL — rocket fuel maker. First noticed in Feb 1958 in Tokyo after a 2-for-1 split. Settled into a 39/47 box for months. Darvas watched it in Barron's, saw "a calm that precedes the storm." Started daily quotes when it approached 45. In Aug 1958, it flexed toward the upper frame — pilot buy: 200 shares at 47¼ ($9,535.26). Three weeks later, added 1,300 at 49⅞ on stop ($65,408.72). Total: 1,500 shares for $74,943.98. Stock rapidly rose into the 52-56 range.

N080 [MUST KNOW] The THIOKOL stock-rights play — THIOKOL issued stock-rights (one right per share; 12 rights = 1 share at $42). With stock at 55 and purchase price at 42, plus a "special subscription account" allowing 75% leverage with no commission, this was irresistible. Darvas sold his 1,500 shares at 53½ ($57,000), used the proceeds to buy 36,000 rights ($49,410), converted them into 3,000 shares at $42 ($126,000) — but only needed $6,000 cash due to the 75% loan. Then he did it AGAIN: sold another batch, bought 36,000 more rights, converted to another 3,000 shares. Total: 6,000 THIOKOL shares for $350,820.

N081 [MUST KNOW] THIOKOL shifted from American to New York Stock Exchange in December 1958 — immediately jumped 8 points, touching 100 the next week. Cable from broker: "YOUR THIOKOL PROFITS NOW $250,000." Darvas was at the Georges V Hotel in Paris.

N082 [MUST KNOW] The "Remember BRUCE" card — with over $500,000 in combined profits (BRUCE + THIOKOL), Darvas felt an overwhelming urge to sell. "Every fiber in my being seemed to be saying, 'Sell, Sell.' It was the biggest temptation in the world." He sat at his dressing table and wrote on a card: "Remember BRUCE!" He carried it around Paris, pulling it out every time he felt like selling. It reminded him that he'd held BRUCE from 50 to 171 by NOT selling an advancing stock. He decided not to sell THIOKOL either. The stock continued to rise.

N083 [MUST KNOW] January 1959 return to New York — Darvas held 6,000 THIOKOL and 6,000 UNIVERSAL CONTROLS. Both performing well. THIOKOL at 100, UNIVERSAL CONTROLS at 45. Brokers told him his investments were "well over half a million dollars."

## Chapter 9: My Second Crisis (pp. 181-199)

N084 [MUST KNOW] The Wall Street disaster — Darvas's most important lesson. Arriving in New York with over $500K, he became overconfident: "I slowly started to imagine I was a Napoleon of finance." He decided to trade from Wall Street directly, visiting a broker's boardroom daily. The margin requirement had been raised to 90%, but he planned to use his $160,000 BRUCE profits for "serious day-to-day dealings."

N085 [MUST KNOW] The boardroom effect — "In a few days of trading, I threw overboard everything I had learned over the past six years." The boardroom was filled with excitement, noise, opinions, gossip. He started talking to brokers, listening to rumors, watching the ticker. "Instead of being a lone wolf, I became a confused, excited lamb milling around with others, waiting to be clipped." He abandoned stop-losses, ignored boxes, put in contradictory orders. The classic vicious circle: BOUGHT AT THE TOP → stock dropped → became frightened → SOLD AT THE BOTTOM → stock started to rise → became greedy → AND BOUGHT AT THE TOP again.

N086 [MUST KNOW] The $100,000 loss — a "period of complete disaster." Detailed trades showing the carnage: HAVEG INDUSTRIES (loss $18,258.66), ROME CABLE (loss $6,650.52), GENERAL TIME (loss $3,744.48), ADDRESSOGRAPH-MULTIGRAPH (loss $4,453.35), REICHHOLD CHEMICALS (loss $2,795.13), BRUNSWICK-BALKE-COLLENDER (loss $5,447.54), RAYTHEON (loss $7,077.31), NATIONAL RESEARCH (loss $6,123.48), AMERICAN METALS-CLIMAX (loss $7,487.13), AMERICAN MOTORS (loss $5,844.30), MOLYBDENUM (loss $5,522.50), SHARON STEEL (loss $11,485.33), WARNER LAMBERT (loss $3,861.41), LUKENS STEEL (loss $7,837.52). TOTAL LOSS: $96,588.66.

N087 [MUST KNOW] The diagnosis — "My ears were my enemy." When abroad, he operated "calmly, neutrally, without interruption or rumor, completely without emotion and ego" via daily telegrams. In New York, "there were interruptions, rumors, panics, contradictory information, all floating into my ear. As a result of this my emotions became involved with the stocks — and the cold, clinical approach had gone."

N088 [MUST KNOW] The recovery in Paris — Darvas sold everything except UNIVERSAL CONTROLS and THIOKOL (which he'd left alone during the crisis — the only thing that saved him from total ruin). Fled to Paris. For two weeks his telegrams were meaningless — "like a drunk who loses touch with reality." Then, at the Hotel Georges V, the figures slowly began to clear. His "feeling" returned. He made permanent rules:
1. Never visit a brokerage office again
2. Brokers must never telephone or give unsolicited information
3. Communication only via daily telegram
4. Only quote stocks HE asks for — never suggest new ones
5. Even in a New York hotel, Wall Street must be "thousands of miles away"

N089 [MUST KNOW] The root cause diagnosis — "It was caused by egotism leading to vanity leading to over-confidence, which in turn led to disaster. It was not the market that beat me. It was my own unreasoning instincts and uncontrolled emotions." The "fire in a crowded theater" analogy: when everyone panics, even someone who knows better gets caught in the stampede. In the boardroom, "instead of being a lone wolf, I became a confused, excited lamb."

## Chapter 10: Two Million Dollars (pp. 201-237)

N090 [MUST KNOW] Return to New York, Feb 1959 — Darvas "erected an iron fence" around himself. Spread deals across SIX brokers so no single one could track his full operation. Telegrams received at 6 PM (he woke up late — nightclub performer). Phone operator instructed to block ALL calls during the day. At 7 PM he studied his telegrams + afternoon newspaper closing prices (tearing out the price pages and throwing away the financial commentary). "I settle down to work while Wall Street sleeps."

N091 [MUST KNOW] UNIVERSAL CONTROLS exit — stock lost its steady upward progress, became "wild, too wild." Advanced from 66 to 102 in three weeks in early March 1959 — then began reversing. Darvas tightened stop-loss to within 2 points of the closing price. Sold next morning between $86 and $89 — 12 points from the high. Selling price: $524,669.97. Profit: $409,356.48.

N092 [MUST KNOW] TEXAS INSTRUMENTS — after selling UNIVERSAL CONTROLS, Darvas needed a high-priced, actively traded stock where his large capital ($500K+) wouldn't unduly influence the market. Found TEXAS INSTRUMENTS. Bought 2,000 at 94⅜ ($189,718.80), added 1,500 at 97⅞ ($147,544.35), then 2,000 at 101⅞ ($204,733.80). Total: 5,500 shares for $541,996.95.

N093 [MUST KNOW] THIOKOL's climax — after a 3-for-1 split, the stock shot to 72 in the first week of May on incredible volume: 549,400 shares, 13¼ point advance, $40M aggregate trading value. Then the NYSE governors suspended all stop orders on THIOKOL. Without stop-losses, Darvas was stripped of his "most powerful tool" — he immediately sold at average price of 68. Original 6,000 shares × 3 (split) = 18,000 shares. Total received: $1,212,851.52. Profit: $862,031.52.

N094 [MUST KNOW] The four-stock selection method — with $1M+ to reinvest, Darvas chose from four candidates (ZENITH RADIO, LITTON INDUSTRIES, FAIRCHILD CAMERA, BECKMAN INSTRUMENTS). Method: pilot buy 500 shares of each with 10% stop-losses — deliberately "vague and clumsy" but designed to let the market eliminate the weakest two. BECKMAN stopped out May 18, LITTON sold May 19. Darvas poured $1M+ into the remaining two winners.

N095 [MUST KNOW] Final positions — ZENITH RADIO: 5,500 shares for $574,842.25 (500 at 104 + 1,500 at 99¼ + 1,000 at 104 + 1,000 at 105¼ + 1,500 at 107½). FAIRCHILD CAMERA: 4,500 shares for $567,820.60 (500 at 128 + 1,000 at 123¼ + 1,000 at 125 + 1,000 at 126¼ + 1,000 at 127). Total reinvested at 90% margin: $1,684,660.

N096 [MUST KNOW] The flow of funds (March-May 1959): Sold UNIVERSAL CONTROLS for $524,670 → Bought TEXAS INSTRUMENTS for $541,997. Sold THIOKOL for $1,212,850 → Bought ZENITH RADIO for $574,842 + FAIRCHILD CAMERA for $567,821. Total received: $1,737,520. Margin debt: $274,600 → $1,462,920.

N097 [MUST KNOW] The $100,000 day — June 9th telegram: "Z 122½ (124-116¾) T 119¼ (121½-117¼) F 125 (126-121)." Next day: 126½. His holdings had appreciated $100,000 in one day.

N098 [MUST KNOW] The $2,250,000 moment — Before leaving for Monte Carlo in early July, Darvas met with his brokers. If he sold everything, he could realize over $2,250,000. His reaction: "I was happy, but not excited. I had been much more excited when I made my first $10,000 out of DINERS' CLUB." He felt "like a runner who has trained strenuously and has suffered many defeats, and now trots to victory."

N099 [MUST KNOW] The final answer — should he sell? "It was the old tried and trusted answer: I did not have any reason to sell a rising stock. I would just continue to jog along with the trend, trailing my stop-loss behind me. As the trend increased, I would buy more. If the trend reversed? I would, as ever, flee like a disturbed burglar." He put stop-losses on all stocks before flying to Europe so his $2M+ would be protected.

N100 [MUST KNOW] Burns' "Darvas Principles in a Nutshell":
1. Go long only in a market that is in an uptrend (above 50-day SMA)
2. Only trade "Monster stocks" — world-changing companies with massive earnings growth
3. Enter at high probability moments (breakout from price box to new all-time highs)
4. Let the winner run — no price targets, only trailing stops
5. If you are wrong, get out quickly (1% of capital risk per trade)
6. Do not waste emotional/mental capital watching every tick
7. Always do your homework — maintain a watch list even in bear markets
8. Pyramiding: add to winners early, never add to losers
9. Burns' key entry points: breakout to new ATH, bounce off bottom of Darvas box, bounce off 50-day MA, break above 50-day MA, bounce off 200-day MA, break above 200-day MA coming out of a bear market

## Time Magazine Interview (pp. 239-241)

N101 [SHOULD KNOW] Time Magazine profiled Darvas in 1959, verifying his trading records through brokerage statements. The article confirmed his $2M+ gains and brought widespread attention to his method. This led to the book being published and becoming a bestseller. The Time profile also attracted SEC scrutiny and skepticism from Wall Street professionals who couldn't believe a dancer could outperform them.

## Appendix (Burns)

N102 [NICE TO KNOW] Burns recommends modern tools for Darvas-style trading: Investor's Business Daily (IBD 50 list for stock selection), candlestick charts (visual representation of Darvas boxes), 50-day and 200-day simple moving averages as key support/resistance levels, and discount brokers for low-commission execution. Burns also recommends the Darvas Trader newsletter as a modern implementation of the method.
