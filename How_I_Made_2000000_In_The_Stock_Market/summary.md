# How I Made $2,000,000 in the Stock Market — Summary
## Nicolas Darvas (with commentary by Steve Burns)

### Overview
Nicolas Darvas, a Hungarian-born professional ballroom dancer, turned $36,000 into over $2,000,000 in the stock market between 1952 and 1959 — mostly while touring the world performing in nightclubs. This book chronicles his evolution through four distinct trading phases, each built on the painful lessons of the previous one, culminating in his "techno-fundamentalist" system that combined price/volume analysis with earnings-driven stock selection.

### The Four Phases

**Phase 1: The Gambler (1952-1953)** — Darvas stumbled into stocks when paid in mining shares for a nightclub appearance. His first trade (BRILUND) was pure luck — $8,000 profit. He then followed tips from nightclub patrons, advisory services, and brokers, buying 25-30 penny stocks at a time. Lost nearly everything except the BRILUND windfall. Key lesson: tips don't work.

**Phase 2: The Fundamentalist (1954-1955)** — Moved to Wall Street, studied balance sheets, P/E ratios, and industry analysis. Made small profits during the bull market but churned his account constantly (calling his broker 20 times/day). The JONES & LAUGHLIN disaster — went all-in on a fundamentally "perfect" steel stock that immediately dropped, losing $9,000 and nearly bankrupting him. Key lesson: fundamentals don't guarantee price performance.

**Phase 3: The Technician (1955-1957)** — After TEXAS GULF PRODUCING saved him (bought purely on rising price action), Darvas developed the Box Theory: stocks move in defined price ranges (boxes) that stack like pyramids. Buy when a stock enters a higher box, sell if it falls below the floor. He added automatic buy-on-stop orders and trailing stop-losses. Traded via telegrams while touring the world — discovering that distance from Wall Street was his greatest advantage. Survived the 1957 bear market with stop-losses intact, but after commissions ended the bull market with a net loss of $889. Key lesson: price action works, but needs a fundamental filter.

**Phase 4: The Techno-Fundamentalist (1957-1959)** — The breakthrough. Darvas married technicals to fundamentals: select stocks technically (rising price, increasing volume, forming boxes), but only buy when the company belongs to an infant industry with rapidly improving earnings. Focus on "stocks of the future" — electronics, missiles, credit cards. This produced his greatest trades: LORILLARD ($21K profit), DINERS' CLUB ($10K), E.L. BRUCE ($295K), THIOKOL ($862K), UNIVERSAL CONTROLS ($409K), TEXAS INSTRUMENTS, ZENITH RADIO, and FAIRCHILD CAMERA.

### The Darvas Box System
1. **Stock Selection**: Find stocks in rapidly expanding industries with improving earnings that show unusual price strength and volume
2. **Box Identification**: Define the trading range (box) — the high and low points between which a stock oscillates
3. **Entry**: Buy when the stock breaks above the ceiling of its current box into a new, higher box (on-stop buy order)
4. **Protection**: Simultaneously place a stop-loss just below the floor of the box
5. **Trailing**: As the stock rises into higher boxes, raise the stop-loss to just below each new box floor
6. **Exit**: Sell only when the stock breaks below its box floor (automatically via stop-loss) — never sell a rising stock
7. **Pyramiding**: Add to winning positions as they confirm the trend; never add to losers

### Key Trading Principles
- **Isolation is an advantage**: Distance from Wall Street, brokers, tips, and rumors enables clear thinking
- **Small losses, big wins**: Expect to be wrong 50% of the time; keep losses tiny and let winners run
- **No emotional attachment**: Stocks are not pets — sell when they stop performing
- **The market is always right**: Price is truth; your opinion is irrelevant
- **Don't fight the trend**: Go long only in bull markets; sit in cash during bear markets
- **Higher-priced stocks are more efficient**: Lower commission costs relative to investment

### The Second Crisis
Darvas's most powerful lesson: after making $500K+, he returned to New York, started trading from a brokerage boardroom, and lost $100,000 in weeks. He abandoned every principle he'd learned — listened to rumors, ignored stop-losses, traded on emotion. The cure: flee back to his telegram-based system and never visit a brokerage office again. "My ears were my enemy."

### Final Result
Starting capital: $36,000. Final portfolio value: $2,250,000+. Time period: approximately 18 months of active profitable trading (within a 6-year learning period). The journey from complete novice to millionaire was built on systematic learning from failure, emotional discipline, and the courage to let winners run while cutting losses ruthlessly.
