If You Only Remember 10 Things

  1. Variant perception — the only edge worth having is a well-founded view that is meaningfully different from market consensus.
  2. Understanding what the market expects is at least as important as understanding the fundamentals themselves.
  3. Euphoria is a reliable sign of a market top; fear is a reliable sign of a market bottom. Even experienced investors fall prey to both extremes, every single cycle.
  4. "The quick and the dead" — responding to change faster than anyone else is what separates great traders from the merely competent.
  5. Instinct is not a gift you are born with. It is the unconscious product of making more judgments, on the same kinds of data, over more years, than almost anyone else.
  6. Put your own money alongside your clients' money, in every investment, every day. If they lose, you lose.
  7. Leverage amplifies both brilliance and stupidity in equal measure, and when the trade is crowded, the exit will be small.
  8. One dollar invested with Steinhardt in 1967 was worth $481 when he closed in 1995. The same dollar in the S&P 500 was worth $19.
  9. Performance must be absolute, not relative. Being down less than the market is still being down — and that is unacceptable.
  10. A career making rich people richer can fund a purpose larger than itself. Steinhardt's greatest legacy is birthright israel, not his track record.

Chapter 1: The Phone Call

The book opens in October 1981, with Michael Steinhardt and his wife Judy on vacation at La Reserve de Beaulieu on the Côte d'Azur. He had been fantasizing about scampi Provençale and loupe de mer with fennel for days. The phone rang twice that afternoon, and both calls would define his life. The first was from his partner John Levin, who skipped the hello entirely: "Congratulations! The bonds have made a huge move. We're up 60 percent for the year." The fiscal year had just started.

The bond trade that triggered this windfall was one of the most audacious bets of Steinhardt's career. In the late spring of 1981, with long-term Treasury rates approaching 16 percent and short-term rates at 17 percent, the universal consensus was bearish on bonds. Henry Kaufman, Salomon Brothers' chief economist and known as "Dr. Doom," predicted runaway inflation. Bonds were called "certificates of confiscation." Steinhardt invested $50 million of his two hedge funds' cash and borrowed another $200 million to buy $250 million of intermediate ten-year Treasury bonds — unprecedented leverage for a man whose entire career had been in equities.

Investors revolted. McKinsey & Company redeemed immediately after hearing his monthly letter. "You're an equity investor," their committee members repeated. "We have you allocated as an equity manager. You cannot buy bonds." A Canadian investor threatened to sue. At one point, the fund suffered $10 million in paper losses. Steinhardt constructed his own indicators, including a personal "NYC taxi index" — tracking the ratio of available cabs to occupied ones as a proxy for economic slowdown. Each Friday he waited anxiously for the M1 and M2 money supply numbers.

When rates finally cracked in October 1981, the $250 million position made $40 million in profit. The fund finished the year up 97 percent. It was, Steinhardt writes, one of the most gratifying moments of his financial life. He had bet against the consensus and been lavishly rewarded.

The mechanics of the bond trade were as audacious as the thesis. Steinhardt managed about $75 million across two funds — the flagship Steinhardt Partners LP and an offshore fund, SP International. Government bonds could be purchased with 95 to 99 percent borrowed money, compared with stocks where Fed margin requirements constrained leverage. He was a neophyte bond investor exploiting a structural advantage that equities didn't offer. Each week, he anxiously awaited Friday's money supply numbers, hoping M1 and M2 would stop growing — signaling the economic slowdown that would vindicate his position.

Minutes after learning about the bond windfall, an FBI agent called. An armored-car robbery in Nanuet, New York, had led to a raid on a Black Panthers apartment in Newark, where agents found an assassination list of "successful capitalists." Steinhardt's name was on it. "We do not want to frighten you," Agent Smith said, "but we felt it was our obligation to tell everyone whose name was on the list."

"The confluence of those two phone conversations struck me as more than a coincidence. The first call validated my life as a hedge fund manager. The second call made me realize how fragile life can be." — Michael Steinhardt

Steinhardt compares himself to Goethe's Faust — always striving toward new goals rather than enjoying past achievements. The addiction, he writes, was in the process of pursuing success, not in savoring it. Joy from winning was "often fleeting and constrained." This restless dissatisfaction would drive both his greatest triumphs and his deepest depressions throughout his career.

The chapter closes with Steinhardt's late-life pivot. He does not believe in God, yet creating a renaissance within the next generation of Jews now dominates his conscious intensity as much as the markets once did. The true worth of his financial success, he argues, lies in its capacity to fund a philanthropic mission he considers "far more significant than making rich people richer."

Chapter 2: Bensonhurst

Sol Frank "Red" Steinhardt, also known as Red McGee, grew up in 1920s Brownsville — one of Brooklyn's toughest neighborhoods. He dropped out of school at twelve, worked manual labor, and became a chronic gambler: horses, sports, crap games, and clobyosh, a card game favored by Eastern European immigrants. Sol married Claire Dolleck in 1939; Michael was born in 1940; they divorced before Michael's first birthday because Sol could rarely bring home money for groceries.

Michael grew up at 2169 Seventy-second Street in Bensonhurst, a lower-middle-class Brooklyn neighborhood of Jews and Italians. His mother worked as a bookkeeper for Uncle Louie's wholesale fruit business on Manhattan's Lower West Side. Sol's $50 monthly child support arrived unpredictably. Michael's maternal grandparents shared the apartment. His grandfather died when Michael was four; his grandmother Rae, luminously beautiful with pure white hair, died a month after his Bar Mitzvah — she had walked in leaning on Claire's arm just as he was about to read his Haftorah portion, and the whole congregation stopped to look.

Michael was intellectually precocious — entered first grade at four and a half, consistently the youngest and smallest in his class. He won spelling bees and was considered brilliant by teachers. But he was socially awkward, bred both insecure and intensely competitive by being the smallest kid surrounded by bigger Brooklyn boys. He became an avid New York Giants fan — "pretty unusual for a kid from Brooklyn, where almost everyone was a Dodgers fan. Perhaps this was the first example of my future propensity to go against conventional wisdom."

Hebrew school at B'nai Isaac, an Orthodox synagogue, was an "abject failure." The synagogue impressed young Michael only as "the Land of Smelling Salts" — elderly men passing them around on Yom Kippur to revive themselves from fasting. He played hooky whenever he could. Yet a small shtiebl in the basement of a Bensonhurst tenement, where old men in long prayer shawls rocked back and forth, had a warmth that called to him. And Holocaust survivors with tattoos on their arms, living in his own neighborhood, destroyed his faith in a just God. Where was God in the age of Auschwitz?

The creation of the State of Israel became his one miracle. He pored through the weekly Jerusalem Post, worried about the Sea of Galilee going dry, fantasized about holding off Arab hordes to protect a settlement. The film Hill 24 Doesn't Answer left lasting images. But Israel alone could not bridge the gap between theology and empirical evidence. By high school, he was a self-avowed atheist — yet one who loved the sound of Yiddish, was stirred by Klezmer music, and felt a love for Jews that "transcends logic itself."

This seeming inconsistency — fierce atheism paired with intense Jewish commitment — would perplex people for the rest of his life. Rabbis he worked with mocked his atheism gently, insisting he was a believer. Steinhardt disagrees. His commitment does not require a supernatural being. "The heroism is all human. The suffering is all human."

The Jewish attribute he cherishes most is peoplehood. "My love for Jews transcends logic itself. I love being helpful to all kinds of Jews, not because I find them blessed, or superior to other people, but because I feel a distinct emotional attachment to them."

Years later, reading about a murder in Bensonhurst, Steinhardt learned the victim — Ralph Ronga — was the same jock who had slammed into him at third base during a junior high softball game. In that game, thirteen-year-old Michael stood his ground, caught the relay throw, tagged the runner, and got clobbered. It was "just about my only memorable athletic feat in a rarely distinguished career."

Chapter 3: The Wise Guy of Forty-Seventh Street

Sol became a fixture on Manhattan's Diamond District as a cash buyer of jewelry, known as "the jeweler to the mob." He knew Meyer Lansky and remained close to Lansky's partner Jimmy "Blue Eyes" Aiello until death. They dined at Patsy's, the Italian restaurant frequented by Frank Sinatra. When Joey Anastasia was shot in the Park Sheraton Hotel barbershop, police picked up Sol for questioning — they had been gambling together the night before.

Sol's code of ethics distinguished between "honest" and "honorable." Honest people paid taxes but cut every corner. Honorable people lived up to personal commitments, no matter how difficult. Loyalty to friends under stress was the sine qua non. He once held two Orthodox jewelers at gunpoint for switching a diamond, then saw their concentration camp tattoos and threw them out in disgust.

The Shlomo Moussaieff story reveals Sol at his best. He met Moussaieff, a struggling Israeli jeweler in London, and left him a piece from King Farouk's royal collection to sell on commission. Moussaieff couldn't sell it. Sol took it back, sold it in the US, then returned to London and handed Moussaieff $25,000 — his share. Moussaieff became one of the world's great jewel dealers. Sol also deposited cash with Moussaieff as a gambling brake — calling at midnight demanding it back, only to thank Shlomo later for refusing.

Sol's Bar Mitzvah gift to Michael in 1953 changed everything: 100 shares of Penn Dixie Cement at $37 and 100 shares of Columbia Gas System at $16 — worth over $5,000. Bar Mitzvah gifts typically ranged from $5 to $20. The fluctuating value captivated the thirteen-year-old: a $1 move in Penn Dixie equaled $100, against his $4 weekly allowance. He could go to sleep with certificates in his drawer and wake up richer or poorer.

Michael began skipping stickball to monitor stocks at the Bache & Company brokerage office in downtown Brooklyn. The stench of cigars, men gesticulating on phones, the noise of the ticker tape — "this was no country club; it was the rough-and-tumble of Wall Street." He graduated to Merrill Lynch's educational office in Grand Central Station and opened his first brokerage account. He fell for bucket shop scams like SwanFinch Oil, losing thousands, and dragged his mother into the losses too.

"I have no doubt that I inherited this love of the rush from my father. Psychologically, emotionally, and perhaps even intellectually, gambling and the manner in which I then invested may not have been importantly different."— Michael Steinhardt

But there was a crucial distinction: the stock market, unlike games of chance, is not random. Developing expertise results in better performance. Sol's gift of those two stocks "played as significant a role in the shaping of my life as anything else that happened to me before or after."

Sol took teenage Michael to the Copacabana nightclub, where Sol was treated like royalty — bouncers greeted him as "Red" or "Red McGee" or "Reuta" in Yiddish. He always ordered Dom Perignon because he believed it to be the best champagne, and everyone knew it was the most expensive on the menu. Jewish comedians like Joey Lewis acknowledged his presence during their acts. The whole scene was "right out of the movie Goodfellas." For teenage Michael, it was glamorous and awe-inspiring — his father was a big shot there, and he felt pride.

But Sol's dark side emerged unpredictably. At a Giants game when Michael was eleven, Don Mueller hit a go-ahead homer — Michael leaped up cheering. Sol, who had bet on Cincinnati, suddenly stood up and punched him in the shoulder. Not playfully — hard, menacingly. "How can you be so stupid? Do you think Don Mueller is going to pay your bills? Do you think the Giants are going to pay for your college education?" Michael started to cry, which only made Sol angrier. "My relationship with my father was never simple."

Chapter 4: The Ivy League, the Army, and the Street

Sol redirected Michael from free City College to Wharton over dinner at the Sacred Cow Steakhouse. He knew about the school from reading wedding announcements in the Sunday New York Times Society Section — wealthy Jewish boys graduated from there. Michael applied last-minute and was accepted. This was the pivotal intervention: without Wharton, no Ivy League credential, no Loeb Rhoades, no career.

The most valuable class was Philosophy of Statistics taught by Dr. Francis Brown — learning to frame problems quantitatively to arrive at unexpected probability distributions. Combined with his sociology major, this gave Steinhardt a unique analytical framework that served him for decades. He condensed Wharton to three years because he wasn't confident Sol would pay all the way through.

During his time at Penn, Sol was arrested by DA Frank Hogan as "the biggest jewel fence in America." The six-week trial was a circus. ADA Burton Roberts pointed at someone in the courtroom and said, "He's a friend of the defendant and he's a killer!" — then grabbed his crotch and added, "And I don't mean this kind of killer either." Sol was convicted on two felony counts, served two years at Sing Sing and Dannemora, and was eventually released on appeal due to prosecutorial misconduct. Roberts later became a judge, rumored model for the judge in Bonfire of the Vanities.

After Wharton, at 19, Steinhardt worried potential employers would identify him as Sol's son. Nobody did. But the job search was brutal — he had no connections, majored in sociology, and was a year younger than his peers. Several interviewers asked if "Steinhardt" was a German name, making him feel self-consciously Jewish. Finally, Calvin Bullock, a traditional white-shoe mutual fund, offered him a $75-a-week position as a statistical assistant.

At Calvin Bullock, a white-shoe mutual fund, Steinhardt experienced Wall Street's genteel anti-Semitism firsthand. Sir Hugh Bullock's annual dinner speech praised "a firm of Christian gentlemen, because like attracts like." The next year, Sir Hugh's eyes found Michael's, and he paused — then said simply "of gentlemen, because like attracts like." Steinhardt calls this "the highlight of my first job on Wall Street."

Army training at Fort Dix included an "Escape and Evasion" exercise where Michael was captured, stripped naked, strapped to a chair with electrodes, and shocked while soldiers shouted antisemitic slurs. It was the only time in his life someone "attacked" him for being Jewish. Throughout his six months of service, he never stopped trading stocks — his friend Eric Sheinberg from Goldman Sachs drove to Fort Dix with margin papers to sign.

After the army, he joined Financial World magazine but was fired at 22 for hiding unanswered subscriber letters in a desk drawer. His boss found the pile while Michael was home sick. He was rehired a week later when his replacement turned out to be a drunk. The lesson stuck — he became diligent about every task.

Father Sol continued loaning cash for investments — sometimes tens of thousands in $100 bills, stuffed in envelopes for a nerve-racking subway ride home. The arrangement was never formally defined. Were they partners? Was it a loan? Neither had ever thought it through.

Chapter 5: The Hottest Analyst on Wall Street

At Loeb Rhoades, hired at 24 as a research analyst covering cyclical industries, Steinhardt's career ignited. The firm carried a great mystique — evolved from a German-Jewish banking family, staffed by highly educated, urbane immigrants like Armand Erpf, Sam Steadman, and Mark Millard. At the head stood John Loeb, a respected if distant leader. Steinhardt loved the camaraderie — fellow analysts shared ideas, played devil's advocate, went out together after work. Without Wharton on his resume, Loeb Rhoades would never have hired him.

He took particular pride in estimating quarterly earnings. One quarter, he predicted General Motors' earnings per share to the penny — a rare feat in the mid-1960s. Then John Loeb Jr. sent him to meet Charlie Bluhdorn, "the mad Austrian" running Gulf and Western Industries. Bluhdorn stomped his feet, cursed, ranted about acquisitions — "my meeting with him was nothing less than exhilarating." Steinhardt wrote a bullish report; the stock tripled in three months. This changed Loeb Rhoades' stance on conglomerates entirely.

He believes he was the first analyst to introduce the word "synergy" in explaining conglomerate investing. He got to know the CEOs of high-profile conglomerates: Derald Ruttenberg of Studebaker Worthington, Saul Steinberg at Leasco (who at 22 tried to buy Chemical Bank), Jimmy Ling of Ling-Temco-Vought. Of six conglomerate stocks he recommended, all went up dramatically — sometimes causing buy-order imbalances that delayed NYSE openings. "For a time, it seemed I was the hottest analyst on Wall Street."

He also first met Alan Greenspan, then a consultant visiting their offices quarterly from Donaldson, Lufkin, Jenrette. "I recall being disappointed that I had learned little from listening to him, and that what he said was mostly an extrapolation of the obvious. There were certainly no clear signs back then that he would one day rule world financial markets."

His worst early mistake was shorting Occidental Petroleum, run by Armand Hammer, "a too-clever man of myriad international conspiracies." He shorted in the mid-20s, averaged up into the 30s, then the stock more than doubled on rumors of a Libyan discovery rivaling Saudi Arabia. "Whenever you lose on the short side, it is that much more frightening because the loss is theoretically without limit." Sitting at his desk after the close, paralyzed and morose, he and partner Howard threatened to hang friend Eric Sheinberg out the window by his thumbs. Eric panicked and screamed until he broke free. They all laughed, and the mood was broken — "at least for the moment."

During his years at Loeb Rhoades, Sol began loaning larger sums for Michael to invest — sometimes handing him envelopes stuffed with $100 bills for a nerve-racking subway ride home. The arrangement was never defined: were they partners? A loan? His father's "easy come, easy go" attitude about money left Michael "almost oblivious" to his own finances. To this day, he does not know how to balance a checking account.

Through a fellow analyst named Howard Berkowitz at A.G. Becker and a friend Jerrold Fine at Dominick & Dominick, the idea of starting their own hedge fund took shape. All three were Wharton men in their mid-twenties, each experiencing a "magical period in which almost everything I touched did well." The name order required extensive debate. Fine opted for the middle (shortest name). Steinhardt and Berkowitz flipped a coin. Steinhardt won.

Their lawyer Paul Roth of Baer and McGoldrick (later Schulte Roth & Zabel) worried the name "sounded more like a Jewish delicatessen than a money management firm." They used it anyway. Paul's counsel over the next thirty years, Steinhardt notes, was "nearly flawless."

Chapter 6: Steinhardt, Fine, Berkowitz & Company

The Six-Day War nearly derailed the launch. At a mass rally outside the United Nations, Rabbi Shlomo Carlebach sang "El Malei Rachamim" — the prayer for the dead. As his voice filled the streets, thousands stood in collective silence. Then he broke down weeping. Steinhardt calls this "a tableau of pain and compassion that I will carry with me for the rest of my life." He tried to fly to Israel to volunteer to fight, but all flights were booked.

Steinhardt, Fine, Berkowitz & Company opened July 10, 1967, with eight employees and $7.7 million in initial capital. The name order was decided by coin flip — Steinhardt won. Lawyer Paul Roth worried it "sounded more like a Jewish delicatessen than a money management firm." The first full year returned 99 percent, versus 9.3 percent for the S&P 500. By fiscal 1969, capital had grown to nearly $30 million. All three partners were millionaires before thirty.

These were the Go-Go years — story stocks bought with scant information. They owned Maine Sugar (growing sugar beets in Maine), National Student Marketing (100x earnings), King Resources (oil and gas discoveries). "A story, whatever its veracity, could be of value, particularly if it were new to the market and had an open-ended quality." They were the greater fools — but they had "an inner tension that came from viewing the portfolio anew with fresh minds every day. We were believers, but we sold."

Their biggest success came with King Resources, whose story was vast oil and gas discoveries and land holdings. They made about five times their money in a few months, relying heavily on chairman John King's promotional pitch. In a few short years, the company went bankrupt. "I learned that a story, whatever its veracity, could be of value, particularly if it were new to the market and had an open-ended quality that provoked one's imagination."

A Fortune article by Carol Loomis listed the top 30 hedge funds and discussed the difficult times most were having in the market — some had declined 70 percent. Steinhardt, Fine, Berkowitz was the only fund on that list still in business ten years later.

The hedge fund philosophy was distinct from A.W. Jones's original 1949 model. Jones sought to eliminate market risk through hedging. Steinhardt wanted flexibility to play both sides and bet on direction. Key principles: invest virtually all personal wealth alongside clients; performance-based fees (1% management plus 20% of profits); pursue absolute returns regardless of market direction. "If our fund lost money, we would not get paid and our personal capital would suffer."

Block trading emerged as a new arena. Steinhardt bought 700,000 shares of Penn Central at 7 from a seller who hadn't checked the NYSE book, sold them at 7¾ eight minutes later for over $500,000 profit. "Those opportunities are gone now. They reflected a transitional period."

Among the many hedge funds that existed in the late 1960s, Steinhardt, Fine, Berkowitz was one of the few that survived the next decade. A Fortune article listed the top 30 hedge funds — theirs was the only one still in business ten years later.

Chapter 7: Judy

Steinhardt met Judy Abrams through a chain of coincidences involving Phil David Fine, a Boston lawyer connected to the Kennedys. Judy had taken the room vacated by Denise Eisenberg, who left to marry Marc Rich (later one of the world's most famous fugitives). The first encounter was in Steinhardt's car pool — Judy sat silently in oversized black sunglasses, resembling Audrey Hepburn. She overheard stock tips (Maine Sugar, Colorado Milling), called her father, who made enough money to buy her first fur coat.

Steinhardt met Judy through a series of coincidences so improbable they read like fiction. The roommate whose departure created the opening was Denise Eisenberg, who left to marry Marc Rich — later one of the world's most famous fugitives. A Boston lawyer named Phil David Fine, connected to the Kennedys, had given Steinhardt Judy's phone number months earlier, but as "a shy bachelor who avoided blind dates," he never called. Their first contact was Steinhardt's car pool — Judy sat silently in oversized black sunglasses, resembling Audrey Hepburn, while everyone else pressed Steinhardt for stock tips.

Their first real date was after Rosh Hashanah. They visited Rabbi Shlomo Carlebach's apartment above his synagogue on West 79th Street, watched Shlomo spar with his imperious mother, then walked along the Hudson River. By the end of the night, Steinhardt was thoroughly infatuated. He broke up with his girlfriend and started seeing only Judy. He gave her geography quizzes — capitals of Cyprus, Bulgaria, Ethiopia — needing to establish superior knowledge. She "never lacked for conviction and was perfectly comfortable with what she knew and what she did not know."

Their wedding on April 28, 1968, produced a memorable clash between Sol and Judy's mother. Sol wanted to buy the champagne — he only drank Dom Perignon. Refused. Then he approached the Lester Lanin bandleader with a $100 bill: "Play some Jewish music." The bandleader wouldn't take the money. Judy's mother: "I'm in charge of this wedding, Mr. Steinhardt, and there will be no Jewish music." One of few people to beat Sol in a confrontation.

Judy was the stabilizing force in Steinhardt's volatile life. She managed the household, the children — David (born March 3, 1969), Daniel (June 6, 1971), and Sara (February 10, 1975) — and absorbed his mood swings when performance faltered. "Judy has an innate balance; she was truly centered in contrast to my volatility." She rarely lost her temper, even when provoked by her husband. He has never once packed his own suitcase.

Her judgment of people far surpassed his — he was "easily finagled by charm" while she was discerning about character. She urged him to buy The Gap when she was shopping there constantly for the kids. He didn't. "She was absolutely right. To my regret, I bought no shares." More than a few times, people confused Judy with his daughter — "a fact I have not always taken so well."

Judy's entrepreneurial streak led to a hydroponic greenhouse venture using waste heat from a New York utility in Queens. With marketing from Milton Glaser, she created "Herbs Alive," selling basil and other herbs still living with roots attached. She pitched supermarket chains and restaurants herself. The business grew but profits didn't; they quit. From early on, Steinhardt tried to enhance his mother Claire's life. He bought her an apartment in The Brevoort on Fifth Avenue. The sales agent was a tough, no-compromise woman named Leona Brown — who later married Harry Helmsley.

Claire eventually married Mark Deskin, one of the original mechanics at the first Ford Motor Company plant. Mark could fix anything — the kids adored him for repairing their bikes and go-carts. Michael was almost as happy as Claire was. "She would now have her own life with a husband to take care of and a husband who would take care of her."

Chapter 8: Variant Perception

This is the intellectual heart of the book. Steinhardt defines variant perception as "holding a well-founded view that was meaningfully different from market consensus." The only analytic tool that truly mattered was "an intellectually advantaged disparate view." This required knowing more and perceiving the situation better than others. Critically, understanding what the market expected was "at least as important as, and often different from, fundamental knowledge."

The Nifty Fifty short was the ultimate test. In the early 1970s, institutional managers worshipped "one-decision" stocks — Avon, Xerox, Polaroid, McDonald's, Eastman Kodak, GE, J&J, Coca-Cola. Research reports projected earnings twenty and thirty years out. Steinhardt went net short the best companies in America on one basis: price. "Who were we to predict when this overvaluation phenomenon would end?" The trade was "truly contrarian, and when views are truly contrarian, they are inevitably uncomfortable. Courage and the ability to withstand pain are required."

Tony Cilluffo, the firm's most colorful character, proved critical. Tony smoked three packs a day, ate the same lunch as long as they were making money, and had "the courage to make market calls in extremis" — predicting that a stock at 80 would trade at 10 within a year. His unswerving conviction kept the firm short when rational arguments urged capitulation.

"Just as outright euphoria is often a sign of a market top, fear is, for sure, a sign of a market bottom. Time and time again, in every market cycle I have witnessed, the extremes of emotion always appear, even among experienced investors. When the world wants to buy only Treasury bills, you can almost close your eyes and get long stocks." — Michael Steinhardt

In fiscal 1974, the market fell 38 percent; Steinhardt was up 34 percent. Most "star" portfolio managers at major banks lost 50 percent; some mutual fund managers lost 80 to 90 percent. Then, when the Dow hit 577 in December 1974, they picked the bottom "nearly perfectly" — switching net exposure from minus 55 percent to plus 35 percent in one month. They bought single-digit stocks trading at 75-80 percent of book value: Howard Johnson, Zapata, Bath Industries, Suburban Propane, Tropicana. "We bought them right and, as usual, we sold them early." Up about 66 percent that year.

In 1970, the firm expanded. They started the offshore fund SP International S.A. with $2 million initial capitalization — it would eventually grow to over $3 billion. Total assets under management were about $35 million. Their Beaver Street office was too small for a trading room but had space for a bumper pool table, which became central to the culture. Analysts from brokerage houses would be challenged to games. "People seemed to play pool in ways that paralleled how they traded." Jerry was classic and accurate, Howard had great technique, and Steinhardt — who had never held a cue — learned to emphasize defense, blocking holes and frustrating opponents.

The process of shorting itself had a peculiar psychology. Short selling requires borrowing shares to sell something you don't own — it feels like "rooting against the home team." Management contacts who viewed you as an ally become hostile. Information flow dries up. One rule makes it harder: you must sell on an "uptick," meaning you sometimes have to establish the short position early, enduring pain before the thesis plays out.

The 1970 market break proved the firm's mettle. They reversed their Go-Go long positions into shorts — National Cash Register, Memorex, Intel, University Computing — often bought over $100, covered under $10. They went net short for the first time, meaning short positions exceeded longs. Most hedge fund peers, who "viewed short selling mostly as cosmetic," performed poorly. Steinhardt stayed short and traded actively.

Not everything went right. They nearly walked into the Equity Funding fraud, buying 100,000 shares at $17.50 just before the scandal broke. Journalist Dan Dorfman warned cryptically: "Not all the pretty girls are as pretty as they look." The stock reopened at $6. And in the "Hmmm" incident, Steinhardt sold a large STP block, then encountered Studebaker CEO Ruttenberg at a Kennedy cocktail party. When told about the sale, Ruttenberg said only "Hmmm." The next morning, Steinhardt bought every Studebaker subsidiary he could find. Trading halted. Buyback announced at a premium. The SEC subpoenaed him: "Would you place orders simply based on a 'Hmmm'?" "Absolutely," he answered. "Instincts are often all one has."

Chapter 9: There and Back

At 38, with a net worth exceeding $7 million, Steinhardt left Wall Street for a sabbatical year. He felt the learning curve had flattened. He viewed professions on a "vertical scale of values" — doctors above accountants, heroes like Schweitzer, Einstein, and Ben-Gurion above financiers. He was making rich people richer — "certainly not a sin, but not a qualification for sainthood."

His health had deteriorated: chain-smoking, 220 pounds, tried every diet, pill, book, shrink, nutritionist, and spa in the country. He had been hypnotized, tried acupuncture, and once had a balloon put in his stomach. On bad trading days, he would leave the office with an uncontrollable craving for nuts — buying quarter pounds of macadamias, cashews, pistachios, and pecans from the Treat Boutique on 86th Street. Within minutes, his anxiety would drop. "Was I chemically addicted to fats? I think so."

He took yoga with five women, piano lessons (learned "March of the Wee Folk"), tennis with a Croatian pro in Queens (became "singularly mediocre"), Torah study with Shlomo Carlebach's twin brother Eli. He hid behind trees in Central Park to secretly watch his son David in gym class. On some days he had absolutely nothing on his schedule and felt "a peculiar sense of guilt."

He built industrial parks in Israeli development towns, turned down an offer to head an interstate banking commission for the Carter White House, declined to manage New York City's $10 billion pension funds. He stopped reading the Wall Street Journal and cut contact with the financial world. "I did not miss being in the game — and that realization shocked me."

When asked why successful businessmen stayed on Wall Street beyond their need for money, he heard hollow answers: "Because I love the game," or "It's the only thing I know," or "It's a way to keep score." Most became passive philanthropists. Steinhardt wanted something more — though he wasn't yet sure what.

He invested directly in Israel, building small industrial parks in development towns like Kiryat Gat and Ofakim near the southern border. These towns, mostly peopled by North African immigrants, represented what Zionism was all about. He wanted Israel to rely on investors, not handouts. The partnership didn't work out stylistically, but he considered it a worthwhile endeavor.

In Bedford, Steinhardt bought a 16-acre property that eventually grew to 52 acres. He was "enamored of water — being near it, walking around the edge of it, and sometimes simply looking at it." A stream ran behind the house, backing up to a tributary that flows into the Croton Lake Reservoir. He and Judy joined Century Country Club in Purchase — the most prestigious Jewish country club in America, founded by German Jewish families described in Stephen Birmingham's Our Crowd.

But by 1979, nothing else matched the intellectual provocation of the market. He returned October 1, 1979, with a new mission: to build a hedge fund larger than any that had existed. Howard Berkowitz left to start HPB Associates. The firm became Steinhardt Partners. Institutional fundraising followed — Larry Tisch committed money from Loews and NYU's endowment. The Common Fund came aboard after its president George Keane was intrigued by a pitch that was "not recommended" for his institution. Eventually, the World Bank, Carnegie Corporation, Yale, and Duke invested. After presenting to Yale's committee, President Bart Giamatti remarked: "I had no idea we had Hamlet running our money."

Chapter 10: Menageries and Movies

The Bedford estate grew to 52 acres with streams, orchards of 70-80 apple varieties, 500+ daylilies, Japanese maples, a winery (wine "comparable to the Rothschilds' in one respect: it cost as much to make"), and an extraordinary menagerie. Steinhardt kept zebras, kangaroos, capybara, lemurs, cranes, camels, spider monkeys, and a llama named Angel that allowed only Steinhardt to kiss it. A camel bit a guest; the animal had to be put down to test for rabies (negative, but too late).

He purchased the Grand and Steeple Jason Islands in the Falklands — the world's largest albatross nesting area, home to three penguin species. Getting there required a once-a-week flight from Punta Arenas, Chile, to Stanley — six hours on a plane with no bathroom. The British military provided helicopters for the final hour. When they arrived, they walked among thousands of eight-pound albatross chicks and curious penguins that nibbled their shoes. Aggressive Johnny Rook birds attacked their heads and colonized the helicopter propellers — they had to throw raw meat to clear them for takeoff. Steinhardt gifted both islands to the Wildlife Conservation Society.

The property also included a house that once belonged to Theodore Dreiser, who had called it Iroki — Japanese for "Garden of Pleasant Surprises." Steinhardt tore down the house but kept the stone substructure. Years later, his daughter Sara got married in this "ruin." He continued the name.

Steinhardt found horticulture as consuming as markets — reading nursery catalogs before bed, acquiring 500 daylilies in one year, collecting over 20 varieties of yellow-blossomed magnolias. He could identify most of his 70-80 apple varieties blindfolded by scent, size, shape, and taste. "In most other areas of my life, it does not fit my personality to be patient and uniformly enthusiastic. In our garden, I have managed to be both."

In 1990, Steinhardt held a summit at Bedford for Israeli economic leaders to discuss the massive Russian Jewish immigration that was straining Israel's economy. The delegation included Finance Minister Beiga Shohat, the Israeli ambassador, Lester Crown (CEO of General Dynamics), and Leonard Garment (former legal adviser to Nixon). After a morning of discussions, a tour of the menagerie led to Steinhardt being pressured into buying a Thai elephant for the Jerusalem Zoo. She was named Michaela. He then told Minister Shohat he had a goose that spoke Hebrew. The Cereopsis hen ran over shaking its chartreuse beak and uttered guttural sounds. "Nothing came of this infrastructure summit, but I am certain that, to this day, there are people in Jerusalem still talking about my goose that speaks Hebrew."

Art collecting with Judy encompassed Klee, Cézanne, Schiele, Picasso, Matisse, Pollock, de Kooning, Goya, and Seurat. They gifted a Rembrandt to Israel — "Israel's first Rembrandt." The gold phiale fiasco was Steinhardt's costliest blunder outside of markets: he bought a Greek gold ceremonial plate for over $1 million after the Met museum authenticated it, only to have US Customs confiscate it on Italy's behalf. He lost every appeal, including a denied Supreme Court hearing. "In stock market terms, I have lost 100 percent of my investment times two."

The Judaica collection grew to include anti-Semitic objects — wooden toys caricaturing religious Jews with humped backs and long noses, and cartoons from nineteenth-century English publications. Steinhardt's motivation was "overwhelmingly visual." He sometimes chastised himself for not becoming fully knowledgeable about the cultures behind the art. "Using stock market parlance, I have often invested before I investigated."

Movie investments began as a favor to Teddy Kollek, mayor of Jerusalem, whose son Amos needed $40,000 to finish a film. Goodbye, New York was a commercial hit. Double Edge, starring Faye Dunaway, lost every penny of $1.7 million. Steinhardt's cameo appearances: soliciting a prostitute in Highstakes, a ticket-taker in Forever Lulu. Hotel Terminus, about Nazi Klaus Barbie, won the Academy Award for Best Foreign Documentary. "I must confess to a continuing love of movie making and to an absolute conviction that I will never make a penny at it."

Chapter 11: The Crash of 1987

In April 1987, six months before the crash, Steinhardt wrote a prescient investor letter warning of extended P/E ratios, low dividend yields, and the risks of program trading. He predicted fifty- or even hundred-point daily Dow moves. "Prescient, yet I would not benefit from my own advice." By summer, the temptation of individual stocks had overcome macro caution. By August, the funds were up 35 percent, and he was net long.

On the Friday before the crash, the market fell over 100 points. Steinhardt called a staff meeting but couldn't get clarity on what had changed. Even over the weekend, the fear in the air and press "largely escaped me."

On Black Monday, October 19, stocks opened dramatically lower. By 10:30 AM, the Dow was down 120 points. Feeling the market was oversold, Steinhardt began buying S&P futures: "Buy 10 S&Ps every 10 ticks." Orders executed far faster than expected. The market briefly rallied from down 250 to down 100, and he thought he had picked the bottom. By 2 PM it was falling again. By 3 PM — 300, 350. Reagan announced he would speak after the close. Down 450. By the closing bell, the Dow was down 508 points — 22 percent, the worst day since the Great Depression. "I was still trying to get a grasp on what the market was saying, but I could not figure it out." They stayed in the office until midnight.

The next morning, Morgan Stanley raised margin requirements. Steinhardt moved all futures clearing to Goldman Sachs and didn't trade with Morgan Stanley for years. The funds went from up 45 percent in September to up only 4 percent by year-end. Then the convertible arbitrage portfolio manager revealed that his bonds had been mismarked — the "low-risk" portfolio was actually down 30 percent, not up 10. When the manager muttered "All I want to do is kill myself," Steinhardt replied: "Can I watch?"

"There was no economic justification for the market's collapse. The crash was overwhelmingly a phenomenon of the market's internal inability to adjust to the vastly increased volume and new products. Ironically, the one time when investors most needed their portfolio insurance to limit losses, it failed them miserably." — Michael Steinhardt

The crash itself was caused by market structure, not economics. No recession followed. Portfolio insurance and program trading created computer-based techniques that sold baskets of stocks as markets declined — tested in "normal" markets but never during dramatic declines. The discontinuity of prices and constant selling of "insured" portfolios exacerbated the crash unprecedentedly. The specialist system had shrunk relative to market size, and volume had increased so dramatically that the tape ran hours late — "people had no idea how low or at what prices most stocks were trading."

He calculated the number of months in all the years he had been in business — profitable months versus losing months. The losing months were less than 5 percent of the total. "In the final analysis, that was a record I could live with, except during those few months when I was actually losing money. Then my memory of past successes faded. I lost perspective."

Feeling depressed, Steinhardt visited the Brooklyn Botanical Gardens on a snowy day and committed $3 million — then the largest gift ever to a Brooklyn institution. "I needed to do something that would make me feel good about myself again." The Steinhardt Conservatory was dedicated exactly one year later, on the anniversary of Black Monday.

He came back with a vengeance: 1988 up 48 percent, 1989 and 1990 each up 26 percent, then three consecutive 60 percent years from 1991-1993. "I have a far keener memory of the painful moments of 1987 than I do of the next six years of glorious success."

Chapter 12: The Steinhardt Style

Steinhardt won a daily market-direction bet 22 consecutive times at Loeb Rhoades in 1965. "This win was not random. At the same time, it was not a reflection of analysis, intelligence, or methodology." Over decades, making "cumulatively more judgments, and more investment decisions based on the same kinds of data, than almost anyone else" produces what appears to be instinct. It is really sharpened pattern recognition — fewer mistakes through repetition.

His framework for analysts was strict: tell me in two minutes (1) the idea, (2) the consensus view, (3) your variant perception, (4) a trigger event. If no variant perception existed, he had no interest. He tested conviction relentlessly: "If the stock went from 10 to 12, are you willing to pay 12 for it? If not, sell it."

"The quick and the dead" was his mantra. He sold Iowa Beef Processing at $29 while the analyst was at lunch — four months later, an $80 tender offer arrived. He sold 3.5 million shares of Bank of New York while his analyst was at the bank's offices praising the stock. Sometimes he liquidated the entire portfolio in one trade with Goldman Sachs or Salomon Brothers to "start all over again."

The confrontational management style was legendary. Employees used terms like "battered children" and "random violence." A psychiatrist observed the firm for months; when he tried to intervene during a tirade, Steinhardt threw him out. "How much time did you do at Steinhardt?" was the joke on the Street. Yet alumni said it was the most formative period of their careers. Head trader John Lattanzio, a high-school dropout from Queens with an encyclopedic scatological repertoire, was the essential buffer.

The dress code — or lack thereof — was legendary even before Silicon Valley made casual dress fashionable. Steinhardt walked around in pullovers and Bermuda shorts, persuading himself this would "somehow ameliorate the pressure." He sometimes blanched when a Swiss banker visited and saw the motley group. The trading room in the 1990s "looked more like a war room" — extra desks squeezed everywhere, boxes piled up, phones strewn across the floor, partially eaten lunches on display. Judy refused to visit because she couldn't stand the disarray.

The IBM trade exemplified his greatest strength and weakness simultaneously. In mid-1993, he bought seven million shares when the consensus was universally negative and the stock was at its absolute low. Made 30 percent in six months — then sold much too soon, missing the multi-year run. "Once I am proved right in the market, the bottom line is of less interest to me. I am ready for the next idea."

"Warren Buffett has said, 'If you are not willing to own a stock for 10 years, do not even think about owning it for 10 minutes.' The truth is, I have never owned a stock for 10 years, but I have had the unique and profitable experience of owning some very good companies for 10 minutes." — Michael Steinhardt

The CUC International short was the most frustrating position. Analyst Herb Chen concluded the company was cooking its books. They shorted from 1989, endured three years of grinding losses, and finally covered after losing $50 million. Years later, CUC merged into Cendant, which revealed $500 million in fictitious profits and $19 billion in investor losses — the largest accounting fraud in history at that time. "No solace to us."

Steinhardt's style was "four yards up the middle in a cloud of dust" — surprisingly few home runs for a 30 percent annualized gross return over 28 years. Gains earned the hard way. Losing months were less than 5 percent of all months traded.

Chapter 13: Dabbling in Politics

Steinhardt's political journey was a study in contrarian evolution. In the 1960s, surrounded by Brooklyn liberals, he supported Barry Goldwater. He read the National Review with enthusiasm and admired William Buckley. But gradually, as the world moved right in the 1970s and 80s, he moved left — always out of step with the mainstream.

In the mid-1980s he became the largest financial contributor to the Democratic Leadership Council — roughly $250,000 per year for nearly a decade. He became its legal chairman and chairman of its think tank, the Progressive Policy Institute. The DLC's ideas included national service, balanced budgets, earned income tax credit, welfare reform, NAFTA, charter schools, and reinventing government. Steinhardt was "awed by the ease with which many of these ideas quickly became part of the national political dialogue."

Al From, the DLC's president — "a chubby little Jewish guy from Indiana who lived and breathed political ideology" — brought in an unknown Arkansas governor named Bill Clinton. "Bill Clinton is the best political animal I've ever met," From said. Clinton's late-night phone calls from Little Rock became a regular occurrence. At a dinner in Steinhardt's apartment, Clinton captivated the room — "particularly the ladies" — wearing fancy cowboy boots while Senator Sam Nunn stood in the background, virtually ignored.

Clinton incorporated DLC ideas into his national persona and won the 1992 election. But the first two years disappointed: gays-in-the- military, the health care fiasco, ultraliberal appointments. Hillary was exercising considerable independence to the left of Bill. Steinhardt proposed a "third way" movement and approached Bill Bradley, who got down on his six-foot- five-inch frame and knelt: "I'm prepared to do many things in politics, but not commit political suicide."

Steinhardt resigned from the DLC in 1995 after seeing Clinton as guest of honor at its convention. "I am not constitutionally cut out for the political world. I see things in black and white, whereas politics deals in grays." The DLC's ideas — moderate Democrat, "the third way" — were eventually absorbed by Clinton's successful second term. "He had the remarkable ability to learn from his mistakes."

The fundraising culture soured him. Several Wall Streeters aggressively solicited contributions with explicit assurances of ambassadorships. One was "perfectly comfortable announcing his fundraising goal and its intrinsic tie to his personal future." Many who joined the process "did not care one whit about ideas. Their goal was personal aggrandizement."

At a dinner with Al From during the 1992 campaign, From's assistant returned from a phone call looking flushed — tomorrow's Star would report Gennifer Flowers' twelve-year affair with Clinton. Steinhardt asked From if it was true. From smiled: "Michael, Bill has always been a man of great appetites."

In his assessment, Clinton as a role model "has failed. In the end, his qualities as a role model will be remembered far longer than the economy he presided over." But the DLC's redefinition of the Democratic Party — from redistribution to growth, welfare to work, big government to reinvention — endures.

Chapter 14: The Worst Year of My Life

Three consecutive 60 percent years bred hubris. The capital base swelled to nearly $5 billion. Steinhardt expanded into global macro: European bonds (French OATs, German BUNDs, Italian BTPs, Spanish BONOs), emerging markets, currencies. The foreign bond portfolio totaled more than $30 billion. Each basis point move meant $10 million. The daily P&L ran thirty pages and "read like the German code from World War II."

With growing confidence, they hired international portfolio managers, analysts, economic consultants, and "think tanks." The organization ballooned to over 100 people trading across global markets. Steinhardt, who had never been a model manager of personnel, now found himself overseeing trades in countries where he joked he "did not know the area code." He was trading the French CAC 40, the German DAX, and the Japanese Nikkei. They shorted volatility across three equity markets. They held convergence trades, swap spreads, and currency crosses including Mark/Swiss, Sterling/Yen, and Mark/Paris.

Wall Street was flush with credit. Derivative products required almost no money down, creating unseen leverage. They purchased and financed their enormous bond portfolio for as little as a 1 percent haircut — meaning $100 million in bonds required only $1 million of capital. By 1993, the foreign bond portfolio exceeded $30 billion.

On February 4, 1994, the Federal Reserve raised rates for the first time in five years. European bonds plummeted as liquidity vanished. The trade was catastrophically crowded — not just hedge funds but the proprietary desks at Bankers Trust, Merrill Lynch, J.P. Morgan, and Goldman Sachs all held the same positions. When Askin Capital Management collapsed trading leveraged mortgage securities, the contagion spread further. "In times of stress, markets that are not normally correlated suddenly are." Keynes's warning proved prescient: "Markets can remain irrational longer than you can remain solvent." They finished liquidating by end of March, down 30 percent.

Steinhardt's instincts, honed over decades in domestic stocks, were simply not applicable to European fixed income. The normal flows of information that would ring warning bells in the domestic world were "perhaps somewhere on a distant continent." He was reliant on new people with whom he had never been in the trenches. "It was a formula for disaster."

"If I was a victim of anything, it was of hubris or unjustified confidence in my own abilities — perhaps the result of too many too-easy successes. I had lost sight of my own limitations." — Michael Steinhardt

Simultaneously, the Treasury note scandal settled. The SEC and Justice Department accused Salomon Brothers, Steinhardt, and Caxton Corporation of colluding to corner the two-year Treasury note market in 1991 — they had owned 94 percent of the notes. Steinhardt settled without admission of guilt, paying over $70 million in fines and fees (75 percent personally). The bond bet from 1990-1993 had made $600 million for investors, but the scandal left him wounded.

The Wall Street Journal's headline declared he had lost a billion dollars. Reporters besieged him. He dreaded each call. His investors were extraordinarily supportive, but their encouragement "inadvertently added to my discomfort." Only family provided real solace. Son David, himself new to the investment business, understood what was happening; his support stood out in an otherwise bleak landscape.

The year ended down 31 percent — the only meaningful loss in 28 years. On Good Friday, he "could hardly function." He began fantasizing about leaving the business but refused to go out after the only bad year of his career. "I could not avoid feeling as if my very worth as a human being depended on my continually making money. What was I worth when I lost?"

Chapter 15: "Steinhardt Quits!"

At 54, Steinhardt felt the calculus had shifted permanently. "The highs from success were not quite as high as in the past, and the lows seemed much lower." He had bounced back from 1987 with six outstanding years, but 1994 "had taken a part of me that could not be retrieved." He could not build a quality organization independent of his leadership. There was no successor. "In too many ways, I had run a one-man show, and I was tired of performing."

He would not sell the firm — he could not envision a firm with his name on it run by someone else. Years earlier, he had turned down an offer from Dreyfus Corporation to buy a minority interest. Later, he worked with Merrill Lynch toward creating one of the largest closed-end funds ever, then changed his mind at the last moment. If someone traded under the name Steinhardt without him, it would have to be his son David.

But he refused to leave after the only bad year. He had to make the money back first. By September 1995, the funds were up about 22 percent, invested heavily in blue-chip financials: Chemical Bank (2.7 million shares, which announced a merger with Chase Manhattan), Sallie Mae, BankAmerica, Aetna, Travelers. The year would end up 26 percent — enough to recoup most of 1994's losses.

Head trader John Lattanzio kept asking: "Are you going to pull the trigger, Mike?" He couldn't answer — he changed his mind weekly. But by September, he had crossed the Rubicon. On October 11, 1995, he gathered the entire firm — partners, traders, analysts, back office — in the conference room. Judy joined them. Twelve top brokers got confidential heads-up calls simultaneously.

Without a prepared script, he announced the closing. Some employees cried. "This business has been my life, but it's now time for me to move on to the next phase. I will miss you all. I will miss this business. But everything must come to an end." The New York Post headline: "Steinhardt Quits!" The most-quoted statistic: one dollar invested in 1967 was worth $481 at closing, versus $19 in the S&P 500. Early investor Richard Cooper told Time he had invested $500,000 twenty-eight years ago — it had grown to over $100 million.

The liquidation ran smoothly: 85 percent returned by year-end, 95 percent by April 1996. Steinhardt agonized over every sale, demanding top dollar. He was generous with severance. Most employees were besieged with job offers.

In the Washington Post, he offered his market view: "We are in the midst of a great bull market, an extraordinary bull market. Never in our lifetime has a smaller percentage of the world's resources been spent on the military. The world in the broadest sense has become a much, much better place. Consumerism is the only ism that seems to count anymore."

On his first day in "retirement," he sat at his desk, saw the Bloomberg screen, and placed S&P futures orders through the old Goldman Sachs line — not realizing all accounts had been closed. His administrator Lisa Addeo told Goldman: "Open one up! This is Michael Steinhardt, for God's sake. He's trading. That's what he does. Anyway, whatever orders he put in, you know he's good for the money." Within weeks he had hired a trader, Chaim Rosenberg. He had been trading since age thirteen. He was not going to stop.

Chapter 16: The Death of My Father

Sol moved to Las Vegas at seventy, continuing to gamble daily with a "love/hate relationship" that produced net losses in the millions. His late-life strategy: make three large bets per casino visit, walk away on losses. He rarely had the discipline. When sports bets went wrong in the last minutes, he once kicked in his television. "The golden years, what golden years? It's all bullshit."

In his eighties, Sol's health collapsed: lymphoma, macular degeneration (near blindness), chronic hearing loss deteriorating to near deafness, shingles so severe he couldn't shave the left side of his face for four years, and prostate cancer. "The golden years, what golden years? It's all bullshit," he would rasp. He had extreme distrust of the medical profession — if frustrated in a hospital, he would literally rip the tubes from his arms and walk out.

His friends told Michael how proud Sol was of him — but Sol never said it directly. Instead, he regularly asked Michael's net worth and took pleasure in the number. More often, he was critical — claiming Michael hadn't given him enough in return for "staking" him early in his career. On his last visit, Sol lay in bed looking like "the ghost of the man he once was" and said, in his paper-thin voice: "We all make mistakes in life, and we do things we regret. I did the best I could do." Even then, Michael could not bring himself to say "I love you."

The flaws in his relationship with Sol affected how he dealt with his own children. With each he felt boundless love — but was he showing it enough? He was harsher with his sons than he wished. He would fret about controlling his temper, consciously comparing himself to his father.

Sol died in August 1999. At the funeral at Riverside Chapel, Michael broke down weeping before he could speak a word of his eulogy. "How I had longed for him to be the father I wanted him to be. How, as a young boy growing up in Brooklyn, I had lain awake in bed at night and wondered what I had done not to have a father." Even at the end, he could not bring himself to tell Sol he loved him.

"If one can be both, my father was 'one of a kind' and 'last of his kind.' His was the chronicle of the Jewish experience in the twentieth century in America." — Michael Steinhardt, eulogy

The last thing Sol asked for: "Get the baby, baby Jacob, a Jewish star. A Jewish star with diamonds. That was my father, a Jewish star with diamonds." Michael still sees him in the mirror sometimes — "particularly when I have a stubby beard, or in profile with my belly protruding."

In the final years, Michael forced himself to visit Sol more regularly than ever, but did so out of obligation. "I saw him because it was the right thing to do. I wish affection for my father had come more naturally to me." It is "one of life's ironies" that the ambiguity he felt toward Sol — the man who gave him stocks at thirteen and Wharton at sixteen — would never fully resolve, even in death.

Mother Claire was diagnosed with Alzheimer's and moved to the Hebrew Home for the Aged in Riverdale. She recognized Michael but spoke disjointedly. Sometimes, with clarity, she asked: "When am I going home?"

Chapter 17: Two Rivers

Steinhardt's metaphor for his life: the confluence of two great rivers. The Eastern European Jewish river — religion, culture, values. And the secular American river — openness, social mobility, material prosperity. For fifty years, he traveled almost exclusively along the American river. Now he works exclusively on strengthening the Jewish one.

The crisis was real and measurable. Within thirty years, the Jewish population outside Israel — about eight million — was projected to decline to four million, driven by intermarriage (half of all Jews marrying non-Jews) and aging populations in the Diaspora. Steinhardt decided his philanthropic focus would be shaped by projections of the future, not commemorations of the past. "When you're playing a losing game, what do you do?" He would commit to innovation as the priority — not just supporting programs but underwriting paradigm shifts.

In 1994 he created the Jewish Life Network to revitalize Jewish identity for non-Orthodox Jews leading secular lives. He opened Makor, a cultural center on West 67th Street for Jewish professionals in their twenties and thirties — no "Jews only" restriction, because integrated Jews would be "turned off" by that rule. About 80 percent self-selected as Jewish anyway. Makor hosted filmmakers, playwrights, musicians, poets. Its mailing list exceeded 25,000. He gifted it to the 92nd Street YMHA in 2001.

He also founded the Partnership for Excellence in Jewish Education, providing matching funds up to $500,000 for communities establishing new non-Orthodox day schools. And as co-chairman of the Hillel Foundation, he helped launch the Jewish Campus Service Corps — eighty interns each year introducing students to "doing Jewish" on campus.

The crown jewel is birthright israel, conceived on a stone wall outside the Israel Museum in Jerusalem with Charles Bronfman. The vision: every Jew aged 18 to 26 gets a free ten-day trip to Israel as a rite of passage. Each put up $5 million in seed money, then $9 million each. Netanyahu committed $20 million per year from the Israeli government. The first trips launched in January 2000 with 6,000 students from around the world.

At the final gala in Jerusalem, Steinhardt delivered a speech modeled on the Gettysburg Address to 5,000 birthright participants: "Four millennia ago, our ancestors brought forth on this earth a new people, nurtured by the belief that every human being is created in the image of God." The standing ovation was thunderous. A young man came up crying: "I never believed I could experience something like this in my life. I feel a connection to the Jews who have come before me that I didn't even know existed."

At a charitable evening at Buckingham Palace for the Israel Philharmonic, Steinhardt struck up a conversation with a man named Chichester and offered to trade identities for the evening — not realizing he was speaking to the Earl of Chichester. The orchestra played "Hatikva," the Israeli national anthem, in Buckingham Palace — perhaps for the first time. It was, Steinhardt muses, a long way from the British mandate.

Steinhardt's desired epitaph is not about money. "I hope that my career as a money manager will not dominate my epitaph. The epitaph I would rather have is that I helped reinvigorate the non-Orthodox Jewish community in America." The tears on that young man's face, he writes, represented "the strong reunion of the two rivers of my life: a reinvigorated American Judaism."

What Can Be Safely Skipped

Chapter 7: Judy — Personal Details

The courtship and wedding details, while charming, don't add investment insight. The Sol-vs-mother-in-law champagne confrontation is the only essential scene. The children's college application stories and Judy's hydroponics venture can be skimmed.

Chapter 10: Menageries — Animal Inventory

The extensive cataloging of every species in the Bedford menagerie and every plant in the garden. The Falklands purchase and art collecting are interesting; the berry varieties and daylily counts are not.

Chapter 10: Movie Investments

Every movie lost money. The details of each film's plot and distribution are inessential. The lesson is the same each time: Wall Streeters are terrible movie investors.

Chapter 13: Dabbling in Politics — DLC Details

The internal mechanics of the DLC — board meetings, individual trustees, policy development — are period-specific. The essential points: Steinhardt helped shape Clinton-era centrism, learned that "ideas mattered little to most of the elected officials I came to know," and discovered he was not constitutionally suited for politics.

Chapter 17: Two Rivers — Philanthropy Details

The specifics of day school funding, Hillel campus programs, and the Buckingham Palace anecdote. The birthright israel concept and the "two rivers" metaphor are essential; the organizational details of PEJE and the Jewish Campus Service Corps are not.

Trading & Investing Lessons

Variant Perception as Alpha Source

Steinhardt's entire edge was holding a well-founded view different from consensus and betting heavily on it. This is the core of every trading edge — knowing something the market doesn't, or interpreting what everyone knows differently. The profit comes during the transition from variant to consensus. Every successful trade begins with the question: what does the market expect, and why might it be wrong?

Crowded Trades and Liquidity Illusions

The 1994 European bond disaster is a masterclass in crowded trade dynamics. When every hedge fund and every prop desk holds the same position, liquidity is an illusion — the bid disappears precisely when you need it most. Markets that aren't normally correlated suddenly are in times of stress. Leverage amplifies this effect catastrophically.

The Danger of Hubris After Streaks

Three consecutive 60 percent years bred the confidence to run $30 billion in foreign bonds. The best time to increase risk management is when everything is working — that is precisely when you are most vulnerable to complacency.

Selling Too Soon vs. Holding Too Long

Steinhardt consistently sold too early — IBM, Iowa Beef, The Gap (never bought at all). He was addicted to picking inflection points, not riding trends. For traders who share this bias, a partial-position strategy (sell half, let the rest run) can capture both the variant- perception profit and the trend-following continuation.

Short Selling Requires Different Psychology

The OXY short, the CUC short, and the Nifty Fifty shorts each demonstrate that being right on shorts requires timing, conviction, and tolerance for theoretically unlimited loss. The maximum gain on a short is 100 percent. The psychological asymmetry is brutal.

Performance Anxiety as Both Edge and Risk

Steinhardt's obsessive daily monitoring — P&L by position, losing months under 5 percent — was both his competitive advantage and his psychological vulnerability. The same intensity that drove 30 percent annual returns also produced clinical depression during drawdowns. Sustainable trading requires a mechanism for separating identity from performance.

Key Takeaways

The Market Is Not Random

Unlike pure gambling, developing expertise in markets produces measurably better results. Steinhardt's father gave him gambling's emotional rush; the stock market gave him an arena where that rush could be channeled into something with positive expected value.

Absolute Returns, Not Relative

Being down 10 percent when the market is down 20 percent is not acceptable. Hedge fund investors pay for absolute positive returns regardless of market direction. This standard forces a manager to be flexible — long, short, bonds, stocks, currencies — rather than tethered to a benchmark.

Instinct Is Trained, Not Innate

The 22-day winning streak in daily market direction bets was not luck. It was the cumulative product of thousands of daily decisions made since age thirteen. "Good instincts" emerge from repetition, not from natural talent.

The Sabbatical Test

Steinhardt spent a year away from markets and found nothing that matched. If a trader cannot find any pursuit as intellectually engaging as markets, that is a signal — this is the right career. If they can, they should leave.

Invest Your Own Money Alongside Clients

This is both a fiduciary principle and a competitive signal. Steinhardt never invested with a manager who didn't do the same. It eliminates the agency problem and ensures the manager's interests are genuinely aligned.

Contrarian Ideas

The Best Managers Focus on Losses

On a day the fund is up 2 percent while the market is up 1 percent, Steinhardt focused not on the gain but on the losing positions. "I will often ask a money manager what his biggest mistake has been. The best traders remember their failures better than their successes."

A Good Short Seller Is Un-American

Short selling was vilified as unpatriotic. Steinhardt compares it to his father rooting against the home team at the Polo Grounds. But shorting overvalued companies is not destruction — it is price discovery. The market is more efficient with short sellers in it than without.

Leverage Is Conservative When Used Correctly

Steinhardt argued that a hedged fund using leverage is actually more conservative than a traditional manager who is 95 percent long all the time. The hedge fund can make money in down markets; the traditional manager cannot. The distinction is not leverage itself but whether both sides of the portfolio are managed with conviction.

Atheism and Jewish Commitment Are Compatible

Steinhardt's most contrarian personal position: devoting his fortune to Jewish renewal while firmly rejecting God. "The heroism is all human. The suffering is all human." He places himself in a tradition of Jewish atheism alongside Spinoza, Freud, and Einstein.

Frameworks

The Variant Perception Framework

(1) Identify the consensus view. (2) Develop a well-founded disparate view based on superior knowledge or perception. (3) Assess the probability of your view becoming the new consensus. (4) Size the position based on conviction and available liquidity. (5) Profit as the market moves from the old consensus to your view.

The Four-Part Analyst Pitch

(1) The idea — what is the investment? (2) The consensus view — what does the market believe? (3) The variant perception — how is your view different? (4) The trigger event — what will cause the market to adopt your view? Any pitch that cannot deliver all four in two minutes is not worth making.

Sol's Code of Honor

"Honest" = pays taxes but cuts every corner. "Honorable" = lives up to personal commitments no matter what. Loyalty to friends under stress is the sine qua non. The code of the streets, not bourgeois society.

What Didn't Fit

The Escape and Evasion Exercise

During army training at Fort Dix, Steinhardt was captured in a nighttime forest exercise, stripped naked, strapped to a chair with electrodes, and shocked repeatedly while soldiers shouted antisemitic slurs: "Another fucking Jew! They all have small cocks!" It was the only time in his life someone attacked him for being Jewish. The memory stayed indelible — not because the enemy was real, but because the hatred was. The episode connects to his lifelong meditation on Jewish identity and vulnerability.

The Hebrew-Speaking Goose

During a summit at Bedford with Israeli economic leaders including Finance Minister Beiga Shohat, Steinhardt claimed his Cereopsis hen spoke Hebrew. The goose ran over shaking its chartreuse beak and uttered guttural sounds. The minister was impressed. Of course, the goose would do the same for anyone speaking any language. Nothing came of the infrastructure summit, but people in Jerusalem reportedly talked about the goose for years.

The Feshbach Brothers and Scientology

The Feshbach brothers from California were superb short sellers who found "terminal" shorts — frauds, bankruptcy candidates, accounting disasters. They were also converts to Scientology. Steinhardt should have seen their best days were behind them when they offered him a ride on their Challenger Jet: "Rarely does a mix of overconfidence, science fiction, and new demonstrative wealth lead to continued success as a money manager."

Sir Hugh's Nautical Metaphors

Calvin Bullock's weekly newsletter was called The Ship's Log. Sir Hugh Bullock spoke of "rough seas," "steady courses," and "the captain at the helm." His model sailing ship sat in a glass case. The culture was genteel, conservative, and entirely male. The goal was "not so much to win the race as to get the ship to port with all hands safely on board" — the polar opposite of what Steinhardt would build.

The Goldman Sachs Account

On his literal first day of retirement, Steinhardt picked up the direct line to Goldman Sachs and dictated S&P futures orders. All accounts had been closed. His administrator Lisa Addeo: "Open one up! This is Michael Steinhardt, for God's sake. He's trading. That's what he does. Anyway, whatever orders he put in, you know he's good for the money." It captures perfectly the addiction that defined his life — trading since age thirteen, unable to stop even in retirement.

No Bull: My Life In and Out of Markets by Michael Steinhardt · John Wiley & Sons · 2001 · 276 pages

The memoir of one of history's greatest hedge fund managers, who averaged 30%+ annual returns over 28 years before retiring to devote his fortune to revitalizing American Jewish identity.

About the Author

Michael Steinhardt (born 1940) grew up in Bensonhurst, Brooklyn, graduated from the Wharton School at nineteen, and began his Wall Street career as a research analyst. In 1967, at twenty-six, he co-founded Steinhardt, Fine, Berkowitz & Company, which became one of the most successful hedge funds in history. Over twenty-eight years, his funds averaged more than 30 percent annual returns — transforming one dollar into $481 while the S&P 500 turned the same dollar into $19.

After retiring in 1995, Steinhardt became one of the most significant Jewish philanthropists of his generation. He co-founded birthright israel with Charles Bronfman, creating a program that has sent over 800,000 young Jews to Israel on free ten-day trips. He also established the Jewish Life Network, the Makor cultural center, and the Partnership for Excellence in Jewish Education. He remains an active private investor and serves on numerous boards.

Steinhardt's self-described atheism combined with passionate Jewish commitment makes him one of the most paradoxical figures in modern Jewish philanthropy. His intensity, honesty about his own failures, and refusal to sentimentalize his remarkable career distinguish this memoir from the typical Wall Street autobiography.

Why This Book Matters

No Bull stands apart from other hedge fund memoirs because Steinhardt is brutally honest about the psychological cost of extraordinary performance. Most Wall Street autobiographies emphasize the triumphs. Steinhardt dwells on the 1987 crash, the 1994 disaster, and the perpetual dissatisfaction that made thirty percent annual returns feel insufficient. The book makes visible the rarely discussed relationship between performance addiction and clinical depression that haunts many of the industry's most successful practitioners.

The concept of variant perception — developed here with more specificity than in any other investment book — remains one of the most useful frameworks for any active investor. Steinhardt's four-part analyst pitch (idea, consensus, variant view, trigger) is taught in hedge fund training programs decades later. And his candid account of how hubris after a winning streak leads to disaster (the 1994 European bond debacle) is a cautionary tale that never loses its relevance.

Beyond markets, the book is a surprisingly moving family story — the absent gambler father whose two impulsive gifts shaped an entire life, the mother whose unconditional love provided the security that ambition requires, and the late-life realization that making money, however skillfully, is not enough. Steinhardt's pivot from the markets to Jewish philanthropy — particularly birthright israel — gives the memoir a narrative arc that most financial autobiographies lack entirely.
