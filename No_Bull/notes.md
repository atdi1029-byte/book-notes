# No Bull: My Life In and Out of Markets — Michael Steinhardt
## Raw Notes

### Chapter 1: The Phone Call (Pages 10-18)

[MUST KNOW] N001 — Steinhardt's fiscal year ended September 30. Each October brought fresh anxiety — "How much have you made for me this year?" The addiction was in the process of pursuing success, not savoring it. He compared himself to Goethe's Faust, always striving toward new goals rather than enjoying past achievements.

[MUST KNOW] N002 — The defining bond trade of 1981: In the face of 16% long-term Treasury rates, 17% short-term rates, and universal bearishness (Henry Kaufman "Dr. Doom" at Salomon Brothers predicting runaway inflation, bonds called "certificates of confiscation"), Steinhardt began buying bonds in late spring 1981. He invested $50M of fund cash and borrowed $200M more to buy $250M of 10-year Treasuries — unprecedented leverage for a man whose entire career had been in stocks. Investors revolted: McKinsey & Company redeemed immediately ("You're an equity investor"), a Canadian investor threatened to sue. At one point, paper losses hit $10M. But when rates cracked in October 1981, the position made $40M profit. The fund finished up 97% for the year.

[SHOULD KNOW] N003 — Steinhardt managed ~$75M across two funds (Steinhardt Partners LP and SP International) in the early 1980s. Bond leverage was possible because, unlike stocks with Fed margin requirements, government bonds could be purchased with 95-99% borrowed money.

[SHOULD KNOW] N004 — His obsessive data gathering: constructed a personal "NYC taxi index" — tracking the ratio of available vs. occupied taxis as an economic indicator. Gathered masses of anecdotal evidence seeking signs of weakness. Waited anxiously each Friday for M1/M2 money supply numbers.

[MUST KNOW] N005 — The same afternoon he learned about the bond windfall, an FBI agent called to tell him his name was on a Black Panthers assassination list — "a list of successful capitalists." The juxtaposition stayed with him: professional fulfillment was fragile, success could bring its own dangers, and good fortune was never permanent.

[SHOULD KNOW] N006 — Steinhardt's later life purpose: creating a renaissance within the next generation of Jews. He does not believe in God but found this philanthropic pursuit offered "almost religious profundity" — more meaningful than making rich people richer.

### Chapter 2: Bensonhurst (Pages 19-28)

[MUST KNOW] N007 — Father Sol "Red" Steinhardt (AKA Red McGee): grew up in 1920s Brownsville, Brooklyn's toughest neighborhood. Dropped out of school at 12, worked manual labor, was a chronic gambler — horses, sports, crap games, card games (clobyosh). Parents divorced when Michael was less than 1 year old because Sol couldn't reliably bring home money. Mother Claire moved back to her parents' house.

[MUST KNOW] N008 — Sol became a "fence" — a handler and dealer of stolen jewelry on 47th Street. He was connected to organized crime figures. Despite being unreliable and often absent, Sol gave young Michael his first shares of stock — Philadelphia & Reading Railroad and a rubber company called Doehler-Jarvis — when Michael was about 13. This sparked Michael's lifelong fascination with stocks. Sol said: follow the stock prices in the newspaper every day.

[SHOULD KNOW] N009 — Mother Claire was the stable force. After divorce, she worked as a bookkeeper, then later at the post office. She was selfless and devoted. Michael grew up in Bensonhurst, a lower-middle-class Brooklyn neighborhood, living with maternal grandparents initially.

[SHOULD KNOW] N010 — Michael was intellectually precocious: entered first grade at 4.5 years old, was consistently the youngest and smallest in his class. Won spelling bees, was considered brilliant by teachers. But socially awkward — the youngest, smallest kid surrounded by bigger, tougher Brooklyn boys. This bred both insecurity and intense competitiveness.

[NICE TO KNOW] N011 — Grandparents were Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe. Grandfather Abe Dolleck was a taxi driver, then hat cleaner. Grandmother Bertha was the family disciplinarian. They lived in a modest apartment on Bay 28th Street.

[SHOULD KNOW] N012 — Michael started following stocks obsessively after receiving those first shares from his father around age 13. He would check prices daily in the newspaper, calculating gains and losses. The stock market became his connection to his largely absent father and a source of intellectual excitement.

### Chapter 3: The Wise Guy of Forty-Seventh Street (Pages 32-45)

[MUST KNOW] N013 — Sol became a fixture on Manhattan's Diamond District (47th Street) as a cash buyer of jewelry, known as "the jeweler to the mob." He knew Meyer Lansky and remained close to Lansky's partner Jimmy "Blue Eyes" Aiello until his death. They dined at Patsy's restaurant. When Joey Anastasia was shot at the Park Sheraton Hotel barbershop, police picked up Sol for questioning — they'd been gambling together the night before.

[MUST KNOW] N014 — Sol's code of ethics: "honest" meant paying taxes (grudgingly); "honorable" meant living up to personal commitments no matter what. Loyalty to friends under stress was the sine qua non. He had a pristine reputation for integrity in his world — the code was of the streets, not bourgeois society.

[SHOULD KNOW] N015 — The Shlomo Moussaieff story: Sol met Moussaieff, a struggling Israeli jeweler in London, left him a piece of jewelry from King Farouk's royal family to sell on commission. Moussaieff couldn't sell it. Sol took it back, later sold it in the US, then returned to London and handed Moussaieff $25,000 — his share of the profits. Moussaieff became world-renowned dealer in precious jewels. Sol also used Moussaieff as a "gambling brake" — depositing cash with him to prevent himself from gambling it away.

[MUST KNOW] N016 — Sol's Bar Mitzvah gift to Michael in 1953: 100 shares Penn Dixie Cement at $37 and 100 shares Columbia Gas System at $16 — worth over $5,000 total. This gift galvanized Michael's lifelong fascination with stocks. The fluctuating value captivated him: a $1 move in Penn Dixie was $100, versus his $4/week allowance.

[SHOULD KNOW] N017 — Sol took teenage Michael to the Copacabana nightclub; Sol was treated like royalty, known as "Red" or "Red McGee." Always ordered Dom Perignon. Scene was "right out of Goodfellas." Also took him to Giants games — but once punched Michael in the shoulder when the Giants won and Sol had bet on Cincinnati. "Do you think Don Mueller is going to pay your bills?"

[SHOULD KNOW] N018 — Michael's early stock market education: visited Bache & Company brokerage office in downtown Brooklyn after school, watched ticker tape. Then graduated to Merrill Lynch's educational office in Grand Central Station, opened his first brokerage account. Fell for "bucket shop" scams like SwanFinch Oil, losing thousands. "In the stock market, almost everyone pays a price for even an introductory education."

[MUST KNOW] N019 — "I have no doubt that I inherited this love of the rush from my father... Psychologically, emotionally, and perhaps even intellectually, gambling and the manner in which I then invested may not have been importantly different." But the stock market, unlike games of chance, "is not random" — developing expertise does result in better performance. His father's gift of stocks "played as significant a role in the shaping of my life as anything else."

### Chapter 4: The Ivy League, the Army, and the Street (Pages 48-63)

[MUST KNOW] N020 — Sol redirected Michael from free City College to Wharton. At dinner at the Sacred Cow Steakhouse, Sol told him about the school in Philadelphia where "wealthy Jewish boys" graduated — he knew from reading wedding announcements in the Sunday NYT Society Section. Sol offered to pay. Michael applied last-minute and was accepted.

[SHOULD KNOW] N021 — At Wharton, the most valuable class was Philosophy of Statistics taught by Dr. Francis Brown — learning to "frame problems quantitatively so as to arrive at unexpected probability distributions." Combined with sociology major, this gave him unique analytical framework. He condensed to 3 years (couldn't afford 4) and majored in sociology because it had no prerequisites chain.

[SHOULD KNOW] N022 — Sol arrested in 1958 by DA Frank Hogan as "the biggest jewel fence in America." Six-week trial was a "low-budget circus." Found guilty on two felony counts, 5-10 years consecutive. Won appeal on prosecutorial misconduct (ADA Burton Roberts, later rumored model for the judge in Bonfire of the Vanities). Released on time served (~2 years) accepting the felony charge.

[SHOULD KNOW] N023 — First job at Calvin Bullock, traditional white-shoe mutual fund. Sir Hugh Bullock's speech at annual dinner: "This is a firm of Christian gentlemen, because like attracts like." The next year, after spotting Michael, he paused and said only "...of gentlemen, because like attracts like." Michael: "It was the highlight of my first job on Wall Street."

[SHOULD KNOW] N024 — Army "Escape and Evasion" training at Fort Dix: Michael was captured, stripped naked, strapped to a chair with electrodes, and shocked repeatedly while soldiers shouted antisemitic slurs. "Another fucking Jew! You know, they all have small cocks!" The only time in his life he was "attacked" for being Jewish.

### Chapter 5: The Hottest Analyst on Wall Street (Pages 64-75)

[SHOULD KNOW] N025 — Fired from Financial World magazine for not answering subscriber letters (hid them in a drawer). Boss found the pile while Michael was home sick. Fired at 22, then rehired a week later when replacement turned out to be a drunk. Lesson learned: "I had been given a second chance at having a career."

[MUST KNOW] N026 — At Loeb Rhoades as research analyst, age 24. Covered cyclical industries. Predicted GM's quarterly earnings per share to the penny — rare feat in mid-1960s. Then met Charlie Bluhdorn, "the mad Austrian" running Gulf and Western. Wrote bullish report; stock tripled in three months. Believes he was first to introduce the word "synergy" in explaining conglomerate investing. "For a time, it seemed I was the hottest analyst on Wall Street."

[SHOULD KNOW] N027 — The conglomerate era: companies like Gulf and Western, Leasco (Saul Steinberg at age 22, tried to buy Chemical Bank), Ling-Temco-Vought. Many had "creative accounting" beyond Wall Street analysts' grasp. Loeb Rhoades initially avoided them as "chewing gum" companies, then reversed after Michael's success.

[MUST KNOW] N028 — First met Alan Greenspan, then a consultant to DLJ: "I recall being disappointed that I had learned little from listening to him, and that what he said was mostly an extrapolation of the obvious... There were certainly no clear signs back then that he would one day rule world financial markets."

[MUST KNOW] N029 — OXY short disaster: Shorted Occidental Petroleum run by Armand Hammer. Stock doubled on rumors of Libyan oil discovery rivaling Saudi Arabia. "Whenever you lose on the short side, it is that much more frightening because the loss is theoretically without limit." So morose he and partner Howard threatened to hang friend Eric Sheinberg out the window by his thumbs.

### Chapter 6: Steinhardt, Fine, Berkowitz & Company (Pages 78-88)

[MUST KNOW] N030 — Six-Day War (June 1967) nearly derailed the launch. At a mass rally outside the UN, Rabbi Shlomo Carlebach sang "El Malei Rachamim" (prayer for the dead) and broke down weeping — "a tableau of pain and compassion that I will carry with me for the rest of my life." Michael tried to fly to Israel to volunteer to fight but all flights were booked.

[MUST KNOW] N031 — Steinhardt, Fine, Berkowitz & Company opened July 10, 1967 with 8 employees and $7.7M in capital. The name order was decided by coin flip (Steinhardt won). Lawyer Paul Roth worried it "sounded more like a Jewish delicatessen than a money management firm." 31% gain in first 3-month fiscal year, 99% in first full year (vs. S&P 6.5% and 9.3%). By end of fiscal 1969, nearly $30M in capital. All three became millionaires in their late twenties.

[MUST KNOW] N032 — The Go-Go years: story stocks like Maine Sugar, National Student Marketing (100x earnings), King Resources. "I learned that a story, whatever its veracity, could be of value, particularly if it were new to the market and had an open-ended quality that provoked one's imagination." They owned them all but had "an inner tension" — viewing the portfolio fresh every day. "We were the greater fools... However, we sold."

[SHOULD KNOW] N033 — Block trading emerged. Key players: Jay Perry at Salomon Brothers, Bob Mnuchin at Goldman Sachs, Willy Weinstein at Oppenheimer. Michael bought 700,000 shares of Penn Central at 7, sold at 7¾ eight minutes later for $500K+ profit. "Those opportunities are gone now."

[MUST KNOW] N034 — Hedge fund philosophy: NOT pure hedging to eliminate market risk (Jones model). They wanted flexibility to play long and short sides, betting on direction AND stock selection. Key principles: (1) invest virtually all personal wealth alongside clients, (2) performance-based fees (1% management + 20% of profits), (3) absolute returns regardless of market direction.

### Chapter 7: Judy (Pages 90-100)

[SHOULD KNOW] N035 — Met Judy through a series of coincidences involving Phil David Fine, a Boston lawyer connected to the Kennedys. Judy had taken the room vacated by Denise Eisenberg, who left to marry Marc Rich (later one of world's most famous fugitives). First encountered Judy in his car pool — she sat silently in black sunglasses, resembling Audrey Hepburn.

[NICE TO KNOW] N036 — Judy overheard stock tips in the car pool (Maine Sugar, Colorado Milling), called her father, who bought them and made enough to buy her first fur coat.

[SHOULD KNOW] N037 — Wedding April 28, 1968. Sol wanted to buy the champagne (only drank Dom Perignon). Judy's mother refused. Sol then asked the bandleader to play Jewish music, was turned down even when offering $100 bill. Judy's mother: "I'm in charge of this wedding, Mr. Steinhardt, and there will be no Jewish music." One of few people to beat Sol in a confrontation.

[SHOULD KNOW] N038 — Judy's commercial instinct: urged Michael to buy The Gap stock when she kept shopping there for kids' clothes. He didn't buy. "She was absolutely right. To my regret, I bought no shares." Her judgment of people far surpassed his — he was "easily finagled by charm" while Judy was discerning.

[NICE TO KNOW] N039 — Judy's hydroponics venture: grew strawberries, lettuce, basil. Created "Herbs Alive" brand with Milton Glaser's marketing help — herbs sold living with roots attached. Business grew but profits didn't; they quit.

### Chapter 8: Variant Perception (Pages 103-114)

[MUST KNOW] N040 — Variant perception defined: "holding a well-founded view that was meaningfully different from market consensus." The only analytic tool that mattered was "an intellectually advantaged disparate view." This included knowing more AND perceiving the situation better than others. Understanding market expectations was at least as important as fundamental knowledge. "The process by which a disparate perception, when correct, became consensus would almost inevitably lead to meaningful profit."

[MUST KNOW] N041 — The Nifty Fifty short (1972-1974): Shorted the "one-decision" glamour stocks — Avon, Xerox, Polaroid, McDonald's, Eastman Kodak, GE, J&J, Coca-Cola — all "the best companies in America." The only overriding basis: price. "Who were we to predict when this overvaluation phenomenon would end?" But with "fits and starts, with many sleepless nights," they stayed the course. Tony Cilluffo's "unswerving vision" proved critical. In 1973, Dow was flat, they were up 15%. In fiscal 1974, market down 38%, they were up 34%. Most institutional managers lost 50%; some mutual fund "stars" lost 80-90%.

[MUST KNOW] N042 — Picked the 1974 bottom "nearly perfectly." Switched net exposure from minus 55% to plus 35% in one month. Bought a broad basket of single-digit stocks trading at 75-80% of book value, 7x earnings: Howard Johnson, Zapata, Bath Industries, Suburban Propane, Tropicana. "Bought them right and, as usual, we sold them early." Up about 66% that year.

[SHOULD KNOW] N043 — Tony Cilluffo (hired from IOS/Bernie Cornfeld): smoked 3 packs/day, had ritual lunch habits tied to fund performance — same food as long as they were making money, changed on a losing day. Had "courage to make market calls in extremis" — would predict a stock at 80 would trade at 10 within a year. "More than anyone I have met, Tony had the courage."

[MUST KNOW] N044 — "Just as outright euphoria is often a sign of a market top, fear is, for sure, a sign of a market bottom. Time and time again, in every market cycle I have witnessed, the extremes of emotion always appear, even among experienced investors. When the world wants to buy only Treasury bills, you can almost close your eyes and get long stocks."

[SHOULD KNOW] N045 — Equity Funding near-disaster: Bought 100,000 shares at $17.50 of what turned out to be a massive fraud (bogus insurance policies). Dan Dorfman warned cryptically: "Not all the pretty girls are as pretty as they look." Stock opened at $6 two weeks later. Founder Stanley Goldblum convicted. Analyst Ray Dirks cleared by Supreme Court after SEC prosecution.

[SHOULD KNOW] N046 — The "Hmmm" trade: Sold large STP block, then met Studebaker CEO Ruttenberg at a Kennedy cocktail party. When told about the sale, Ruttenberg said only "Hmmm." Next morning, Steinhardt bought shares in all Studebaker subsidiaries. Trading halted; Studebaker announced buyback of all subsidiaries at premium. SEC subpoenaed him. "Would you place orders simply based on a 'Hmmm'?" He answered: "Absolutely; instincts are often all one has."

### Chapter 9: There and Back (Pages 115-125)

[MUST KNOW] N047 — Sabbatical year (1978-1979): Left Wall Street at age 38, net worth $7M+. "In my overall investment career, I had accomplished most of what I had set out to do. For me, the learning curve had flattened." Viewed professions on a "vertical scale of values" — doctor > accountant; heroes were Schweitzer, Einstein, Ben-Gurion. He was "making rich people, including myself, richer — certainly not a sin, but not a qualification for sainthood." Tried yoga, piano, tennis, Torah study. Found nothing as intellectually provocative as the market. Returned October 1, 1979.

[SHOULD KNOW] N048 — Health problems driven by trading intensity: chain-smoker, 220 lbs, tried every diet/hypnosis/acupuncture/balloon-in-stomach. Had ritual of buying quarter pounds of macadamias, cashews, pistachios, and pecans from the Treat Boutique on 86th Street after bad days — "an opiate" that reduced anxiety. "Was I chemically addicted to fats? I think so."

[SHOULD KNOW] N049 — Howard Berkowitz left to start HPB Associates upon Steinhardt's return. Firm renamed Steinhardt Partners. New goal: build a hedge fund larger than any that had previously existed. Raised institutional money — Larry Tisch committed Loews/Lorillard pension funds and NYU endowment. Then The Common Fund, which managed money for school endowments, invested after George Keane was intrigued by John Levin's pitch. Eventually attracted World Bank, Carnegie Corporation, Yale, Duke, University of Minnesota. Institutional Partners LP grew to over $1 billion.

[NICE TO KNOW] N050 — After presenting to Yale's investment committee, President Bart Giamatti remarked: "I had no idea we had Hamlet running our money."

### Chapter 10: Menageries and Movies (Pages 144-157)

[SHOULD KNOW] N051 — Bedford estate: 52 acres with stream, menagerie of exotic animals (zebras, kangaroos, capybara, lemurs, cranes, camels), extensive orchards (70-80 apple varieties), 500+ daylilies, Japanese maples, winery. Personal relationship with llama named Angel who only lets him kiss it. Bought the Falklands' Grand and Steeple Jason Islands (world's largest albatross nesting area) — gifted them to Wildlife Conservation Society.

[SHOULD KNOW] N052 — Art collecting with Judy: Klee, Cezanne, Schiele, Picasso, Matisse, Pollock, de Kooning, Goya, Seurat. Gifted a Rembrandt to Israel ("Israel's first Rembrandt"). Also collected Judaica including tzedakah boxes (a third proved inauthentic). The gold phiale fiasco: bought a Greek gold ceremonial plate for $1M+ after Met museum authenticated it, then US Customs confiscated it on behalf of Italy. Lost court battles and Supreme Court declined to hear it — cost another $1M in legal fees. "In stock market terms, I have lost 100 percent of my investment times two."

[NICE TO KNOW] N053 — Movie investments: financed films with Amos Kollek (Teddy Kollek's son). Goodbye, New York was a hit. Double Edge starred Faye Dunaway, lost every penny of $1.7M. Cameo appearances: soliciting a prostitute in Highstakes, ticket-taker in Forever Lulu. Hotel Terminus (Klaus Barbie documentary) won Academy Award for Best Foreign Documentary. "I must confess to a continuing love of movie making and to an absolute conviction that I will never make a penny at it."

### Chapter 11: The Crash of 1987 (Pages 158-168)

[MUST KNOW] N054 — In April 1987, six months before the crash, wrote prescient letter warning of extended P/E ratios, low dividend yields, program trading risks, potential 50-100 point daily Dow moves. "Prescient, yet I would not benefit from my own advice." By summer, he was net long again — temptation won. "The immediacy of opportunity — the attraction of individual stocks — precluded concerns about overvaluation." Funds were up ~35% by end of August.

[MUST KNOW] N055 — Black Monday October 19, 1987: Dow down 508 points, 22% decline, worst day since Great Depression. Steinhardt bought S&P futures on the way down ("Buy 10 S&Ps every 10 ticks"), market briefly rallied from down 250 to down 100, then collapsed to down 508. Lost nearly the entire year's profit — from up 45% in September to up only 4% by year-end. "I took the crash personally... My confidence was shaken. I felt alone."

[SHOULD KNOW] N056 — Convertible arbitrage portfolio manager had mismarked bonds — what showed as up 10% for October was actually down 30%. Only monthly downward P&L revision in firm history. When the manager said "All I want to do is kill myself," Steinhardt replied: "Can I watch?"

[MUST KNOW] N057 — The crash was caused by market structure failure, not economics: portfolio insurance and program trading created unprecedented selling cascades. "Ironically, the one time when investors most needed their portfolio insurance to limit losses, it failed them miserably." No recession followed. "In the end, few economic lessons were to be gleaned from the Crash of 1987."

[SHOULD KNOW] N058 — The Brooklyn Botanical Gardens gift: feeling depressed after the crash, visited the Gardens on a snowy day and committed $3M — then the largest gift ever to a Brooklyn institution. "I needed to do something that would make me feel good about myself again. This gift was the perfect antidote." Steinhardt Conservatory dedicated one year later on the anniversary of Black Monday.

[MUST KNOW] N059 — Came back with a vengeance: 1988 up 48%, 1989 and 1990 each up 26%. Then 1991-1993 approximately 60% per year. "Retrospectively, I have a far keener memory of the painful moments of 1987 than I do of the next six years of glorious success."

### Chapter 12: The Steinhardt Style (Pages 169-188)

[MUST KNOW] N060 — "Instinct and intuition": Won a daily market direction bet 22 days in a row at Loeb Rhoades in 1965. "This win was not random. At the same time, it was not a reflection of analysis, intelligence, or methodology." Over decades, making "cumulatively more judgments, and more investment decisions based on the same kinds of data, than almost anyone else. This process unconsciously leads to a sharpening, a fine tuning... one develops 'good instincts.'"

[MUST KNOW] N061 — Variant perception framework for analysts: Tell me in two minutes: (1) the idea; (2) the consensus view; (3) your variant perception; (4) a trigger event. If no variant perception existed — "solid growth recommendations within consensus" — he generally had no interest.

[MUST KNOW] N062 — Trading philosophy: "The quick and the dead" — if you don't respond fast enough to change, you lose. Sold Iowa Beef Processing at $29 while analyst was at lunch (analyst had bought at $24). Four months later, an $80 tender offer was made. Sold 3.5M shares of Bank of New York while analyst was at the bank's offices praising the stock. Would sometimes liquidate the entire portfolio in one trade with Goldman Sachs or Salomon Brothers and "start all over again" with a clean slate.

[SHOULD KNOW] N063 — Management style: "Two Michael Steinhardts" — charming 5-to-9 personality vs. ruthless 9-to-5. Employees used terms like "battered children," "mental abuse," "random violence." A psychiatrist observed the firm for months; when he tried to intervene during a tirade, Steinhardt told him to get the "@!%&$" out. "Many of my employees either did not make the grade or could not stand the pressure... The joke was: 'How much time did you do at Steinhardt?'"

[MUST KNOW] N064 — IBM trade (mid-1993): Bought when Wall Street consensus was negative, stock out of favor since 1987 crash. Bought 7 million shares near the bottom, intended to buy 20 million. Made about 30% in six months, then sold much too soon. "Once I am proved right in the market, the bottom line is of less interest to me. I am ready for the next idea." Warren Buffett: "If you are not willing to own a stock for 10 years, do not even think about owning it for 10 minutes." Steinhardt: "I have never owned a stock for 10 years, but I have had the unique and profitable experience of owning some very good companies for 10 minutes."

[SHOULD KNOW] N065 — CUC International short: Analyst Herb Chen concluded CUC was "cooking the books." Shorted from 1989, lost $50M over three years as the stock kept grinding higher. After they covered, CUC merged with HFS → Cendant, then massive accounting fraud revealed — $500M fictitious profits, $19B investor losses. "The largest accounting fraud ever... No solace to us."

[SHOULD KNOW] N066 — "Four yards up the middle in a cloud of dust" — his investment style. Surprisingly few "home runs." Gains earned the hard way through constant trading. Gross return over 30% per year for 28 years. Losing months were less than 5% of all months.

### Chapter 13: Dabbling in Politics (Pages 190-197)

[SHOULD KNOW] N067 — Involvement with the Democratic Leadership Council (DLC). Largest financial contributor (~$250K/year for nearly 10 years), became legal chairman and chairman of the Progressive Policy Institute think tank. DLC ideas: National Service, balanced budget, earned income tax credit, charter schools, NAFTA, welfare reform, reinventing government. Met Bill Clinton when Al From brought him to chair the DLC. "Bill Clinton is the best political animal I've ever met."

[SHOULD KNOW] N068 — Clinton's presidency disappointed the DLC. Early appointments were left-leaning, health care fiasco, gays-in-military. Steinhardt resigned from DLC after seeing Clinton as guest of honor at 1995 convention. "I am not constitutionally cut out for the political world. Philosophically, I see things often in black and white, whereas politics deals in grays." But DLC ideas — moderate Democrat, centrist Democrat, "the third way" — were eventually absorbed by Clinton's second term.

[NICE TO KNOW] N069 — At dinner with Al From during the 1992 campaign, learned about Gennifer Flowers story breaking. Asked From if it was true. From smiled: "Michael, Bill has always been a man of great appetites."

### Chapter 14: The Worst Year of My Life (Pages 199-210)

[MUST KNOW] N070 — 1994: the worst year. Three consecutive 60% years had bred hubris. Capital base swelled to nearly $5B. Expanded into global macro — European bonds (French OATs, German BUNDs, Italian BTPs, Spanish BONOs), emerging markets, currencies. Foreign bond portfolio totaled $30B+. Each basis point move = $10M gain or loss. Daily P&L was 30 pages long, "read like the German code from World War II."

[MUST KNOW] N071 — February 4, 1994: Fed raised rates for first time in 5 years. European bonds plummeted as liquidity dried up. Too many hedge funds had made the same bets — the trade was "crowded." Their own brokers (Bankers Trust, Merrill, JP Morgan, Goldman) had the same positions and competed to sell. "Markets can remain irrational longer than you can remain solvent" (Keynes). Finished liquidating by end of March, down 30%. "If I was a victim of anything, it was of hubris."

[MUST KNOW] N072 — Simultaneously, the Treasury note scandal settled. SEC/Justice Dept accused Salomon Brothers, Steinhardt, and Caxton of colluding to corner the two-year Treasury note market in 1991 (owned 94% of the notes). Settled without admission of guilt — paid fines and fees totaling $70M+. Steinhardt paid 75% personally. The bond bet from 1990-1993 had made $600M+ for investors, but the scandal "left me wounded."

[MUST KNOW] N073 — Year ended down 31% — the only meaningful loss year in his career (only other loss years were 1969 and 1972, each -1.5%). "I could not avoid feeling as if my very worth as a human being depended on my continually making money. What was I worth when I lost?"

### Chapter 15: "Steinhardt Quits!" (Pages 213-220)

[MUST KNOW] N074 — Retired October 11, 1995, closing all Steinhardt funds. "The highs from success were not quite as high as in the past, and the lows seemed much lower." Had to recover from 1994 first — couldn't leave after the only bad year. Funds ended 1995 up 26%. Returned most money by year-end. $1 invested in 1967 would have been worth $481 at closing, versus $19 in the S&P 500. One early investor turned $500K into $100M over 28 years.

[MUST KNOW] N075 — Washington Post quote on investing: "The hardest thing over the years has been having the courage to go against the dominant wisdom of the time, to have a view that is at variance with the present consensus and bet that view... if you take a variant point of view, you will always be bombarded for some period of time by the conventional wisdom as expressed by the market."

[SHOULD KNOW] N076 — On his first day in "retirement," sat at his desk and placed S&P futures orders through Goldman Sachs — but all accounts had been closed. Lisa Addeo told Goldman: "Open one up! This is Michael Steinhardt, for God's sake. He's trading. That's what he does."

### Chapter 16: The Death of My Father (Pages 223-231)

[SHOULD KNOW] N077 — Sol moved to Las Vegas at 70, continued gambling daily. Had a "love/hate relationship with gambling." Net losses in the millions. Developed a late-life strategy: make only three large bets per visit, walk away on losses (rarely had the discipline). Multiple health crises in his 80s: lymphoma, macular degeneration (near blindness), deafness, shingles, prostate cancer. "The golden years, what golden years? It's all bullshit."

[MUST KNOW] N078 — Sol's death (August 1999) and Michael's eulogy: "If one can be both, my father was 'one of a kind' and 'last of his kind.' His was the chronicle of the Jewish experience in the twentieth century in America." Broke down weeping before speaking. "How I had longed for him to be the father I wanted him to be." Even at the end, could never tell Sol "I love you." The last thing Sol asked for: "Get the baby, baby Jacob, a Jewish star. A Jewish star with diamonds. That was my father, a Jewish star with diamonds."

[SHOULD KNOW] N079 — Mother Claire: moved to Hebrew Home for the Aged in Riverdale after Alzheimer's diagnosis. She recognized Michael and Judy but spoke "disjointedly." Would ask with clarity: "When am I going home?"

### Chapter 17: Two Rivers (Pages 234-251)

[MUST KNOW] N080 — "Two rivers" metaphor: the river of Judaism (culture, values, peoplehood) and the river of secular America (openness, mobility, prosperity). "For the first 50-plus years of my life, I too traveled, almost exclusively, along the secular river. Now I work, almost exclusively, on strengthening the flow of the river of my heritage." Created the Jewish Life Network (1994) to revitalize Jewish identity for non-Orthodox Jews living secular lives.

[MUST KNOW] N081 — "birthright israel" (1999): conceived on a stone wall outside the Israel Museum in Jerusalem with Charles Bronfman. Vision: every Jew aged 18-26 gets a free 10-day trip to Israel as a rite of passage. Each put up $5M seed money, then $9M each for first trips. Israeli PM Netanyahu committed $20M/year, UJA/Federations $20M/year, private donors $20M/year. First trips January 2000 — 6,000 students from around the world. "I feel that I have made all of this money, but I have changed nothing. Together, we can make a difference."

[SHOULD KNOW] N082 — Steinhardt's speech at the birthright gala in Jerusalem, a takeoff on the Gettysburg Address: "Four millennia ago, our ancestors brought forth on this earth a new people... We must promise that this people, in partnership with the living and the dead, with God and humanity, shall have a new birth of freedom." 5,000 kids gave a standing ovation. A young man came up crying: "I never believed I could experience something like this in my life."

[SHOULD KNOW] N083 — Makor center on West 67th Street in Manhattan: cultural center for Jewish professionals in their 20s-30s. Open to all (no "Jews only" restriction). Mailing list exceeded 25,000. Gifted to 92nd Street YMHA in 2001.

[SHOULD KNOW] N084 — Buckingham Palace anecdote: at an Israel Philharmonic event, tried to trade identities with "Mr. Chichester" — turned out to be the Earl of Chichester. "Hatikva" (Israeli national anthem) played in Buckingham Palace, perhaps for the first time.

[MUST KNOW] N085 — "I hope that my career as a money manager will not dominate my epitaph. The epitaph I would rather have is that I helped reinvigorate the non-Orthodox Jewish community in America."
