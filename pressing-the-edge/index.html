Pressing the Edge

1. The Three Stages, and Which Part of the P&L Each One Controls

StageSkillWhat it governs Where it gets you
1Managing risk The left tail — how bad a bad trade gets Break-even
2Managing profit The middle — how much of an ordinary winner you keepConsistently profitable
3Exploiting profit The right tail — how big a great trade becomesOutsized returns

Stage 1 is defensive: fixed stops, fixed risk per trade, no blow-ups. It stops the bleeding but adds nothing. Stage 2 is where most profitable traders live: trailing stops, scaling out, knowing when a winner is done. Stage 3 is the rare one, because it asks you to do something that feels wrong — put profit you have already secured back to work while the trade is still open.

Where each stage acts on the distribution of trade outcomes. Most trades are small expenses; the account is made in the right tail.

Why the third stage is the whole game at a low win rate

Matt wins roughly 20–30% of his trades. Expectancy per trade is p × avgWin − (1 − p) × avgLoss. At a 25% win rate with an average losing trade of 0.7R (a mix of scratches and full stops):

Average winnerExpectancy per trade
2R−0.03R
3R+0.23R
5R+0.73R
8R+1.48R

Capped at 2R, a 25%-win-rate trader is break-even no matter how clean the risk management is. Nothing about stages 1 and 2 changes the win rate; the only lever left is the size of the average winner, and pyramiding is how a 3R move becomes an 8R trade.

2. What “Exploiting Profit” Means

Exploiting profit is converting a winning trade’s open profit into additional position, so the payoff grows faster than the move itself. It is not “letting it run” (that is passive) and it is not “adding risk” (that is stage zero).

The mental shift Matt describes: the instant a trade starts working, the questions he asks are where do I add and where do I take profit — together, not in sequence. They are the same question, because profit that is taken, or locked by a raised stop, is the budget that pays for the add.

Three things you can do with open profit:

  1. Leave it open — let the trade run. Passive.
  2. Lock it — raise the stop, or bank a slice. This is stage 2.
  3. Spend it — use the locked amount to fund a larger position. This is stage 3, and it is only possible after step 2 has created a budget to spend.

3. The Mechanics: Adding Size Without Adding Risk

Definitions. E is the entry price of a unit, S the current stop (shared by all units, never lowered), Q the size of a unit, and R the dollar risk you accepted on the first unit.

Risk-at-stop is what the whole position is worth if the stop fills: Σ Qi × (S − Ei). Units entered below the stop contribute a locked gain; units entered above it contribute risk.

The rule. Before any add, raise the stop. Then size the add so that risk-at-stop for the whole position stays within budget:

Q_add ≤ (B + L) / (E_add − S) L = profit locked on existing units at the new stop = Σ Q_i × (S − E_i) B = the risk you are willing to carry at the stop

Two variants of B:

  • Constant-risk pyramid — B = R. Total risk at the stop never exceeds the risk on the original trade.
  • Zero-risk pyramid — B = 0. Adds are funded entirely by locked profit; a stop-out returns you to flat.

Worked example

$100k account, 1% risk = $1,000 per trade. Entry 100, stop 96, so 250 shares. Rule: constant-risk, but no single add larger than two initial units.

The worked example as a price path. The stop steps up before each add; by leg 2 the average entry has climbed to meet it.

Leg 1. The stop at 102 locks $500 on unit 1. Budget = $1,000 + $500 = $1,500. Risk on a new share with entry 108 and stop 102 is $6, so up to 250 shares fit. The position doubles; risk is unchanged.

Leg 2. The stop at 110 locks $2,500 on unit 1 and $500 on unit 2. Budget = $4,000, which would allow 666 shares; the two-unit cap says 500. Risk at stop is now zero — 1,000 shares, four times the original position, with nothing at risk if the stop fills at 110.

The payoff. If price reaches 130: no adds = $7,500 (7.5R). With adds = $20,000 (20R). Same entry, same stop discipline, same move — 2.7× the result.

The short side. Everything here is written long. A short pyramid uses the same arithmetic with the signs flipped, with two asymmetries: the loss on a short is unbounded, and squeezes move faster than declines. Late adds on the short side are more dangerous for it, and the noise floor in the next section should be wider.

4. What the Add Actually Costs

Look at leg 2 again. Before the add, the stop at 110 guaranteed $3,000. After it, the stop at 110 guarantees $0. The add spent $3,000 of locked profit to buy 500 shares of continuation exposure — which pays an extra $7,000 if price reaches 130, and more beyond.

What each position size pays at every possible exit price, as of the leg-2 add. The pyramid gives up $3,000 at the stop to buy the steepest line.

So the add is a bet: stake 3R of secured profit to win 7R if the trend continues. As a rough lens, that bet needs continuation odds above about 30% to be worth taking. The honest framing of “zero added risk” is not “free” — it is “I have chosen to spend my cushion on optionality.”

This has a consequence people miss: pyramiding is a bet on continuation. It only has positive expectancy in instruments and regimes where moves persist. In a mean-reverting market it systematically buys tops and sells bottoms with your largest size, and every pyramid resolves to a scratch.

The inverted pyramid

Notice the average entry after leg 2 is 110 — exactly the stop. Adding big late pulls the position’s break-even up toward the current price, so an ordinary pullback stops out the entire position at flat, after you had been up $6,000 open. The classical advice (Livermore, the Turtles) — largest unit first, each add smaller — exists to keep the average entry low so the pyramid survives normal noise. Matt’s version sizes up harder, funded by the cushion. The reconciliation:

  • Size the add from the budget (Section 3), and
  • Check that the stop sits beyond normal noise. If the position’s break-even is within roughly 1.5–2 ATR of price, you have built something a random day will knock over. Either add less or wait for a bigger cushion.

5. Where to Add

The trigger has to be mechanical. Common designs:

  • Fixed increments. Add every k × ATR (or every 1R) of favorable movement. The Turtle version: one unit at entry, one more every ½N, maximum four units, stop trailed 2N behind the most recent add.
  • Structural. Add when the trend re-proves itself — a breakout from a consolidation inside the trend, a new high after a higher low, a close beyond the prior swing.
  • On strength vs. on pullback. Adding on strength (new highs) keeps you in sync with the trend, and the previous unit is always in profit when you add. Adding on pullbacks gets a better price but means adding while the position is moving against you.

One rule separates a pullback add from averaging down: never add below the entry of your last unit. Above it is a pyramid; below it is averaging down with a nicer name.

Hard caps: a maximum number of units, and an add only fires if the previous unit is in profit and the original trend condition still holds.

Trend age

The odds on the fourth add are worse than on the first. The further a move has run without consolidating, the nearer the reversal, and late adds are the ones that build the inverted pyramid of Section 4. The max-unit cap handles this bluntly. The direct rule: no adds once price is more than N ATR from the base — the entry, or the moving average the trend is measured against — or once the move has run a set distance without a rest. Add on the resumption after a consolidation, not on the extension.

6. Where to Take Profit

The other half of the question. Taking profit is not the opposite of adding — it is what funds it.

  • A raised stop locks unrealized profit. It holds only if the stop fills where you placed it. A gap or a flash move through it turns “zero risk” into real risk, and the bigger the final position, the bigger that exposure.
  • A partial exit banks realized profit. That cushion cannot be taken back. Realized dollars are harder currency for funding adds than a trailed stop.

A workable structure: bank a fixed slice of the first unit at a fixed multiple (say a third at 2R), trail the rest, and treat the banked slice plus the locked amount as the add budget. The scale-out schedule and the add schedule are one plan written from both ends.

Scheduled events. Earnings, a central-bank decision, a major data release — these are where the gap in the first bullet lives. The standard rule: reduce to the core unit before the event and re-add on the trigger after it. A pyramid held through a binary event is not a zero-risk position; it is a large bet on the print.

7. Unwinding the Pyramid

The trailing stop in Section 3 treats the pyramid as one position: everything exits at 110. That is the simplest design, and it is a cliff. The units are not equal, though. At 116, unit 1 has 16 points of cushion, unit 2 has 8, unit 3 has none. The last unit in has the worst price and the thinnest cushion, so it is the natural first unit out.

The pyramid at 116, newest unit on top. Peeling from the top removes the thinnest cushion first.

Last in, first out. Set a weakness trigger tighter than the position stop — a close below the prior swing low, or a give-back of one ATR from the high — and peel the newest unit on the first trigger, the next-newest on the second, leaving the original unit on the wide trailing stop. One cliff becomes a staircase.

In the example: price fails at 116 and closes at 113. LIFO sells unit 3 (500 @ 116) at 113 for −$1,500. The remaining 500 shares have an average of 104 and the stop at 110, so the worst case for the trade is now +$3,000 − $1,500 = +$1,500, against $0 if everything rode down to 110. If the trend resumes, unit 3 goes back on at the next trigger; the peel cost an expense, not the trade.

The trade-off. LIFO cuts the largest exposure early, so it lowers give-back, but it also cuts exposure right where continuation would have paid most, and each peel-and-re-add is another expense. In a trend with routine 1-ATR pullbacks, a 1-ATR trigger peels a unit on every one of them. Set the trigger wider than the instrument’s typical pullback inside a trend, and tighter than the position stop.

Two designs, then: a single position-level stop (simple, cliff-edged, Turtle style), or staggered unit-level stops that produce LIFO automatically — each unit’s stop sits m ATR below its own entry, so the newest unit always has the tightest one. Pick one and write it down. The failure mode is deciding during the pullback.

8. The Swing at Full Size

At 250 shares a $3 move was $750 — three-quarters of R, an ordinary day. At 1,000 shares the same $3 is $3,000: three R, in an afternoon, in a position that was up $6,000 an hour earlier. Nothing about the trade has changed. The size has, and with it the meaning of ordinary noise.

This is the part of stage 3 that is rare. The math of adding fits in a spreadsheet; sitting through a normal pullback with four times your usual size does not. Traders who get this far mostly fail by flinching: they cut the pyramid on a routine dip, book a fraction of what the rule would have delivered, and conclude that pyramiding does not work for them.

Size the pyramid to the swing you can hold. Before the add, compute the one-ATR swing at the new size (position × ATR) and express it in R. If a one-ATR day at full size is a number you would not sit through, the pyramid is bigger than your nerves, whatever risk-at-stop says. Cap it there.

Rules that help:

  • Decide the exit design before the add (Section 7), never during the pullback.
  • Watch the stop level, not the P&L. The stop is the decision; the P&L is noise about the decision.
  • Count in R, not dollars. $3,000 is a number that makes people act; 3R on a trade whose worst case is zero is not.
  • Reduce screen time at full size. A pit trader saw one price at a time; a screen shows the open profit ticking, which is the flinch made visible.
  • Do not re-ask the entry question. “Would I put this position on here, at this price?” always answers no at full size after a run. The only question is whether an exit rule has fired.

9. Scratch Stop vs. Break-Even Stop

Matt draws this distinction, and pyramids make it matter.

  • Scratch stop — a unit’s stop moved to that unit’s own entry. If hit, that unit exits flat, less commission and slippage: an expense, not a loss.
  • Break-even stop — a stop placed so the whole trade exits at zero. With locked or banked profit as a buffer, the remaining position’s stop can sit below scratch and the trade as a whole is still flat.

For a single unit these are the same price. For a pyramid they diverge, and confusing them is how traders lose money on a trade they thought was “risk-free”:

Every level that matters in the worked example. The scratch stop for unit 1 and the position's break-even are ten points apart by leg 2.

Know which one you have set. “My stop is at scratch” and “the position is at break-even” are different claims once you have added.

10. Re-entry After a Scratch

The scenario: the pyramid is stopped at 110 for a flat trade. Three days later the market takes out the high and runs to 135 without you. At a 25% win rate this happens often, and the traders who never re-enter are the ones who were right about the trend and made nothing from it.

A scratch resets the trade, not the thesis. If the original entry condition fires again — a new breakout, a higher low that holds — re-enter with unit 1 at the full initial R, as a new trade. Not with the old pyramid’s size; the cushion that justified that size is gone. This is Livermore’s probing: small until the market proves the idea, then sized.

The re-entry often has a better stop than the first entry. The shakeout low is now a reference point that did not exist before, and a stop beneath it is tighter than the original: same R, smaller distance, more shares.

Guardrails. Each re-entry is a full expense, so cap them — two or three per trend. A third scratch is the market saying it is not trending; stand aside until new structure forms. Keep re-entries in the accounting alongside the scratches, so the cost of persistence stays visible.

11. Losses as Business Expenses

A stop that fills where you planned it is a cost of doing business — commission, slippage, the small planned loss. A loss is what happens when the stop is moved, ignored, or sized wrong. The distinction is not semantic: expenses are bounded and budgeted; losses are not.

At a 25% win rate, three of every four trades are expenses. The business is profitable only if the fourth is exploited. Two things follow:

  • The expense line must stay small and boring — stage 1 discipline, every time, especially on trades that feel like they will turn around.
  • Scratching a trade early is not failure. It is a cheaper expense than the full stop, and it frees the risk budget for the next attempt.

12. Trade Less to Make More

Adds consume two scarce resources: risk budget and attention. Both have to be available when the trade that deserves to be pressed shows up. Twenty mediocre positions fragment the budget into pieces too small to press with, and fragment focus so the press is late or missed.

Portfolio-level rules that follow:

  • Cap total open risk across all positions. Adds draw from the same cap.
  • Correlated winners are one bet — pyramiding three positions that move together is one large pyramid, not three small ones.
  • Reserve part of the budget for adds so the winner is not starved by new entries.

Fewer positions, bigger when right.

13. Adding to Size at the Account Level

“Adding to size” has a second meaning. Everything above happens inside a trade. Across a career, the same idea is raising R itself — the base risk per trade — as the account and the evidence grow. This is what turns stage 3 into financial freedom rather than one good year.

Fixed-fraction sizing already scales: at 1% of equity, R in dollars grows with the account on its own. Sizing up means raising the fraction, and that is a different decision from watching the dollars grow.

Why it has to be slow

With a 25% win rate, losing streaks are long. The chance of ten losses in a row is 0.7510, about 5.6%, and over 500 trades the longest losing streak you should expect is around 20. At R = 1% with 0.7R average losses, a 20-loss streak costs about 13%. At R = 3%, about 35%.

Kelly gives the ceiling. For p = 0.25 and a win/loss ratio of 7 (5R against 0.7R), full Kelly is about 14% per trade — a number no one survives in practice, because the inputs are estimates and the streaks are real. Quarter-Kelly, around 3.5%, is where the ceiling sits; most traders should live well below it.

Base risk stepping with the equity curve: up on evidence, down on drawdown, restored on recovery.

The rules

  • Step on evidence, not on streaks. Raise R only after a defined sample at the current size — fifty to a hundred trades — with expectancy holding. A hot month is not a sample.
  • Tie the step to equity. Raise R by a fixed increment (0.25%) when equity makes a new high a set distance above the equity at the last step-up (say 25%). Step down one notch on a defined drawdown from peak (10%), and restore only when the old peak is regained. Slow up, fast down.
  • Scalable means liquid. Matt’s third process requirement: a rule that works at 250 shares must work at 2,500. Check slippage and fill quality at the next size before stepping, and drop the instruments that stop scaling before you drop R.
  • Never step mid-trade. R changes between trades, on a schedule. Changing it while a pyramid is on is adding risk under another name.

14. Codifying It

Matt’s process requirement — consistent, repeatable, scalable — translates directly into parameters:

What to measure, beyond expectancy:

  • Tail capture — on the top-decile trades, realized R divided by the R available from entry to peak. This is the number stage 3 exists to raise.
  • Give-back — (max open profit − realized profit) ÷ max open profit. Pyramids raise this; you want the trade-off, not a blowout.
  • P&L attribution by unit — how much comes from units 2..U. If it is small or negative, the adds are not earning their cost.
  • MAE after adds — how far trades go against you once the position is at full size.
  • Peel churn — how often the LIFO trigger fires and the peeled unit is re-added within a few bars. A high number means the trigger sits inside the noise.
  • Re-entry cost — expenses spent on re-entries per trend actually captured.
  • Slippage at full size — the last unit is where fills are worst and gaps hurt most.

Warning. k, m, x and B interact strongly and overfit easily — optimize them together and test them across regimes, including flat ones, to see the damage. Pyramiding rules that look wonderful in a trending sample can be net negative over a full cycle.

15. Dangers

  • The inverted pyramid — adds so large that the position’s break-even chases price, and normal noise scratches the whole thing.
  • Late adds — adding on the extension rather than the resumption. The fourth add in a run is where the odds are worst.
  • Averaging down in disguise — adding on a pullback below the last unit’s entry.
  • Gap and flash-move risk — scales with final size. “Zero risk at the stop” assumes the stop fills.
  • Events at full size — holding the whole pyramid through a scheduled binary event. That is a bet on the print, not a zero-risk position.
  • The flinch — cutting a rule-based pyramid on a routine pullback because the dollar swing is new (Section 8).
  • Correlated pyramids — several pressed positions in the same factor are one very large bet.
  • Regime mismatch — pressing continuation in a mean-reverting market.
  • The euphoria add — adding because the trade feels great rather than because the rule fired. And its mirror: refusing to add because you are “up enough,” which is stage 2 masquerading as prudence.
  • Spending the cushion twice — counting the same locked profit against two separate adds.
  • Stepping up R on a hot streak — raising base size on a month rather than a sample. The twenty-loss streak arrives eventually.

16. The Rule Sheet

  1. Raise the stop before every add. Never lower it.
  2. Size the add from the budget: Q_add ≤ (B + L) / (E_add − S).
  3. Never add below the last unit’s entry.
  4. Only add if the previous unit is in profit and the trend condition still holds.
  5. Add on the resumption after a rest, not on the extension. No adds beyond ext_max ATR from the base.
  6. Keep the position’s break-even beyond normal noise (at least 1.5–2 ATR from price).
  7. Size the pyramid to the one-ATR swing you can sit through, not only to risk-at-stop.
  8. Cap units per position and size per add.
  9. Decide the unwind design before the add: one stop, or last-in, first-out.
  10. Know whether your stop is a scratch stop or a position break-even.
  11. Prefer realized profit as the add budget where gaps are possible.
  12. Reduce to the core unit before scheduled binary events.
  13. A scratch resets the trade, not the thesis: re-enter with unit 1 when the setup re-triggers, capped at two or three tries per trend.
  14. Cap total portfolio risk; correlated winners count as one.
  15. Raise base R on a sample of trades and an equity threshold; step it down on a drawdown; never mid-trade.
  16. Every planned stop is an expense. Only unplanned ones are losses.
