## Introduction: Economics, Everywhere

### The Lunch-Break Frame

- [MUST KNOW] Core thesis: **economics is everywhere** — every mundane daily
  decision embeds fundamental economic questions. The authors walk through
  a Bank of England economist's lunch break as a tour through markets,
  supply chains, climate change, trade theory, the nature of money, banking,
  and interest rates.

- [MUST KNOW] **Markets as coordination mechanisms:** Within the Square Mile
  there are over 500 cafes and takeaways. No one centrally directs what
  they serve. The sandwich available precisely when desired emerges from
  millions of individual decisions — farmers, factories, logistics, shops —
  none of them trying to feed *this particular* economist. "This is the
  power of what economists call markets. They have a way of coordinating
  millions of individual decisions to get an outcome that, in theory at
  least, suits everyone." The caveat "in theory at least" is embedded in
  the opening pages.

- [MUST KNOW] **The same forces cut both ways:** The Thames has risen more
  than **1.5 metres** since the Bank of England was established in 1694.
  "This is the flipside of those very forces that allowed our economist to
  get their sandwich. On this occasion, the cumulative decisions of billions
  of humans haven't led to a positive outcome... but instead to a negative
  one (climate change)." Same market mechanism that delivers sandwiches also
  creates **negative externalities**.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Deindustrialisation** introduced via London's Docklands:
  once a hive of activity shipping steel, coal, and cars to Calcutta and
  Canberra. Now warehouses converted to restaurants. The principle of
  **comparative advantage** explains why Britain used to export manufactured
  goods and now imports most clothes and fruit.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Banks create money** — introduced as a major reveal:
  commercial banks (whose logos appear on Canary Wharf skyscrapers) "create
  the vast majority of the money that we use in our daily lives — something
  that most people who handle that money don't even realise."

### What Is Economics?

- [MUST KNOW] **Two competing definitions:**
  1. **Narrow:** The study of scarcity — how to allocate finite things
     (land, people, time) in the best possible way
  2. **Broad:** Everything — the cumulative effect of all billions of human
     decisions and how they interact with the world
  - Truth is "somewhere in the middle." Etymology: from Greek *eco*
    (household) + *nomos* (accounts) = household management.

- [MUST KNOW] **Adam Smith** (the "grandfather of modern economics"):
  *The Wealth of Nations* argued self-interest drives efficient resource
  use and makes everyone better off (the **invisible hand**). But Smith
  *also* wrote *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (before Wealth of Nations),
  emphasising ethical and social questions. The moral dimension is less
  famous but equally important to his framework.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Aristotle** talked about a "just price" at which people
  *should* trade and thought money for its own sake was morally bad.
  Economics has always carried a moral dimension, even when modern
  economists try to strip it out.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Alfred Marshall** — late 19th century; tutored Keynes;
  led the formalisation of economics as a hard science. Economists
  literally borrowed **missile guidance modelling techniques** (originally
  for ballistic projectiles) for economic models. Irony: by the end of his
  career, Marshall himself thought mathematics was "at most a tool" and at
  its worst "an active hindrance to economists" — but the formalisation
  he set in motion outlived him and dominated for a century.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Modern broadening:** Behavioural economics emerged to
  challenge the "coldly rational" human assumption, incorporating
  psychology. Today's economists incorporate sociology, psychology, and
  even **biology**. Synthesis of Marshall's scientific rigour with the
  pluralistic ethos of Aristotle and Smith.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Diane Coyle** (Cambridge): noted that economics contains
  "an implicit and unavoidable moral framework... especially when it comes
  to setting economic policy." Resource allocation requires value
  judgments — what kind of society do we want?

### Why Economic Literacy Matters

- [MUST KNOW] **Economic literacy has measurable personal benefits:**
  "Economic literacy has been shown to improve your life expectancy, mental
  well-being and lifetime earnings." In the UK, **over 80% of people**
  say economics is relevant to their everyday lives — but a significant
  majority think it is inaccessible even when explained by skilled
  communicators. The **Global Financial Literacy Excellence Center** found
  only **one-third of people worldwide** have basic financial literacy —
  correlated with low financial inclusion and disconnection from the
  financial world.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Economists are partly to blame:** Abstract models on
  "bizarre-sounding assumptions, often written in complex mathematical
  formulae." **Andy Haldane** (former Chief Economist, Bank of England)
  joked: "an economist with strong social skills is one who stares at your
  shoes, rather than their own, when engaged in polite conversation."

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Bank of England's history of opacity:** A former Governor
  famously said the Bank should **"never apologise, never explain."** City
  insiders claimed to read policy signals from the Governor's **eyebrow
  movements**. The Bank has acknowledged this as a failure of its longest-
  established duty.

## Chapter 1: Where Does My Breakfast Come From?

*On supply and demand, the (sometimes) magical power of markets,
and why Woolworths' Pic'n'Mix was the start of your economic education.*

### Three Types of Constraints

- [MUST KNOW] Every economic decision is shaped by three overlapping constraints:
  1. **Budget constraints** — how much money you have to spend
  2. **Resource constraints** — physical/natural limits on available inputs
  3. **Time constraints** — only so many hours in a day
  - These interact: working more eases the budget constraint but tightens
    the time constraint. The core economic problem: given finite resources,
    how do we allocate them so goods reach people when and where needed?

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Woolworths Pic'n'Mix** as consumer economics: On
  **21 February 2009** the last Pic'n'Mix was sold for £14,500
  (collectible auction). The pick-and-mix stand is a teaching metaphor:
  children unconsciously navigated **budget constraints** (weekly
  allowance), **preferences** (cola bottles vs. chocolate raisins), and
  **trade-offs** (one heavy gobstopper vs. three light sweets) —
  the complete microcosm of consumer behaviour. When the bag got too heavy,
  you put back the sweet you had twenty of, not the one you had only one
  of — **diminishing marginal utility** without knowing the term.

### Utility and Opportunity Cost

- [MUST KNOW] **Utility** = the pleasure, satisfaction, or benefit you
  get from doing or consuming something. Economists assume every individual
  tries to *maximise utility* subject to constraints — not just monetary
  gain but enjoyment, stimulation, meaning. Someone taking a lower-paying
  job isn't violating the model — they derive more utility from autonomy
  or purpose. **Prices as utility signals**: the more utility you get from
  something, the more you're willing to pay.

- [MUST KNOW] **Opportunity cost** = the cost of something is not just
  what you pay, but *what you give up* by choosing that option over the
  next best alternative. Cutting your own hair saves money but costs time
  and potentially career opportunities (bad hair at interview). **Tim
  Harford** reframes it as a practical tool: instead of thinking about
  what you're saying *yes* to, think about what you're saying *no* to.
  Staying late at work = saying no to reading your daughter a bedtime
  story. The "false economy" (buying cheap but losing more elsewhere) is
  everyday opportunity cost. Cost of going to university: tuition +
  foregone earnings — not just tuition.

### Law of Demand

- [MUST KNOW] **Law of demand**: as something becomes more expensive, we
  want less of it; as it becomes cheaper, we want more. One of economics'
  closest things to a universal law — both intuitively correct and backed
  by large bodies of data. **Happy hour example**: more than 60% of bars'
  alcohol sales can come from happy hour periods. Quality unchanged; only
  price changed.

- [MUST KNOW] Two mechanisms explain why lower prices increase demand:
  1. **Income effect**: lower price means your money goes further —
     effectively richer. Your £10 buys three beers, not two.
  2. **Substitution effect**: the good is now relatively cheaper than
     alternatives, so you switch toward it. The run to the bar just before
     happy hour ends: substituting a future drink (more expensive) for a
     present one (cheaper now).

### Price Elasticity

- [MUST KNOW] **Price elasticity of demand** = how sensitive quantity
  demanded is to a price change. Elastic goods: small price change → big
  quantity change. Inelastic goods: large price change → little quantity
  change.
  - **Elastic example — carrier bags**: Wales introduced a 5p charge in
    2010. A tiny absolute increase (from zero to 5p) reduced single-use
    bag consumption by over **90%**. Policy worked far better than critics
    expected.
  - **Inelastic example — Daraprim**: **Martin Shkreli** (Turing
    Pharmaceuticals, "Pharma Bro") raised the price from **$13 to $750**,
    betting demand would be inelastic for immunocompromised patients with
    no alternative. Partially right short-term, but misjudged: public
    backlash + development of cheaper alternatives caused Turing's sales
    to fall within a few years. Lesson: inelastic demand doesn't mean
    *perfectly* inelastic — very high prices still destroy markets via
    substitution over time.
  - **Cigarettes**: addiction creates inelasticity. A 2013 study found
    every 1% increase in price reduced demand by only ~0.33% — less than
    one cigarette per pack.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Non-price demand shifters:**
  - **Tastes & fashions**: in 1963, ~40% of US adults smoked; the Rat
    Pack made it glamorous. Since the 1960s, health awareness has driven
    decline — UK cigarette demand fell 25% between 2011 and 2018 (~25
    billion fewer cigarettes).
  - **Income effects**: as countries grow wealthier, meat consumption rises.
    **China meat example**: in 1961 China consumed <4kg of meat per person
    per year; today ~63kg — a near 16× increase driven almost entirely by
    rising incomes.
  - **Cross-elasticity:** Substitutes (Coke/Pepsi — positive
    cross-elasticity: if Coke price rises, Pepsi demand rises).
    Complements (Xbox + Xbox games — negative cross-elasticity: fewer
    consoles means fewer games).

### Violations of the Law of Demand

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Veblen goods** (named for **Thorstein Veblen**, 1899):
  higher price *increases* demand because the price signals status.
  Examples: luxury cars, fine art, expensive wine. **Conspicuous
  consumption** — people value what buying something expensive signals to
  others. Real-world example: 21% of restaurant diners order the
  second-cheapest wine — if that wine became cheaper than the cheapest,
  demand would *fall*.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Giffen goods** (named for **Robert Giffen**, Victorian
  era): higher price increases demand for poverty-driven reasons — the
  opposite mechanism from Veblen goods. Poor families split income between
  a cheap staple (bread) and a luxury (meat). When bread's price rises,
  they can no longer afford meat and buy *more* bread to survive.
  **Robert Jensen** and **Nolan Miller** (2008) studied rice demand in
  China and found classic Giffen properties — higher rice price led to
  higher rice consumption among the poor. Giffen goods are rarer now but
  not extinct.

### Firms and Profit Maximisation

- [MUST KNOW] A **firm** = any group of people who come together to
  produce something to sell, drawing on **inputs**: workers, machines,
  raw materials. The working assumption: firms **maximise profits**
  (revenue minus all costs). **Marginal logic**: keep producing until the
  cost of one more unit (**marginal cost**) exceeds the additional revenue
  (**marginal revenue**). Profit is maximised where MR = MC.

- [MUST KNOW] **Worked example:** Start selling 100 T-shirts at £20,
  costing £15 each → £500 profit. T-shirt #101: must drop price to
  £19.99 for ALL shirts. Marginal revenue = £19, MC = £15 → profit
  rises by £4, do it. T-shirt #102: must drop price to £19.90. Marginal
  revenue = £10 (price cut applies to all 101 previous shirts too), MC
  = £15 → MR < MC, don't do it. Key insight: even though selling price
  (£19.90) exceeds marginal cost (£15), *real* marginal revenue is lower
  once you factor in the price cut on all units. This is why MR ≠ price.

### The Friedman Doctrine and Law of Supply

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Milton Friedman**, "The Social Responsibility of Business
  Is to Increase Its Profits" (*New York Times*, 1970): if individuals value
  ethical production, they'll spend their own money on it; companies have
  no right to impose that choice by reducing shareholder/worker returns.
  When companies act "ethically" at the cost of profit, they transfer money
  to causes those individuals may not have chosen themselves.
  Critique: assumes individuals have enough information and power to make
  good ethical decisions as consumers — a debatable premise for systemic
  issues like climate change. Modern CSR may simply be profit maximisation
  via ethical branding — winning customers who value moral credentials.

- [MUST KNOW] **Law of supply**: as price rises, businesses want to produce
  more; as price falls, they want to produce less. Mechanism: at low prices,
  many producers can't cover costs and exit; at high prices, previously
  unprofitable producers enter, expanding supply.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Shale oil as the law of supply in action**: Scientists
  long knew oil existed in shale but couldn't extract it cheaply. In the
  1990s–2000s, hydraulic fracturing ("fracking") made it possible. Shale
  rigs typically require oil above **$40–$50 per barrel** to be profitable.
  Whenever prices rose above that threshold, the number of active shale
  rigs increased noticeably — supply responding directly to price signals.
  Creates a natural supply ceiling on oil prices; if prices spike, shale
  supply expands until prices fall back.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Supply elasticity:** Some goods' supply responds strongly
  to price (elastic); others barely at all (inelastic). **Euro 2020 final
  (July 11, 2021)**: standard tickets originally ~£250; England's first
  major final since 1966 → demand surged. Supply was **perfectly inelastic**
  (Wembley holds max 90,000; only 67,000 due to Covid). **Ticket touts**
  charged up to **£35,000 per ticket** — almost 14,000% increase. When
  supply genuinely cannot respond, price absorbs all demand pressure.
  **Mark Twain** on land: "They aren't making it any more" — classic
  insight into perfectly inelastic supply; land value has increased more
  than **fifty times** over the past ~100 years.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Non-price drivers of supply:** Input costs matter too. If
  workers become cheaper, firms earn more profit at the same selling price
  and supply more. Supply curves shift when *anything other than the price
  of the output changes* (input costs, technology, regulations).

### Markets, Equilibrium, and the Invisible Hand

- [MUST KNOW] A **market** = any place where buyers and sellers come
  together to agree how much to exchange and at what price — from a street
  stall to a stock exchange to the internet. "There are markets for luxury,
  markets for labour, even markets for love."

- [MUST KNOW] **Uber's surge pricing (December 15, 2014, Sydney siege)**:
  a gunman took hostages in the CBD; as people fled, Uber prices "rocketed
  to eye-watering levels." Public backlash was severe; Uber refunded fares.
  But the episode is a textbook example of market equilibrium in real time.
  When demand > supply, the algorithm raised the price → attracted more
  drivers (increases supply) + deterred some passengers (decreases demand)
  → price kept rising until drivers = people wanting rides → **equilibrium**
  where everyone who wants a ride at that price gets one.

- [MUST KNOW] **Market equilibrium:** If demand > supply at current price,
  buyers bid up prices until equilibrium is restored; if supply > demand,
  prices fall. **Prices as signals**: rising price → demand rising relative
  to supply → produce more; falling price → supply rising → produce less.
  These signals arise from millions of individual decisions with no central
  coordinator.

- [MUST KNOW] **Adam Smith's invisible hand**: the avocado grower, wheat
  farmer, lorry driver, baker — none know you or care about your breakfast
  personally. They act from self-interest. Yet their coordinated actions
  reliably deliver your breakfast. *"It is not by the benevolence of the
  butcher, of the baker that we expect them to provide our food, but their
  own self-interest."* Command economies (USSR, East Germany) tried to
  replace markets with state allocation but struggled to process the
  billions of individual choices markets handle automatically — leading to
  inefficiency and shortages.

- [SHOULD KNOW] Markets have real failures even Smith acknowledged: they
  can drive unsustainable fossil fuel demand, enable dominant businesses
  to exploit market power, and reward those with an information edge at
  others' expense. Sets up Chapter Two.

## Chapter 2: Can Economics Solve Climate Change?

*On the theory of perfect competition, how and why markets fail,
and why economists always take too many chips.*

### The Tragedy of the Commons

- [MUST KNOW] **Bank of England canteen chip problem**: Economists helped
  themselves to far more chips than they needed (free/flat-price buffet),
  causing waste and shortages for latecomers. Everyone acted rationally
  in self-interest; the collective outcome was inefficient.

- [MUST KNOW] **Tragedy of the Commons** (coined by **Garrett Hardin**,
  1960s): when a resource is shared, every individual has an incentive
  to take as much as possible before others do — even though if everyone
  does this, the resource is destroyed. Hardin's example: common land
  for grazing. Each farmer adds more animals (more income) but ignores
  the collective cost of overgrazing → land degrades → useless to all.
  *"Each man is locked into a system that compels him to increase his
  herd without limit, in a world that is limited."* The chip pan is the
  microcosm; the atmosphere is the real-world example.

- [MUST KNOW] **Market failure**: situations where rational individual
  behaviour produces a collectively worse outcome than possible.
  Explains: why most people use Facebook rather than dozens of competing
  networks, why states provide free education, and why the planet faces
  a climate emergency.

- [MUST KNOW] **George Box**: *"All models are wrong, but some are useful."*
  A model that perfectly represented reality would just BE reality —
  useless for analysis. The right question: not "Is the model true?" (it
  never is) but "Is it good enough for this application?" Economics uses
  **perfect competition** as the baseline model — not because it describes
  the real world, but because understanding how reality departs from
  perfection tells you exactly where and why markets fail.

### Perfect Competition: The Model

- [MUST KNOW] **Perfect competition** requires three conditions:
  1. **Homogeneous goods**: all sellers offer identical products — no
     branding, no quality differences. Buyers have no reason to prefer
     one seller over another.
  2. **Many buyers and sellers, no barriers to entry**: no single actor
     can influence the price; new sellers can freely enter if there's
     profit to be made.
  3. **Perfect information**: all buyers and sellers know quality,
     approximate demand/supply, and prices of all alternatives.

- [MUST KNOW] In perfect competition, every participant is a **"price
  taker"** — no individual can raise or lower the market price. Price
  settles at **minimum acceptable profit** for sellers: enough to keep
  them in business, but no excess profit. A price rise would send all
  informed buyers to identical competitors; new entrants would undercut
  any across-the-board rise.

- [MUST KNOW] A perfectly competitive market is **"efficient"** in two
  specific senses:
  - **Productively efficient**: goods produced at the lowest possible
    average cost — can't produce more of one thing without producing
    less of another.
  - **Allocatively efficient**: the utility consumers gain equals the
    cost to producers of making it — exactly the right amount of effort
    goes into each product.
  - Real-world approximations: **milk** and **refined sugar** — nearly
    identical regardless of seller, many sellers, low brand loyalty.
    But most markets fall well short.

### Imperfect Competition

- [MUST KNOW] In reality, most markets have **imperfect competition**:
  - **Monopoly**: single seller (*mono* = one, *polein* = sell)
  - **Duopoly**: two sellers
  - **Oligopoly**: a handful of sellers
  - Real-world example: **YKK** (Japanese company) — at the turn of
    the 2010s, made the vast majority of zips worldwide; still accounts
    for ~half of all zips globally. One of the most complete monopolies
    most people have never heard of.

- [MUST KNOW] **Collusion**: in oligopolistic markets, the few sellers
  may cooperate to mimic monopoly pricing. **Adam Smith** centuries ago:
  *"People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment
  and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the
  public, or in some contrivance to raise prices."* Collusion is illegal
  in most jurisdictions but historically common.

### How Monopolies Harm Consumers

- [MUST KNOW] A monopolist is a **"price setter"** — it can raise prices
  without losing all customers because there's no competitive alternative.
  Strategy: produce *less* than a competitive market would, push price
  higher on the reduced supply. Result: higher profits for the monopolist;
  some buyers are priced out entirely even at prices that would be
  profitable under competition.

- [MUST KNOW] **Deadweight loss**: the economic waste created by monopoly
  — resources misallocated because not enough of the good is produced.
  "The monopolist is better off; the world is worse off."

- [MUST KNOW] **Monopolies stifle innovation.** Case study: **Microsoft
  and Internet Explorer (1999)** — US Department of Justice brought charges
  for anti-competitive behaviour; Microsoft bundled IE into its OS and made
  installing competitor software difficult, creating an artificial barrier
  to entry. Microsoft had consistently deterred competitor innovations that
  would have benefited consumers. "History shows that when a monopoly is
  in town, innovation can slow to a halt."

- [SHOULD KNOW] Governments respond through **antitrust/competition
  policy**: UK's **Competition and Markets Authority (CMA)**, US's **FTC**
  and **DoJ**.

### When Monopolies Can Be Good

- [MUST KNOW] **Fixed costs vs. variable costs:** Ice-cream van: ~£30,000
  fixed (van, registration, training) + £1 per lolly variable. Sell 1
  lolly → cost £30,001. Sell 30,000 lollies → cost per lolly just £2.
  **Economies of scale**: as output rises, average cost per unit falls.
  High fixed costs also act as **barriers to entry** — orders of magnitude
  more for a power plant or railway network.

- [MUST KNOW] **Natural monopoly in railways**: in 19th-century USA,
  competing rail companies laid parallel tracks yards apart — massive
  inefficiency. Over time, market consolidated toward single providers
  that could exploit economies of scale. Customers got a better deal from
  one efficient provider than from multiple wasteful competitors. Sometimes
  **less competition is more efficient** — "competition good, monopolies
  bad" is too simple.

- [MUST KNOW] **Patents** = legally mandated monopolies. The paradox:
  normally monopolies reduce innovation; patents use monopoly to *create*
  innovation. Developing a new drug costs ~**$1 billion** on average before
  a single dose is sold. Without patent protection, a competitor could copy
  the formula immediately after launch, charge less (no R&D costs), and
  undercut the innovator → rational firms stop investing in new drugs.
  Patent grants temporary monopoly → innovator can recover R&D costs →
  incentive to invest in risky innovation.

- [MUST KNOW] **Downside of pharmaceutical patents**: Oxfam (2021) claimed
  **Pfizer/BioNTech** and **Moderna** used patent-granted monopoly power
  to charge ~**$41 billion above** estimated production cost for Covid-19
  vaccines. Patents are necessary to incentivise innovation, but enable
  price exploitation that harms access — especially in global health
  emergencies.

- [MUST KNOW] **Network effects**: the value of a product increases as more
  people use it. **Social media** paradigm: a platform nobody uses is
  nearly worthless; the dominant platform is extremely valuable.
  **Facebook** ~3 billion users, **Instagram** ~1.4 billion, **Twitter**
  ~400 million. New entrants face near-impossible odds even with a superior
  product — everyone rationally stays on the existing dominant network.

- [MUST KNOW] **Networked integration** creates cross-market monopoly:
  Amazon Prime + Kindle → extract more value from **Alexa** than from a
  competitor's device. Dominance in one sphere (e-commerce/reading)
  extends to another (smart home). The barrier is not capital expenditure
  but **accumulated users/data/integrations** — switching costs + integrated
  value.

- [MUST KNOW] **Balanced verdict on monopolies:** governments respond to
  natural monopolies with **regulated monopolies** — state watchdogs
  oversee pricing and behaviour to preserve scale/network benefits while
  preventing exploitation. Industries commonly regulated: train networks,
  energy, finance, telecoms. A **hybrid of state and market** — not pure
  competition, not pure state control.

### Externalities

- [MUST KNOW] **Externality**: the side-effect of an activity on people not
  directly involved — social cost/benefit diverges from private cost/benefit.
  This divergence is a core mechanism of market failure.

- [MUST KNOW] **Positive externality** from higher education: private
  benefit to student < social benefit to society. Private benefits: higher
  lifetime earnings, social networks. Social benefits (NOT factored in by
  the individual): more productive workforce, more law-abiding citizens,
  higher civic engagement. Market failure: individuals only buy to the
  point their private need is met; ignores wider social benefit. Result:
  market **under-provides** education relative to what is socially optimal.
  - UK tuition fee history maps directly to beliefs about externality size:
    pre-1998 = free; 1997 legislation = max £1,000/year; 2006 = £3,000;
    2012 = £9,000 (widespread protests). Tuition fee debates are really
    debates about how large the positive externality is.

- [MUST KNOW] **Negative externality — climate change:** In 1990, coal
  electricity cost just over 1p/kWh; wind power cost more than 15× that.
  Rational energy companies chose coal — fossil fuels made up over 91%
  of UK energy consumed in 1990. The market price of coal didn't reflect
  its true cost: coal generates ~20× more carbon emissions than wind.
  The price of burning fossil fuels doesn't include the environmental
  cost to everyone else. Just as positive externalities → under-production,
  negative externalities → **over-production**. "Climate change is the
  ultimate negative externality."

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Tragedy of the Horizons** — **Mark Carney** (former
  Governor, Bank of England) coined this term. Economic evidence shows
  people heavily discount the future relative to the present. Climate
  change operates on multi-generational timeframes that exceed most
  people's planning horizon. This short-termism is itself a market
  failure that compounds the negative externality problem.

### Economic Solutions to Climate Change

- [MUST KNOW] **Solution 1: Regulation** — government caps on production
  (e.g., limit how much coal can be mined). Problem: to set the right
  quota you must calculate the precise size of the externality across
  hundreds of years — "the numbers would be dizzying." Usually we leave
  such calculations to markets; but here the market isn't working — that's
  the problem.

- [MUST KNOW] **Solution 2: Pigouvian tax** — named for **Arthur Cecil
  Pigou** (wrote the foundational book on externalities in 1920).
  Idea: tax goods with negative externalities to raise their price to
  reflect true social cost. If true cost of fossil fuels is double the
  market price → tax should be 100%. Effect: decisions remain
  market-driven, but priced at their true cost → self-interest aligns
  with social good. Mirror image for positive externalities: a **Pigouvian
  subsidy** lowers price to the individual to reflect the social benefit
  (free tuition is an example). **The Pigou Club**: group of leading
  economists who have advocated applying Pigou's ideas to fossil fuels
  for ~20 years.

- [MUST KNOW] **Solution 3: Cap-and-trade / Emissions Trading Scheme
  (ETS)** — based on **Ronald Coase**'s critique of Pigou (1960s).
  Coase's argument: externality problems arise not because markets fail
  but because there are **missing markets** — no market exists to trade
  the externality itself. Fix: create a market for the right to emit CO₂.
  EU set up the **ETS in 2005**: total cap on CO₂ set and reduced over
  time; companies buy permits to emit; those who reduce emissions below
  their permit can sell the excess. Market price of permits reflects the
  true externality through supply/demand dynamics. At time of writing:
  30+ ETS schemes worldwide. Key difference from Pigouvian tax: market
  finds the price rather than government setting it.

- [MUST KNOW] **Elinor Ostrom** — Nobel Prize-winning economist; challenged
  the inevitability of the Tragedy of the Commons. Studied communities
  worldwide (India, US, Kenya, Turkey). Finding: when communities have
  control over their own common resources, they often do NOT deplete them
  — they come together to ensure sustainability. Key condition: those
  affected by resource depletion must have a voice in decision-making.
  Policy implication: **community collaboration** can prevent Tragedy
  of the Commons without state intervention. Both Hardin and Ostrom are
  right under different conditions — institutional design matters enormously.

### Information Asymmetry

- [MUST KNOW] **George Akerlof** — Nobel Prize winner; 1970 paper "The
  Market for Lemons." A **lemon** = a dud second-hand car (US slang).
  Central insight: when buyers can't accurately assess quality, they assume
  average quality and pay average price. Consequence: sellers of high-quality
  cars won't sell (can't get a fair price); sellers of lemons will sell
  (getting more than their car is worth). Feedback loop: good cars exit
  the market → average quality falls → buyers lower expectations → good
  cars exit further → "ever-diminishing quality." This is **information
  asymmetry** as market failure: seller knows far more than buyer.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Adverse selection**: insurer can't tell risky from safe
  driver. Charges average price; reckless driver gets a deal, safe driver
  overpays. Solution attempt: in-car monitoring boxes. But adverse
  selection persists: bad drivers opt out (uncertainty works in their
  favour); good drivers opt in; insurers now treat box-refusal as a risk
  signal. Akerlof's insight arms insurers: "opt-out signals higher risk."

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Fake reviews as information asymmetry**: 2020: Amazon
  deleted ~20,000 fake reviews after the *Financial Times* alleged
  reviewers were paid for 5-star reviews. Estimate: ~60% of products
  on Amazon.co.uk had fake reviews. Sellers exploit buyers' information
  deficit — buyers look for extra signals to compensate for lack of
  direct knowledge; sellers manufacture those signals.

- [SHOULD KNOW] Imperfect information compounds the climate crisis. People
  don't know the full environmental cost of their choices: is an electric
  car better if battery production exceeds emissions savings? Without full
  information, consumers can't make socially optimal choices.

### Chapter Two Conclusion

- [MUST KNOW] The Bank of England canteen eventually differentiated chip
  prices by portion size. Initial uproar; staff had grown accustomed to
  all-you-can-eat. Outcome: less food waste; latecomers found chips
  available. Lesson: pricing can internalise the cost of overconsumption
  and solve common resource problems.

- [MUST KNOW] Chapter synthesis — markets fail in multiple ways
  simultaneously; climate change is where they all collide:
  - **Under-production** of useful things (education)
  - **Over-production** of harmful things (carbon dioxide)
  - **Monopolies** that act against consumer and social interest
  - **Information asymmetry** that prevents informed decisions
  > "Solving the climate emergency will take a combination of economic
  > understanding, political will and social change. Economic theories,
  > models and studies can only take us so far. But they undoubtedly have
  > a role to play."

## Chapter 3: How Do I Get a Pay Rise?

*On where wages come from, frictions in the Springfield labour market,
and why you should almost always consider going back to school.*

### Labour Markets Introduction — Fortnite World Cup

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Fortnite World Cup** (July 2019, Arthur Ashe Stadium,
  New York): 100 finalists had outperformed 40 million players. Kyle
  'Bugha' Giersdorf (teenager) won $3 million; now earns $50,000/month
  from subscribers watching him play online. Top gaming celebrities earn
  tens of millions/year. Point: labour markets explain why some people
  are paid enormous sums for video games while others in "worthwhile"
  jobs earn little.

- [MUST KNOW] **Marginal product** — the foundational theory of wages:
  your wage equals how much monetary value you add to your employer's
  output. A baker who makes ten cakes/day is worth more than one who
  makes five. Pro gamers earn millions because they have a high marginal
  product — massive audience demand for their entertainment generates
  revenue far exceeding their salary. The authors' Mario Kart video
  would earn £0 — their marginal product as YouTube entertainers is
  essentially zero.

### Labour Supply and Demand

- [MUST KNOW] **Labour supply** — what determines how many people work and
  how many hours:
  - Macro level: size of working-age population (UK: ages 16–64)
  - Changes due to net migration (Miami's working population increased
    7% in 1980 due to mass Cuban migration), and cultural shifts
    (women's workforce participation has vastly expanded since mid-20th
    century, adding millions to labour supply)
  - Micro level: whether earnings exceed non-work income; whether you
    enjoy the work; whether you prefer leisure to additional income
  - As hourly wages rise, people often work **fewer** hours — they need
    to work less to maintain the same income.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Derived demand** — the distinctive nature of labour
  demand: employers don't want workers for their own sake. Demand for
  labour is derived from demand for the goods or services workers produce.
  Recessions → consumption falls → businesses downsize → less demand for
  workers. Automation → robots substitute for humans → reduces demand for
  certain labour types. Your wage depends partly on how much consumers
  want what you help produce.

- [MUST KNOW] **Supply-demand balance determines wages**: shortage of
  workers in a skill area → wages rise. Computer scientists: not enough
  coders relative to demand → high wages. Commercial deep-sea divers:
  few people willing to spend months on a boat in dangerous conditions
  → compensating premium. The **invisible hand** trying to recruit people
  into hard-to-fill roles. Simplest answer to "how do I get a pay rise?":
  take a job that employers struggle to fill.

### Labour Market Frictions

- [MUST KNOW] **Labour market frictions**: forces that prevent supply and
  demand from matching smoothly, ensuring there will **always** be some
  unemployment in any economy. Even with strong demand, wages don't
  always rise instantly to attract more supply. Employers and employees
  often don't even know what jobs exist.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Classical economists' view of unemployment**: unemployment
  is simply over-supply of labour. If more window cleaners than windows
  to clean, wages fall → some workers find the wage too low to bother →
  they "choose" unemployment. The market wage is below the worker's
  **reservation wage** (minimum at which they'd accept a job). In the
  classical view, if wages are flexible the market clears — unemployment
  is voluntary.

- [MUST KNOW] **Keynesian critique**: **John Maynard Keynes** challenged
  classical theory in the early 20th century. Unemployment is caused by
  lack of demand for goods and services — not worker choice. The problem
  is on the demand side of the economy, not the supply side.

- [MUST KNOW] **"New classical" theory** (modern consensus): economists
  no longer assume people choose unemployment. Most unemployment is
  **involuntary** — people are victims of circumstances. Focus shifted
  to changes in labour supply conditions and reasons why markets fail
  to clear.

### Three Types of Unemployment

- [MUST KNOW] **Cyclical unemployment**: unemployment driven by economic
  cycles. During booms, businesses expand and hire; during recessions,
  businesses close and downsize. Labour supply adjusts much more slowly
  than labour demand — during downturns, many workers are forced into
  involuntary unemployment. **Christopher Dow** and **Leslie Arthur
  Dicks-Mireaux** (1950s) demonstrated this empirically and named their
  graphical representation the **Beveridge curve** (after **William
  Beveridge**, founder of the British welfare state, who had nothing to
  do with the curve). It plots job vacancies against the unemployment
  rate: during a downturn, vacancies fall and unemployment rises.

- [MUST KNOW] **Structural unemployment**: caused by a permanent change
  in what the economy needs — workers have skills mismatched to available
  jobs. Classic example: US construction workers post-2007–8 financial
  crisis — housing demand collapsed, but available jobs (bankruptcy
  lawyers) required entirely different skills; bricklaying didn't qualify
  anyone. Takes a long time to fix because it requires retraining from
  scratch. **Key distinction from cyclical**: an economy can simultaneously
  have high unemployment AND high numbers of unfilled vacancies if the
  mismatch is structural.

- [MUST KNOW] **Frictional unemployment**: exists even in a healthy,
  booming economy because of the time lag between leaving one job and
  finding the next. In the UK, between 600,000 and 1 million jobs are
  advertised every three months — people often don't know these jobs
  exist. Technology (LinkedIn, Indeed) has reduced frictions somewhat
  but will never eliminate frictional unemployment. Zero unemployment
  is structurally impossible. Nobel Prize winners **Peter Diamond**,
  **Dale Mortensen**, and **Christopher Pissarides** developed formal
  **search-and-matching models** of how long it takes to match people
  to jobs given information gaps.

### The Phillips Curve and Bargaining Power

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Bargaining power** illustrated via *The Simpsons*,
  "Last Exit to Springfield": Mr Burns offers workers a new contract
  replacing their dental plan with a free keg of beer; Homer rallies
  the union and they strike. The ease with which workers can be replaced
  determines whether they can realistically ask for a raise.

- [MUST KNOW] **Phillips Curve**: relationship between unemployment and
  wages, developed by **William Phillips** in the late 1950s. Phillips
  was an eccentric figure — in 1949 he built an analogue computer that
  ran on **water** (the **MONIAC** — Monetary National Income Analogue
  Computer) to model how money flows through an economy. Core
  relationship: when unemployment is *high*, wage growth is *low*
  (large pool of replacement workers weakens bargaining power); when
  unemployment is *low*, wages *increase* (employers must bid up to
  attract or retain workers — "tight" labour market).

- [MUST KNOW] **Phillips Curve — policy importance and controversy:**
  Used at the heart of economic models for ~50 years; integral to central
  bank policymaking because wage inflation feeds price inflation.
  Controversial: some economists doubt it exists; others think it holds
  some of the time but is unreliable because myriad other factors affect
  workers' relative power. Economy-wide unemployment data may not reflect
  local conditions — workers are not perfectly mobile.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Monopsony in labour markets**: when workers in a
  locality have only one realistic employer (e.g. Springfield Nuclear
  Power Plant), that employer acts like a monopoly buyer of labour —
  it can keep wages and benefits low because workers can't easily leave.
  Workers' relative mobility directly informs their bargaining power.

### Automation and Collective Bargaining

- [MUST KNOW] **Automation and worker bargaining power**: technology that
  replaces human tasks reduces workers' relative bargaining power.
  Important caveat: automation is not always bad — some robots *complement*
  existing workers (making them more productive and raising wages) rather
  than replacing them. *Simpsons* illustration: Mr Burns brings in robot
  workers marked "100% loyal" — they promptly go rogue ("Crush, kill,
  destroy").

- [MUST KNOW] **Collective bargaining**: the key driver of labour power
  is *organisation among workers* — collective power exceeds the sum of
  individual power. Homer convinces co-workers to form a union; their
  collective power exceeds any individual's; Burns eventually accedes.
  Success of strikes depends on **substitutability** — how easily workers
  can be replaced; if replacement is hard (as in Springfield after the
  robots malfunction), strikes tend to succeed. Core rule: a worker must
  add **marginal product** to be employed; to get a pay rise, they must
  demonstrate higher marginal product — it must be more costly for the
  employer to find a replacement than to grant the raise.

### Human Capital and Signalling

- [MUST KNOW] **Human capital** (developed by **Gary Becker**): when you
  invest in learning new skills, you make yourself more productive — and
  in turn more profitable for your employer. Ways to build human capital:
  formal education, on-the-job training, apprenticeships, informal
  learning (even gaming, as the e-sports example illustrates).

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Education and earnings — empirics:** University boosts
  lifelong earnings by hundreds of thousands of pounds; effect is larger
  for commercially focused subjects. In the UK, 5 years after graduation,
  **medicine, economics, and mathematics** graduates earn the most;
  **art, agriculture, and psychology** graduates earn the least. A
  Master's degree earns more than a PhD in the UK — PhD holders typically
  want to be university lecturers (lower pay) rather than investment bankers.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Human capital depreciation and hysteresis:** Human
  capital can *depreciate* — a professional athlete loses fitness after
  injury; long-term unemployed workers can lose skills, motivation, and
  training opportunities. **Hysteresis**: when this depreciation is
  *permanent* — skills lost in a recession never fully recover. Evidence:
  students graduating *during* a recession earn less than those graduating
  during a boom, even ten years later — the scar persists.

- [MUST KNOW] **Signalling theory of education** (**Michael Spence**,
  Nobel Prize winner, NYU): contrarian view — education does not in
  itself boost marginal product or wage; it *signals* to employers who
  is high-value. High-achievers get good grades not because the education
  trained them — they already had high intelligence; the degree signals
  a pre-existing trait, not a created skill. Policy implication: if you're
  an innately low-productivity worker, spending heavily on education is
  a bad investment — it won't actually raise your marginal product.
  **Consensus caveat**: whether education works via human capital (Becker)
  or signalling (Spence), the outcome is the same for the individual —
  a good grade in a useful subject increases lifetime earnings.

## Chapter 4: Why Am I Richer Than My Great-Great-Grandma?

*On the causes of economic growth, the promise and perils of GDP,
and why the economy is more like a cake than you might think.*

### The Time-Machine Opening — Material Progress

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Vivid baseline for economic progress**: imagine a time
  machine drops you in Britain in the mid-1970s. More than half of
  households did not own a car; holidays abroad were rare. In 1977,
  average household earned less than *half* of 2020 income in real terms.
  UK was dubbed "the sick man of Europe"; almost a quarter of the population
  living below the poverty line. Going further back: 1900: average worker
  earned equivalent of ~£75/week in today's money; 1800: ~£25/week. Since
  1970, average lifespan in Britain has increased by **ten years**.

- [MUST KNOW] **Root cause of inter-generational wealth differences:**
  **economic growth** — the rate at which aggregate income increases.
  Growth creates more resources to allocate. The economy is a **cake** —
  growth makes the cake bigger, meaning larger slices for everyone.

### GDP — Origin, Definition, and Formula

- [MUST KNOW] **GDP — origin story**: Great Depression, 1930s — US
  policymakers knew the economy was collapsing but had *no rigorous way
  to measure it*. US Congress brought in **Simon Kuznets** (NBER). Over
  three years from 1931, Kuznets' team produced *National Income* —
  which developed **gross domestic product (GDP)**, now used by every
  country. **Franklin D. Roosevelt's** government used GDP to develop
  economic policies during WWII; other countries then adopted it.

- [MUST KNOW] **GDP — three equivalent definitions:**
  1. Total *value* of everything an economy produces that can be bought
     and sold
  2. Sum of everyone's *income* from producing those goods and services
  3. What everyone in the economy has *spent* in a given period
  - All three measure the same thing from different angles — spending =
    income = value of output in a closed system.

- [MUST KNOW] **GDP formula**: GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
  - **C** = Consumption (households buying goods/services)
  - **I** = Investment (spending on machinery, equipment)
  - **G** = Government spending (roads, health service)
  - **X** = Exports (money flowing in from foreigners buying UK goods)
  - **M** = Imports (money flowing out to buy foreign goods)
  - Net exports (X − M) capture the trade balance contribution.

### GDP — Flaws and Omissions

- [MUST KNOW] **GDP does not measure quality of life** — Kuznets acknowledged
  this himself. Does not measure happiness, environmental degradation, or
  inequality. Famous quote from **Robert Kennedy**: GDP "measures
  everything, in short, except that which makes life worthwhile." An
  economy can be growing while its people are getting unhealthier.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **GDP omits illegal economic activity** — historically.
  In 2014 the UK's **ONS** broadened the definition to include prostitution
  and purchases of illegal drugs, causing measured GDP to jump by ~**£10
  billion** (~5%) overnight — not because anything real changed, but
  because the boundary of "the economy" was redrawn. GDP figures are not
  objective measurements but reflect definitional choices made by
  statisticians.

- [MUST KNOW] **GDP omits unpaid household labour**: In 2016, unpaid
  household work (cleaning, cooking, childcare) was estimated to add
  **£1.24 trillion** to the UK economy — equivalent to **63% of GDP**
  at the time — yet counted as zero. Absurd consequence: if you vacuum
  your own living room, GDP is unchanged; if you and your neighbour each
  pay each other £20 to vacuum for them, GDP rises by £40 — even though
  nothing economically different has happened.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **GDP is an estimate, not a fact:** Statisticians survey
  only a *sample* of economic activity and scale it up — there is simply
  too much activity to capture everything. In the UK, the ONS surveys
  thousands of companies (cars made, houses sold, cheese exported) and
  extrapolates. The headline figure is always slightly off, even when
  it's a very good guess.

### GDP Per Capita and Growth's Benefits

- [MUST KNOW] **GDP per capita**: GDP divided by population — the more
  meaningful welfare measure. France and India have similar total GDPs,
  but France has vastly fewer people — per capita, France is more than
  **twenty times richer** per person. The *slice of cake* each person
  gets is what matters for welfare, not just the total cake size.

- [MUST KNOW] **GDP growth reduces poverty**: One study — when GDP per
  capita *doubles*, income of the bottom one-fifth of the population also
  doubles on average. Between 1981 and 2010, China lifted **680 million
  people** out of poverty — more than the entire current population of
  Latin America — largely through economic growth. **Indonesia example**:
  1975–2020, GDP per capita grew 3.5% per year on average; life
  expectancy rose from 55 to 72 years; infant mortality fell from ~100
  to 20 per 1,000 live births.

- [MUST KNOW] **Scale of global economic growth** (source: *Our World in
  Data*, Oxford University):
  - 1500: world economy ~$431 billion
  - 1700: $643 billion
  - 1900: $3.42 trillion (nearly 8× the 1700 figure)
  - 2015: ~$108 trillion — **250 times** bigger than in 1500
  - Per-person slice in 2015 was more than **15 times bigger** than in
    1500 despite massive population growth. "We're not just richer than
    our ancestors: we're almost inconceivably richer."

### The Four Factors of Production (Solow-Swan)

- [MUST KNOW] **Solow-Swan model** — the dominant growth framework,
  developed by **Robert Solow** and **Trevor Swan**, building on **Adam
  Smith** and **David Ricardo**. Four **factors of production**:
  1. **Land** — physical space and natural resources (iron ore, coal,
     water). Hardest to acquire — fixed asset. UAE built Palm Islands to
     expand land supply. Taiwan's 2021 drought (worst in 50 years)
     strained semiconductor production, which requires large water volumes.
  2. **Labour** — the people producing goods and services. Larger
     populations = more potential workers = more output. US and China
     (the two largest economies) are also the two most populous nations.
     **Ageing populations drag on growth**: Japan (~30% of population 65+,
     86,000+ centenarians) — some economists estimate GDP will decline
     25%+ over 40 years from demographics alone. US: a 10% increase in
     the share of over-60s reduces GDP per capita growth by 5.5%.
     **Gender barriers**: if US women's workforce participation (1970–2016)
     had matched Norway's (76%), the US economy would be ~**$1.6 trillion
     larger** — ~$5,000 more per person.
  3. **Capital** — machines and physical assets that help workers produce
     more. Without scissors and sewing machines, clothes factory workers
     can't make clothes. Capital **depreciates** — equipment breaks,
     software goes obsolete — without continuous investment, growth stalls.
  4. **Technology** — the efficiency with which labour and capital combine
     (total factor productivity). Technology = anything that improves use
     of other factors. Unlike land/labour/capital, technology is
     **non-rival**: one person using knowledge doesn't prevent another
     from using it. **Ha-Joon Chang** (Cambridge) argues the **washing
     machine** has done more to drive growth than the internet: it freed
     people (especially women) from domestic chores, effectively
     **doubling the labour supply**.

### Growth's Dark Side: Three "Bumps"

- [SHOULD KNOW] Politicians and economists are obsessed with growth —
  when it shrinks, it's seen as a national disaster. But growth is NOT
  an unambiguous good. **Simon Kuznets** himself raised the inequality
  problem in a 1955 paper.

- [MUST KNOW] **Bump 1: Inequality — the Kuznets Curve**: Kuznets argued
  in early stages of growth, income inequality *rises*; as economies
  mature, inequality eventually *falls* (inverted-U). Mechanism:
  technological change disrupts old jobs; benefits flow unevenly; some
  workers' skills become obsolete. Example: **supermarket self-checkouts**
  benefit customers (faster) and owners (cheaper than cashiers) — but
  cashiers lose jobs, skilled managers keep theirs and may get pay rises.
  Agricultural-to-industrial transition: factory owners can hire cheap
  rural labour → urban incomes rise, rural incomes stagnate; gap widens.
  Kuznets believed inequality would eventually self-correct. Caveat: many
  modern economists dispute whether the Kuznets Curve self-corrects in
  today's technology-driven economies.

- [MUST KNOW] **Bump 2: Happiness — the Easterlin Paradox**: **Richard
  Easterlin** (University of Pennsylvania) studied happiness data from
  the 1970s onward. First finding: richer people ARE happier; only 25% of
  the lowest income group reported being "very happy" vs. ~50% in the
  highest group. BUT: once a nation's wealth reaches a certain level,
  happiness *levels off* or even *declines* — the **Easterlin Paradox**.
  In Chile, China, and South Korea: GDP per capita doubled but overall
  happiness *fell slightly*. Individual threshold: happiness rises up to
  ~$75,000 salary ($90,000 in today's money); beyond that, further income
  gains do NOT increase happiness. Explanation — **social comparison**:
  people are happy partly because they are richer *relative to others*;
  when everyone gets richer, the relative benefit disappears. Also
  **diminishing marginal utility of income**: a first car dramatically
  improves life; a fourth car barely moves the needle.

- [MUST KNOW] **Bump 3: Environment**: Economic growth consumes natural
  resources — in the short term this improves quality of life, but
  depleting the natural world reduces it long-term. **Easter Island
  parable**: pre-15th century, population of 10,000–20,000 on a thriving
  island. Islanders chopped down forests (for moai-transport tools or
  fishing canoes). Without trees: soil erosion → crop failures → civil war
  → population collapsed to ~3,000 by 1720s; island almost entirely
  deforested by 1722. Overexploitation of natural resources drove
  civilisation collapse.

- [MUST KNOW] Most economic models **omit the environment entirely** —
  don't account for depreciation of natural resources. Studies estimate
  the 2011 value of the **entire biosphere** was $125–145 trillion/year —
  vs. global GDP of $72 trillion at the time; nature is worth more than
  the entire formal economy. Term: **natural capital** — the economic
  value of clean air, clean water, green space, biodiversity. Climate
  change may reduce global GDP by **nearly 15% by 2050** according to
  some studies. 2018 California wildfires cost the US economy ~£110
  billion (~0.7% of US GDP in that year). Growth now could impede growth
  later.

- Chapter conclusion: GDP growth has overwhelmingly improved human welfare
  historically. But it is not unconditionally good: it can bring inequality,
  stagnating happiness, and environmental destruction. Technology is the key
  to **green growth** — investing in green technology can tackle environmental
  damage while continuing to grow GDP.

## Chapter 5: Why Are So Many of My Clothes Made in Asia?

*On comparative advantage, the remarkable power of trade,
and the great bra shortage of 2005.*

### The Global Supply Chain

- [SHOULD KNOW] Everything around us is made in multiple countries: an
  IKEA Billy bookshelf sold in the UK is designed in Sweden, uses wood
  from Poland, screws from China and Poland, manufactured in Germany.
  **Smartphones** travel around the world ~20 times during manufacture,
  drawing on inputs from ~50 countries. Aircraft wings cross borders
  ~12 times before being attached to a plane.

### The Triathlete Analogy: Power of Specialisation

- [MUST KNOW] **Specialisation** — focusing on one narrow skill makes
  you better at it, and frees others to be better at what they do. The
  Bank of England's Jennifer Clark completed the 2021 Europe Triathlon
  Championships in 2h 11m — fast by amateur standards. World record holder
  **Simon Lessing** completed the 51.5km Olympic course in 1h 39m 50s.
  BUT specialist world records beat the triathlete:
  - Swimming (1.5km): **Sun Yang** (China) — 14m 31s
  - Running (10km): **Joshua Cheptegei** (Uganda) — 26m 11s
  - Cycling (40km): **Victor Campenaerts** (Belgium) — ~43m
  If the world's best specialist swimmer, cyclist, and runner each did
  their relay leg, they would beat Lessing's world record by **15 minutes
  and 12 seconds**. Triathletes also lose 30–45 seconds *just changing
  kit* between legs; specialists eliminate this loss.

### Adam Smith's Pin Factory

- [MUST KNOW] **Adam Smith** in *The Wealth of Nations*: specialisation
  by workers is the primary force driving economic efficiency — the
  **division of labour**. Smith visited a pin factory and observed each
  pin requires ~**18 distinct tasks** (drawing wire, cutting, attaching
  head, etc.). If 10 workers each did all 18 tasks: ~2 pins per worker
  per day = ~20 pins total. If each worker **specialised in one task**:
  same 10 workers could produce **48,000+ pins per day**. This insight
  was so important that the *old* £20 Bank of England note depicted the
  pin factory on its reverse.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Why specialisation works** (Smith's three reasons):
  1. **No time lost switching tasks** — not just physically moving between
     stations but mentally getting into each new role.
  2. **Lower training costs** — teach each worker one task instead of
     all eighteen; saves time and money, boosts profit margins.
  3. **Expertise compounds** — repetition builds skill; workers naturally
     gravitate toward the task they're best at.

### From Pin Factory to Global Economy

- [MUST KNOW] Since Smith's time, specialisation has deepened far beyond
  what he imagined: modern factories often produce only *one component*
  of a final product. In 2020, **Apple** sourced parts from nearly
  **30 countries** for iPhones — each country making a highly specialised
  component. The principle scales from workers → factories → **countries**.

- [MUST KNOW] **Countries as specialists** — division of labour at the
  national level. Countries specialise in what they're best at and trade
  the surplus: **Taiwan** (microchips), **Germany** (cars), **France**
  (wine), **UK** (financial services). Without trade, every nation would
  have to produce everything domestically.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Absolute advantage**: one country can produce a good
  more cheaply or efficiently than any other, often due to climate,
  history, or accumulated expertise. Britain has no banana farms: Ecuador
  has an absolute advantage; British climate is simply worse. Specialisations
  self-reinforce: the UK's financial services strength — strong legal
  system + educated workforce → attracted people, who built more expertise.
  **Cremona, Italy** — 16th-century violin-making hub because local
  Lombardy region had maple and spruce (the two essential violin woods);
  sector accumulated momentum; today Cremona produces **80% of Italy's
  exported bowed instruments**.

### Comparative Advantage

- [MUST KNOW] Problem: what if one country is better at *everything*?
  Does the other country have nothing to offer? **Comparative advantage**
  = produce the thing with the *lowest opportunity cost* relative to
  other countries.
  - Example (UK vs Ecuador — strawberries and bananas):
    - UK: bananas cost £1, strawberries cost 50p (bananas cost 2×
      strawberries)
    - Ecuador: bananas cost 10p, strawberries cost 40p (strawberries
      cost 4× bananas)
    - Ecuador has **absolute advantage** in both fruits
    - BUT: Ecuador's opportunity cost of strawberries is very high
      (giving up 4 bunches of bananas per punnet)
    - UK's opportunity cost of bananas is lower in relative terms
    - Optimal: Ecuador focuses on bananas; UK focuses on strawberries;
      both trade → **both countries are better off**
  - This result is **counterintuitive**: even if Ecuador can grow both
    fruits more cheaply, it still benefits from specialising in just
    bananas and trading for strawberries.
  - Even if UK climate were good enough for bananas, land on the south
    coast is scarce — shifting to bananas means producing less wheat,
    potatoes, or strawberries. Opportunity cost makes import still rational.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **David Ricardo** — the economist who formalised
  comparative advantage; born ~50 years after Smith; a wealthy businessman
  who came to economics by reading *The Wealth of Nations*; applied
  Smith's factory-level specialisation to *entire nations*. Comparative
  advantage only delivers its full benefits **if trade exists** — countries
  must exchange with nations that have complementary advantages. Described
  as one of the great theoretical innovations of the 19th century.

### The Modern Scale of Globalisation

- [MUST KNOW] Global trade has increased by more than **4,000% since
  1900**. **Globalisation** = growing interdependence of world economies.
  Keynes (1919) captured the pre-WWI globalised world:
  > "The inhabitant of London could order by telephone, sipping his
  > morning tea in bed, the various products of the whole Earth, in
  > such quantity as he might see fit, and reasonably expect their early
  > delivery upon his doorstep."

- [MUST KNOW] **Why T-shirts are made in Asia**: China's comparative
  advantage is **low labour costs** — average Chinese worker earned
  ~$14,000 (90,501 yuan) in 2019 vs $53,490 average US salary. Fewer,
  more-educated US workers → higher wages → unskilled labour-intensive
  tasks shift to developing economies. China: 128 million manufacturing
  jobs, nearly **one-third of China's GDP**.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Modern trade breakdown** (World Trade Organization,
  2020): physical goods still dominate at **77%** of global trade;
  services = **23%** (exchange of ideas: R&D, legal advice, finance).
  **Digital trade** (email, streaming, social media) is largely NOT
  included in official statistics — if counted, would add **$8.3 trillion**
  in value annually (~20% of total trade flows). In 2020, China exported
  just under **one-third of the world's clothes**; but in services trade,
  the **US exports 14% of world's services** — far ahead of all others.
  China (the largest clothes exporter) is 5th in services, behind France.

### The Dark Side of Free Trade

- [MUST KNOW] **Offshoring and political backlash**: since the late 20th
  century, rich countries saw manufacturing sectors decline as jobs were
  offshored to lower-income countries with cheaper labour. In the mid-20th
  century, T-shirts were probably made in Britain; now they are not.
  Political backlash against free trade has grown across the developed
  world over the past 20+ years. Some countries responded with
  **protectionism** — policies to shield domestic industries from foreign
  competition.

- [MUST KNOW] **The Bra War of 2005**: The **Multi Fibre Agreement**
  (later *Agreement on Textiles and Clothing*) imposed worldwide **quotas**
  on textiles — limiting how many clothes developing countries like China
  could export to developed countries, to protect EU/US clothing
  manufacturers. Early 2005: the Agreement was abolished → quota-free
  trade in clothes began. US saw **100% increase** in clothing imports
  from China; EU saw **200%+ increase** — within months. EU imposed annual
  quotas; China "dumped" more than a quota's worth into Europe within
  months; EU then refused to allow those clothes into shops → result:
  **80 million clothing items stranded in ports** including **4 million
  bras** (hence "the Bra War"), **48 million jumpers**, and **17 million
  pairs of trousers**. Who won? No one: consumers faced shortages and
  higher prices; European retailers couldn't source at prior prices;
  Chinese manufacturers lost market access. EU and China eventually
  compromised: excess counted against following year's quota.

- [MUST KNOW] **Trade wars produce no winners in the long run.** US–China
  trade war (beginning 2018): expected to lower both countries' GDP by
  ~**2%** and global GDP by ~**1%** at the time. EU's longer-term
  solution: shift toward **high-value-added production** (wind turbines,
  electric vehicles) where they have comparative advantage.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Trade blocs as a solution to trade wars**: bilateral
  agreements or multi-country **trade blocs** commit members to shared
  trading rules. Oldest significant bloc: **Zollverein** (German customs
  union, 1834) — before it: ~**1,800 customs barriers** within the
  German Confederation. Modern examples: **EU** (effectively zero cross-
  border shipping costs within members), **USMCA** (formerly NAFTA),
  **RCEP** (Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership, 2020): largest
  ever trade agreement — ASEAN + China, Japan, South Korea, Australia,
  New Zealand; those countries represented ~**30% of world GDP** in 2019.

- [MUST KNOW] **Comparative advantages change over time**: Asia was once
  purely a cheap-labour clothes exporter; it has since become the world's
  primary producer of mobile phones, computers, and solar panels; may
  become leading car exporter within decades. Today's largest producers
  may one day import the very goods they currently dominate.
  Sources of comparative advantage:
  - Resource-based (Switzerland → watches; Belgium → beer; Cuba → cigars)
  - Social/institutional (China → low-cost manufacturing; UK → professional
    services due to skilled workforce and legal system)

## Chapter 6: Why Aren't Freddos 10p Any More?

- [MUST KNOW] **Inflation** = prices across the economy gradually drifting
  upward, eroding the purchasing power of money. Measured by tracking a
  **representative shopping basket** (bread, milk, shampoo, gym memberships,
  etc.). If the basket cost £100 last year and costs £102 now = **2% inflation**.

- [SHOULD KNOW] Not all items move identically. UK examples: coach fares +95%
  since 2010; peanuts +70%; Tower of London entry +60% — vs. men's football
  boots -20%; computer games -30%; laptops -50%+. UK prices rose ~**20%
  cumulatively since 2010** (at time of writing). UK basket compiled by the
  **Office for National Statistics (ONS)**.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **CPI** = most common measure; captures what average
  households consume. House prices NOT included in most headline CPI (you
  don't "consume" a house). The basket varies by country: France includes
  frogs' legs; 1947 UK basket had gramophone records; 2021 UK basket added
  Netflix, hand sanitiser, condoms.

- [MUST KNOW] **Shrinkflation** = producers reduce product size without
  reducing price — economically identical to a price rise. In 2017, UK ONS
  identified **206 products** that had shrunk: fish fingers (12→10 per pack),
  teabags (80→75), toilet roll (221→200 sheets). Toblerone bars were narrowed
  in 2016 → furious consumer backlash → reverted 18 months later.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Quality adjustment problem**: Nokia 3310 (2000) cost £129.99
  vs. iPhone 12 (2020) at £579+ — implies ~450% inflation but the iPhone has
  camera, internet, apps, GPS. On a **quality-adjusted basis**, prices of goods
  in the UK are roughly what they were in **1988**.

- [SHOULD KNOW] The headline inflation rate is an average; **your personal
  inflation rate** depends on your actual spending. People consistently *feel*
  inflation is higher than official measures — partly due to **frequency bias**
  (items bought often, like groceries, are psychologically over-weighted) and
  **negativity bias** (price rises noticed more than price falls).

- [MUST KNOW] 2% annual inflation sounds modest — but it **erodes roughly
  half of your cash spending power over three decades**. Japan contrast:
  wage rises <1% for a decade BUT inflation averaged ~0% → real purchasing
  power still improved. **Lower nominal pay rise + lower inflation can be
  better** than higher nominal rise + matching inflation.

- [MUST KNOW] **Zimbabwe 2008**: inflation of almost **80 billion percent** —
  prices doubled every ~24 hours. Mid-2008: Zimbabwe was running out of paper
  to print money because the paper cost more than the denominations. Old
  watermarks were reused — a 2008 $25 billion note had a watermark intended
  for a $500 bill. By January 2009, highest denomination = **$100 trillion**
  — not enough for a bus ticket.

- [SHOULD KNOW] Hyperinflation has occurred somewhere in the world **on
  average every two years** for the last century. Recurrent examples: Germany
  1920s (contributed to political upheaval leading to WWII), Hungary, Greece,
  Argentina, Venezuela, Lebanon.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Menu costs** = literal cost to businesses of updating prices
  during high inflation. US supermarket study: menu costs equal to as much as
  **one-third of profit margins**. **Shoe leather costs** = high inflation causes
  people to make more frequent bank trips to manage cash, wearing out their shoes.

- [MUST KNOW] **Inflation = a tax on keeping money**. Also simultaneously
  **reduces the real value of debts** — beneficial to borrowers, harmful to
  lenders. If you borrow £100 at 10% interest and inflation turns out to be 20%,
  the borrower gains; grandma loses purchasing power. Debts are set in nominal
  (fixed) terms.

- [MUST KNOW] **Inflation as a redistributive force**: erodes savings (bad for
  savers), reduces real cost of debts (good for borrowers), tends to shift
  wealth from old (asset-holders) to young (borrowers), and tempts debt-laden
  governments to engineer inflation to reduce their own burden.

- [MUST KNOW] **Deflation** seems appealing but is deeply dangerous. US Great
  Depression: prices fell at **10% per year by 1932**. Mechanism: demand
  collapsed → businesses cut production → workers got lower wages → those
  workers also spent less → vicious deflationary spiral. Unemployment hit
  **25%**. "Notoriously difficult to drag out of."

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Deflationary spiral logic**: if something will be 10%
  cheaper tomorrow, rational consumers delay purchasing. Permanently deferred
  demand reduces prices further, making delay even more rational. Self-
  reinforcing and nearly impossible to reverse.

- [MUST KNOW] **Deflation raises the real cost of debt** — the opposite of
  inflation. Borrowed £100, prices fall, that £100 is worth more when you
  repay it. Compounds debt burdens across the whole economy.

- [MUST KNOW] **Optimal target: low, stable, positive inflation**. High enough
  to guard against tipping into deflation; low enough not to risk hyperinflation.
  Most high-income countries target **2% per year**. Near-universal agreement
  that costs become severe once inflation hits ~**10%**.

### Causes of Inflation

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Covid haircut price example**: barbers had to space out
  customers, clean equipment between clients — reducing throughput per chair.
  Price of a haircut rose **more than 8%** in 18 months after the first
  UK lockdown. Illustrates that inflation arises from millions of individual
  business decisions about costs and demand.

- [MUST KNOW] **Two categories of inflation drivers**:
  1. **Cost-push inflation** — forces that push up costs of inputs, forcing
     businesses to raise prices
  2. **Demand-pull inflation** — forces that increase demand beyond the
     economy's ability to supply, allowing sellers to charge more

- [MUST KNOW] **Four major drivers of cost-push inflation**: (1) commodity
  price rises, (2) exchange rate movements, (3) wage increases (when not
  matched by productivity), (4) taxation.

- [MUST KNOW] **OPEC oil embargo, October 1973**: OPEC (mostly Middle Eastern
  oil-exporters) embargoed western countries supporting Israel. Over **five
  months, the oil price tripled**. Oil is used in virtually everything — so
  a rise in oil prices = a rise in the cost of almost all goods. Consumer
  prices rose **over 14% worldwide in 1974**.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Exchange rates and inflation**: when your currency
  depreciates, the cost of imported inputs rises immediately. Swiss franc
  depreciated ~10% in a single day in September 2011 → Swiss chocolate makers'
  costs instantly rose 10% in franc terms.

- [MUST KNOW] **Not all wage increases cause inflation**. Productivity-driven
  pay rises do not inflate costs (worker makes more widgets, cost per widget
  unchanged). Only demand-driven pay rises (company pays more to attract
  workers, no productivity gain) push up costs and may pass through to prices.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Taxation as cost-push**: 2018 UK **sugar tax** → Coca-Cola
  raised prices. January 2011 UK **VAT rise** (17.5% → 20%) → inflation
  jumped from ~3.5% to over **5%**. Without the VAT rise, inflation estimated
  at ~4%.

- [MUST KNOW] **Demand-pull inflation**: when demand for goods outstrips the
  economy's ability to increase supply. Keynes' term: **"animal spirits"** —
  impulsive swings in public mood that drive spending behaviour.

- [MUST KNOW] **Milton Friedman**: inflation is *"always and everywhere a
  monetary phenomenon."* Without an increase in the money supply relative to
  output, prices cannot persistently rise. He and **Anna Schwartz** showed
  statistically that as money supply increased, inflation reliably increased.

- [MUST KNOW] **Quantity Theory of Money**: the amount of money being spent
  must equal the value of things produced. Same quantity of goods + more money
  = higher prices. More money without more real output = inflation, not more
  actual economic activity (**long-run neutrality of money**).

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Monetarism in practice**: became dominant in 1970s-80s —
  adopted by **Margaret Thatcher** (UK) and **Ronald Reagan** (US). Friedman
  advocated a fixed rate of growth for the money supply to achieve constant,
  stable inflation.

- [MUST KNOW] **Keynesian vs. Monetarist divide**: Both agreed that in the
  short run, money creation can boost real economic activity. Monetarists said
  the effect wears off fast → just feeds inflation. Keynesians said the
  adjustment period is long enough to be useful ("in the long run we are all
  dead" — Keynes). By the **early 1990s**, the empirical link between money
  supply and inflation started breaking down.

- [MUST KNOW] **Velocity of money**: the speed at which money changes hands.
  Even with the same money quantity, if it circulates faster, more economic
  activity occurs. Monetarists assumed velocity was stable; post-1980s evidence
  showed it can change a lot and unpredictably — breaking the simple
  money-supply → inflation link.

- [MUST KNOW] **Inflation expectations are self-fulfilling**: if you expect
  high inflation, you demand larger pay rises → those wage rises push up costs
  → which creates the inflation you feared. Conversely, if people believe
  inflation will be low, they accept low pay rises → costs stay down →
  inflation stays low. **(Goodhart's Law:** when a measure becomes a target,
  it ceases to be a good measure — applied to money supply targets.)

- [MUST KNOW] **Why explicit inflation targets exist**: because inflation is
  partly self-fulfilling, central banks (including the **Bank of England**)
  publish explicit targets. If people believe policymakers will hit the target,
  they form consistent expectations — which helps keep inflation at that level.
  "Oddly, by believing they know what inflation will be, they keep it there."

## Chapter 7: What Actually Is Money?

- [MUST KNOW] **Money has existed for ~5,000 years**. Ancient **Mesopotamia**:
  debt systems built around grain storage — workers kept grain in "grain banks"
  (temples). Stone tablet ledgers tracked ownership. You could lend grain or
  exchange claims without physically moving it. This is **money as memory** —
  keeping score of who owes what to whom.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Coins** invented by the ancient **Lydians** ~700 BCE: casting
  precious metal into standard sizes and stamping with an official mark.
  A coin by **King Croesus of Lydia** (6th century BCE) is in the British
  Museum. **Paper money** developed more than a millennium later in China.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Rai Stones of Yap** (Pacific island): massive stones mined
  across dangerous water, valued partly by lives lost in transit. The *record of
  ownership* mattered more than physical possession — stones that sank to the
  seabed still held value because islanders kept records. Pattern repeated
  throughout history: cumbersome commodity money replaced by lighter record-
  keeping. Historical currencies also include cigarettes in POW camps and
  **mackerel in US prisons** in the early 2000s.

- [MUST KNOW] **Three functions of money** (first by **Adam Smith**, ~250 years
  ago — still the standard framework):
  1. **Medium of exchange**: accepted not because you want to consume it, but
     because you can use it to pay for other things. Distinct from barter.
  2. **Store of value**: carries spending power from now into the future —
     a "time machine for spending power." Bananas fail this; gold passes.
  3. **Unit of account**: common measuring stick. Without it, 150,000 products
     would require ~**11 billion prices** in a barter economy (every pair of
     goods). Money collapses this to 150,000 prices: one per good.

- [MUST KNOW] **Double coincidence of wants** — the fundamental problem money
  solves. In barter, you need someone who wants what you have AND has what you
  want, simultaneously. Money breaks this: exchange what you produce for money,
  hold it, then spend it on what you want.

- [MUST KNOW] **Money is fundamentally trust**: "at its core, money is trust.
  Money works because we all agree, as a society, that it has value... It works
  because we believe it works." Workers accept money because shopkeepers accept
  it; shopkeepers accept it because others will too.

- [SHOULD KNOW] UK banknotes have carried **"I promise to pay the bearer on
  demand..."** since **1694**. For most of that history it was a literal promise
  to pay in gold. Now it means the Bank of England guarantees you can exchange
  it for other Bank of England notes — backed by institutional trust, not a
  commodity.

### Commodity Money and the Gold Standard

- [MUST KNOW] **Commodity/representative money**: currency that represents a
  claim on an underlying asset (gold, silver, even a cocoa bean standard in
  South America). Precious metals dominated because they are durable and
  universally valued. Gold's value is largely circular — **Thomas More**
  satirised this in *Utopia* (1516): a society that made gold into toilet pans.

- [MUST KNOW] **The Gold Standard**: UK and colonies in the 1800s agreed to
  exchange currency for gold at specified rates; by end of 19th century most
  of the world had adopted it. Mechanism: economies had to hold enough gold to
  fulfil currency demand → **limits on how much money they could create**.
  **Bretton Woods** (post-WWII): US promised dollars-to-gold at fixed rate;
  other countries pegged to the dollar (effectively pegging to gold).

- [SHOULD KNOW] **President Herbert Hoover (1933)**: "We have gold because we
  cannot trust governments." The Gold Standard as a constraint on government
  money-printing — it anchors currency value and prevents governments from
  inflating away their debts.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Gold Standard's fatal flaw**: changes in gold supply and
  demand were outside policymakers' control → couldn't adjust money supply
  to stabilise the economy during recessions. *Goldfinger* analogy: villain
  tries to irradiate US gold reserves to boost his own gold's value —
  illustrates how gold supply shocks could destabilise the whole currency system.
  - UK left the Gold Standard in **1931**
  - US President **Richard Nixon** broke the dollar-gold link in **1971**

- [MUST KNOW] **The fundamental tension in commodity money**: requiring
  currencies to be backed by a fixed gold supply limits money creation — 
  constraining both inflation AND the ability to respond to economic downturns.
  This tension between "anchor" (price stability) and "flexibility" (crisis
  response) is the core argument for and against the Gold Standard.

- [MUST KNOW] **Fiat money** ("let it be done" in Latin): money with no
  intrinsic value, deemed money by government decree. This has benefits:
  (1) supply can vary as demand fluctuates → keeps prices more stable;
  (2) gold locked up backing currency can't be used for jewellery or computer
  chips — better to use something with very little intrinsic value.
  **Chartalist theory**: fiat money gets value partly because the state lets you
  pay taxes with it → creating reliable built-in demand.

### How Money Actually Exists in Modern Economies

- [MUST KNOW] **Three forms of money** in most modern economies:
  1. **Banknotes** — created by central banks; direct claim on the state;
     anyone can hold them.
  2. **Reserves / narrow money** — electronic money held by banks at the
     central bank; only accessible to banks; used to clear inter-bank debts.
  3. **Bank deposits** — make up **79% of the money supply** and **96% of
     money used by the general public**; NOT a claim on the state — an IOU
     to a **private company** (a bank).
  - Core revelation: **most money we use every day is not created by the Bank
    of England** but by commercial banks — "they simply write it down on a
    spreadsheet somewhere."

- [MUST KNOW] **The "loanable funds" model was WRONG**: old textbook view
  said banks take in deposits and lend them out. Reality: banks don't need
  deposits to make a loan — they simply add money to your account; on the
  other side of their books they hold your IOU (the loan). The money in your
  account "didn't exist five seconds ago." This happens every time a mortgage
  is issued, a credit card used, a business loan granted. **Paying back debt
  destroys money** — the bank cancels your debt AND reduces your bank balance.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Regulation constrains money creation**: bank of England
  regulates how much commercial banks can bring into circulation, and
  influences borrowing demand via **interest rates** → indirect control of
  total money in circulation.

### Bitcoin

- [MUST KNOW] **Bitcoin's origin**: In 2008, **Satoshi Nakamoto** (anonymous)
  published "Bitcoin: a peer-to-peer electronic cash system." Goal: money
  independent from central banks, governments, and commercial banks. Trust
  mechanism: **blockchain**. Total supply **hardwired at 21 million bitcoins**
  (~2041). The very first block referenced a *Times* headline: "Chancellor on
  the brink of second bailout for banks" — a deliberate anti-bank statement.

> "Anyone can create money, the problem is to get it accepted."
> — **Hyman Minsky**, American economist

- [MUST KNOW] **Bitcoin fails all three of Adam Smith's functions of money**:
  1. **Store of value FAIL**: 10x more volatile than most major exchange rates.
     Could be worth £200 or 10p tomorrow — useful for speculation, not spending.
  2. **Unit of account FAIL**: few people set prices in Bitcoin; even in
     **El Salvador** (Bitcoin became legal tender in 2021), prices are listed
     alongside US dollars.
  3. **Medium of exchange FAIL**: transactions much slower than traditional
     systems; can't handle global payment volumes; massive energy cost.
  - **Fixed supply = Gold Standard problem**: rigid supply makes it impossible
    to react to demand changes → significant price volatility.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Private money consistently fails without state backing**.
  US early 1800s "free banks": >90% of notes in circulation in 1830 were
  private bank notes; people didn't trust them outside their local area.
  **Scottish banknotes**: privately issued but backed one-for-one by Bank of
  England money via special notes called "**Giants**" (£1 million each) and
  "**Titans**" (£100 million each) — yet still face suspicion in other parts
  of the UK. **UK local community currencies** (Bristol, Brixton): suppliers
  outside the area won't accept them.

### Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC)

- [SHOULD KNOW] **CBDC**: technology now makes it feasible for central banks
  to offer accounts to everyone. UK cash use fallen **70% in a decade**;
  digital payments dominate but require private bank money. A CBDC would be
  a **direct digital claim on the state** — not a commercial bank IOU.
  - **Benefit 1**: unlike physical cash, a CBDC could **pay interest**.
    **Milton Friedman** identified the inefficiency of holding non-interest-
    bearing paper money; CBDC eliminates this by letting the central bank set
    interest on digital balances.
  - **Benefit 2**: state-backed digital payments — currently only possible
    via private bank money.
  - **Key risk**: if everyone holds CBDC, no one holds commercial bank
    deposits → could destabilise the banking sector, which performs a crucial
    role in keeping the economy running.

- [MUST KNOW] **Three-level answer to "what is money?"**:
  1. **Anything we all agree it is** — gold, banknotes, precious rocks,
     cigarettes, mackerel.
  2. **Functionally**: must fulfil Adam Smith's three criteria.
  3. **In the modern world**: largely a system of IOUs issued by private
     companies (banks), created out of thin air when they lend. "The bulk of
     the money we use every day is nothing more than a promise by our bank to
     pay us back."

## Chapter 8: Why Shouldn't I Hoard All My Money Under the Mattress?

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Israeli mattress story (2009)**: woman surprised her mother
  with a new mattress; the old one — thrown away — contained **$1 million in
  cash** (the mother's life savings, hoarded after distrust of banks following
  2007-8). Daughter searched three tips for a week; money was lost. During
  Covid-19 (2020), nearly **1 in 10 people** started hoarding extra cash
  (Bank of England survey). **2018 YouGov survey**: two-thirds of people said
  they didn't trust banks.

### History of Banking

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Banking origin — Babylonian temple priests (~2000 BCE)**:
  Priests stored people's gold for a fee; by 1800 BCE began lending it out,
  realising most depositors didn't need it all the time. "These business-savvy
  priests had created what is now perhaps the most important business in the
  world." Model spread to Greece, then Rome; dormant for almost a millennium
  after Rome's collapse.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Medieval banking revival — Lombard moneylenders (12th-13th
  century, northern Italy)**: "**Banks**" from *banca* = Italian for bench.
  "**Bankruptcy**" from *banca rotta* = "rotten bench" (when a moneylender
  failed, their bench was broken in half). Initially lent to farmers buying
  grain; by 15th century: deposits in exchange for bills of exchange (precursor
  to banknotes). By 17th century: **Barclays** (England), **Berenberg**
  (Germany) — some became "central banks." **Bank of England** founded **1694**
  to offer loans to the state.

### Three Core Functions of Modern Banks

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Function 1: Storing money**. UK average person (2021): >£5,000
  in savings accounts. Bank of England vaults: ~**400,000 gold bars** worth
  >£200 billion = ~**one-fifth of all gold in the world**; stacked height =
  46 Eiffel Towers.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Function 2: Enabling payments**. UK: >£8 million in
  transactions per second; **£700 billion** of bank account exchanges every
  single day. Banks keep tallies and **settle** what they owe each other at
  end of day via the Bank of England. In 2020, Bank of England processed nearly
  **£92 trillion** of payments.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Function 3: Matching borrowers to savers** — solving
  **asymmetric information**. Borrower knows more about their own
  creditworthiness than any lender; banks are specialists at evaluating this
  risk at scale (a form of market failure from Ch. 2).

- [MUST KNOW] **Banks as specialised credit-risk processors**. Historical
  evolution: 20th century = man in suit interviews you. Today: computers analyse
  gym memberships, pub spending, monthly income. Banks distinguish good loans
  from bad — this expertise is a competitive moat and social function. Interest
  rate = price of the bank's risk assessment: higher default probability →
  higher rate. Banks "inadvertently" allocate capital efficiently while
  screening for creditworthiness.

### How Banks Create Money

- [MUST KNOW] **17th-century London goldsmiths: how banks learned to create
  money**. Goldsmiths issued receipts for stored gold, then realised they could
  lend out deposited gold for a fee. The multiplier mechanism:
  - Goldsmith lends gold → borrower invests → returns some proceeds → deposited
    again → goldsmith lends out again → MORE money in the system than originally
    deposited.
  - Modern banks do the same without a gold constraint.
  - Both goldsmiths and modern banks perform **maturity transformation**: short-
    term deposits (withdrawable anytime) → long-term loans. This mismatch is
    fundamental to banking.

- [MUST KNOW] **Fractional reserve banking**: keeping only a **fraction** of
  deposits in reserve at all times — fine under normal conditions (not everyone
  withdraws simultaneously), catastrophic if confidence breaks. Core tension: by
  definition, a fractional reserve bank has LESS cash than total deposits.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **The Chicago Plan (1930s)**: six University of Chicago
  economists proposed banks hold 100% of deposits at all times ("full reserve
  banking"). Rationale: fractional reserve is inherently fragile because it
  irreconcilably blends two functions: (a) creating money and (b) allocating
  credit. Solution: **separate the two** — leave money creation to government,
  credit allocation to banks as pure intermediaries. Never became law — gained
  traction after 2008, still discussed by IMF economists.

- [MUST KNOW] **The banking paradox: trust is the foundation**. "The modern
  banking system rests on a paradox": we believe banks keep our money safe, and
  mostly they do — but if everyone simultaneously stopped believing and tried to
  withdraw, the whole system would collapse. **It's a Wonderful Life** (1946)
  illustrates the real mechanism: rumors → rational withdrawal → self-fulfilling
  prophecy. George Bailey explains honestly: deposits are loaned out to other
  customers — he cannot give everyone money back simultaneously.

### Bank Runs

- [MUST KNOW] **Northern Rock 2007: anatomy of a real bank run**. September
  2007: BBC News reported Northern Rock was seeking emergency Bank of England
  loan → queues immediately formed outside branches. **Within three days**:
  the first UK bank run since the 19th century. Rational mechanism: even a very
  small chance the bank cannot pay creates a strong incentive to be first to
  withdraw — every person joining the queue increases the actual probability
  the bank will run out. Individual decisions became a **self-fulfilling
  prophecy**. At the time, deposit insurance only guaranteed £2,000 plus 90% of
  the next £33,000 — large savers stood to lose substantially. Post-reform:
  **FSCS** now guarantees **all deposits up to £85,000**. During the Great
  Depression, **7,000 US banks collapsed between 1929 and 1933**.

### The Lender of Last Resort

- [MUST KNOW] **Bagehot's rule**: when facing a bank run, commercial banks can
  turn to the central bank — the "lender of last resort." **Walter Bagehot**
  (Victorian polymath, editor of *The Economist*) wrote the definitive text:
  ***Lombard Street***. Named for the street where **Overend, Gurney and
  Company**, the largest moneylender in the City, was located. **May 1866**:
  Overend Gurney suspended payments → "Black Friday." Bank of England REFUSED to
  rescue Overend Gurney (bad business decisions, not salvageable) but supported
  OTHER banks and brokers → prevented systemic collapse.

  **Bagehot's dictum** (summarized by former BoE Deputy Governor **Paul Tucker**):
  > "To avert panic, central banks should lend early and freely (i.e. without
  > limit), to solvent businesses, against good collateral, and at 'high rates'."
  - *Solvent only*: don't rescue fundamentally broken banks
  - *High rates*: discourage unnecessary borrowing from the central bank
  - *Early and freely*: act fast to prevent panic spreading
  - Analogy: "central banks are a little like economic seat belts"

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Moral hazard in banking: the Peltzman effect**. If banks
  know the central bank will bail them out, they have incentive to take
  excessive risks (private gain, socialised loss). Economist **Samuel Peltzman**
  argued mandatory seat belts would INCREASE car accidents because drivers would
  feel protected and take more risks — "risk compensation." Peltzman turned out
  to be wrong about seat belts in the UK (road injuries fell after compulsory
  seat belts in 1983). BUT the concern has genuine relevance in banking.
  **Gordon Tullock** proposed "Tullock spikes" (a spike in the steering wheel)
  as a visceral skin-in-the-game solution. Banking equivalent: conditions on
  emergency lending + regulation.

### Bank Capital and Regulation

- [MUST KNOW] **Bank capital**: difference between what a bank OWNS (assets)
  and what it OWES (liabilities). Source: shareholders (equity). Purpose:
  protective buffer so banks can absorb loan losses without becoming insolvent.
  Pre-2008 consensus: **banks had too little capital**. Post-2008: UK banks now
  hold **more than 3× as much capital** as before the crisis.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Countercyclical capital buffer (CCB)**: post-2008 innovation
  — capital requirements should be **flexible**, moving opposite to the credit
  cycle. In a credit boom: hold MORE capital (build buffer). In a bust: hold
  LESS capital (free up lending). UK's **Financial Policy Committee** sets the
  CCB; reviewed COVID-19 (March 2020): reduced required capital holdings →
  helped banks keep lending during the shock.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Regulation infrastructure**: **Prudential Regulation
  Authority (PRA)** (part of Bank of England) supervises banks' adherence to
  capital rules. **Financial Conduct Authority (FCA)** also polices compliance.
  **Resolution**: when banks DO fail, there is now an orderly wind-down process
  to prevent contagion. Result: "your money should be safer in a bank now than
  ever before — and certainly safer than if you hoarded it under that mattress."

## Chapter 9: Why Didn't Anyone See It Coming?

- [SHOULD KNOW] Over an 85-year life, you're likely to live through **eight
  economic crises** — double the number of times the average Briton moves house.
  No two crises are the same: fallout from 1929 ≠ 1987. Oldest recorded
  financial crisis: **ancient Rome, 33 CE** — land prices fell, landowners
  couldn't repay loans, lenders stopped loaning.

- [MUST KNOW] **Triggers of economic crises — wide variety, no single cause**:
  speculative bubbles, bank runs, interest rate rises, house price falls, trade
  wars, oil price changes, wars, famines, social unrest, pandemics. Financial
  crises are particularly common and, in recent decades, their implications
  are broader because financial markets are more integrated into the rest of
  the economy.

### South Sea Bubble (1720) — The Classic Speculative Bubble

- [MUST KNOW] **South Sea Company** founded **1711** to trade with Spanish
  colonies — primarily enslaved Africans. Government at war with Spain →
  massive debt → sold debt to the company; shareholders paid annual interest.
  Company **bribed ministers and MPs** to secure the arrangement. By 1720:
  company owned the government's entire **£30 million** of debt. Fundamental
  flaw: South Seas were Spanish/Portuguese colonies; the transatlantic slave
  trade was horrifically risky (high mortality = loss of "property" = loss of
  profits). Despite this, shares soared to nearly **£1,000** (≈£200,000 today).
  Investors: the King's mistress, aristocrats, lower-middle-class Londoners
  alike. Then sudden realisation the company wasn't worth close to the price
  → share value plummeted.

- [MUST KNOW] **Minsky moment**: the tipping point when speculative activity
  reaches an unsustainable extreme — when everyone realises the asset they
  invested in is worth far less than they thought. Named after **Hyman Minsky**,
  whose work was semi-forgotten until rediscovered in the fallout of the
  **2007-8 financial crisis**. A particular event triggers the initial price
  fall → panic → more selling. **Isaac Newton** lost ~£20,000 (>£4 million
  today) in the South Sea Bubble:
  > "I can calculate the motions of the heavenly bodies, but not the madness
  > of people." — Isaac Newton

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Herd behaviour**: people making decisions based on others'
  choices, not independent analysis. Even Newton was swept up when social
  pressure overrode rational calculation.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **How asset bubbles spill into wider crises — and when they
  don't**. South Sea collapse: banks in London went bust as debts went unpaid.
  Same mechanism: 1929, 1987, 2007-8. **The Great Beanie Baby Bubble (late
  1990s)**: soft toys worth ~$5 → one sold on eBay for >$5,000; Beanie Baby
  Bandit (77-year-old) stole $1,200 of toys; one family lost **$100,000**.
  But the Beanie Baby crash didn't cause a wider crisis: only a small number
  of investors, not linked to the broader financial system. **Lesson: bubbles
  only cause economy-wide crises when connected to credit, banks, or housing.**

- [MUST KNOW] **Irrational exuberance** — **Robert Shiller** won a Nobel Prize
  for explaining this: as prices increase, investors want to get in even if
  irrational. If all your friends rave about an investment and say it can only
  go up, it's difficult to believe they're all wrong. Rising prices make people
  more confident → less risk-averse → they double down → prices rise further.
  Self-reinforcing loop explains why bubbles inflate far beyond fundamental value.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Black swan events**: rare, unpredictable catastrophic events.
  **Covid-19**: national lockdowns in 90+ countries; stock market fell nearly
  **10% on 12 March 2020** — worst single-day drop since 2008 crash. During
  2020: **global GDP fell nearly 4%** — likely the biggest contraction since
  the Great Depression.

### The Cost of Crises

- [SHOULD KNOW] The 2007-8 crisis cost every American approximately
  **$70,000**; every Briton **£21,000** — enough to buy every UK person a new
  Ford Fiesta. But averages mislead: crisis costs fall disproportionately
  depending on what triggered it and what policies were implemented.

- [MUST KNOW] **Consequence 1: Fall in confidence → fall in spending**.
  **Alan Greenspan's underwear index**: men put off buying new underwear in
  downturns — former Fed chair used falling underwear sales as a gauge. **Iceland
  2009**: GDP fell 8%; krona lost 50% of its value in a year → McDonald's
  couldn't import beef/cheese/vegetables at viable cost → all **three Reykjavik
  McDonald's closed permanently** (last Big Mac preserved in an airtight glass
  case in a museum, still livestreamed). UK 2007-8 victims: **HMV**, **MFI**,
  **Woolworths**.

- [MUST KNOW] **Consequence 2: Unemployment**. Great Depression: **one in four
  American workers** unemployed. Younger and lower-skilled workers hit hardest:
  workers aged 18–30 experienced higher unemployment AND larger falls in real
  wages than any other group in 2007-8. Long-term scarring: unemployed for long
  periods → permanently lose skills → less employable for decades.

- [MUST KNOW] **Consequence 3: Greater inequality**. Post-2008 (2009-10): top
  1% saw incomes rise nearly **12%**; remaining 99% saw incomes stay roughly
  flat. Post-1929: income share of the **top 5%** rose from just over a quarter
  to more than a third. **Intergenerational inequality**: after 2008-9, the %
  of British children earning ≥ their parents **declined** → inheritance became
  more important in wealth accumulation. Recessions undermine future generations'
  **social mobility**, which itself slows long-run economic growth.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Health and social effects of recessions**. After 2007-8, as
  unemployment rose, **male suicide in the UK increased by more than 15%**.
  Heart attacks and strokes increase in downturns. Children's need for mental-
  health services increased **by half** during the last recession. Divorce rates
  tend to increase after recessions (financial strain). Positive side effects:
  during Covid-19, collapse in Chinese manufacturing led to a **sharp fall in
  air pollution**; during the Great Depression, mortality rates in high-
  unemployment US areas actually **fell** (fewer cigarettes, fewer traffic
  accidents).

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Creative destruction** — **Joseph Schumpeter**: bad businesses
  go bust; resources freed from bad businesses flow to good or new ones. **Dot-
  com crash (2000)**: Nasdaq crashed → hype-based businesses closed → crash
  **accelerated the rise** of genuinely viable online businesses like **eBay**.

### Predicting Crises

- [MUST KNOW] **The economist-as-meteorologist analogy**. Economists are good
  at predicting broad trends (is the economy heading toward boom or bust); bad
  at predicting exact timing or precise trigger. Like a meteorologist who knows
  August is warmer than December but can't predict rain at 3:14pm Tuesday.
  Two warning signs to watch: (1) disruptions in the global economy (bubbles,
  pandemics, unsustainable debt), (2) decreases in consumer or business
  confidence.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Unorthodox leading indicators** economists use:
  - **Cardboard usage**: rising cardboard sales = companies producing more.
  - **RV/camper van sales**: declines in 1989, 2000, 2006 all came shortly
    before recessions.
  - **R-index** (*The Economist*): number of articles in *NYT* and *WaPo*
    mentioning "recession." Higher count → recession more likely. Correctly
    predicted 1981 and 2001 recessions. Reflects the **self-fulfilling nature
    of crises** — they happen partly when more people start worrying about them.

- [MUST KNOW] **The rationality assumption — and why models fail**.
  Economic models assume people are rational (utility-maximising). **Behavioural
  economics**: studies people's *actual* behaviour, which is often emotion-
  driven. Irrationality is why people kept investing in overpriced South Sea
  Company shares. Models are only as good as their assumptions.

- [MUST KNOW] **Economists' dismal record at predicting recessions**. In 2007,
  the Bank of England predicted **1–4.5% growth** for 2008 while noting
  "turmoil in financial markets" — failed to predict a global crisis. **Andy
  Haldane** (former BoE Chief Economist) called this economists' "Michael Fish
  moment" (BBC weatherman who laughed off a hurricane warning in 1987 — that
  night was the most destructive storm since 1703). Damning statistic:
  economists have **failed to predict 148 of the past 153 recessions** around
  the world since the 1990s. Yet models remain the best method available: "a
  flawed model is better than no model at all."

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Why economists missed the 2007-8 crisis — the British
  Academy letter**. On **22 July 2009**, economists wrote to the Queen in
  response to her question about why no one saw the crisis coming. Answer: some
  economists *did* foresee the crisis; they just didn't know the exact trigger,
  timing, or severity. Models were good at predicting small/near-term risks but
  couldn't see systemic interconnection — how falling house prices → bank
  problems → wider economic collapse. Core conclusion: **every crisis is
  different; future crises won't be identical to past ones**. Economists' role
  isn't just to prevent crises but to **react to them** using the policy levers
  available.

## Chapter 10: Can't We Just Print More Money?

- [MUST KNOW] **The scale of modern "money printing"**. Since the 2007-8
  crisis:
  - **Bank of England**: created almost **£1 trillion** of new money = almost
    **£15,000 per person** in the UK.
  - **US Federal Reserve**: created over **$7 trillion**.
  - **European Central Bank**: created a similar amount.

### Monetary Policy: Interest Rates

- [MUST KNOW] **Japan 1990s: why conventional monetary policy hit its limits**.
  1980s Japan = global darling of capitalism. Early 1990s: **two bubbles burst
  simultaneously** — house prices and stock market. Economy slowed → demand
  fell → inflation fell. Bank of Japan needed to stimulate and raise inflation.
  Tool chosen: **interest rates** — standard weapon of monetary policy.

- [MUST KNOW] **How central bank interest rates flow through the economy —
  the monetary transmission mechanism**. Central banks are the bank for other
  banks. When they change their rate, knock-on effects ripple via five channels:

  **Channel 1 — Interest Rate Channel**: central bank rate change →
  commercial banks change what they charge borrowers and pay depositors
  (tracker mortgages move directly; competition forces pass-through for others).
  Higher rates → more incentive to save, less to spend → economy slows.
  Lower rates → opposite.

  **Channel 2 — Wealth Channel**: interest rate ↓ → prices of financial
  and physical assets (stocks, houses) tend to rise → people feel wealthier →
  spend more (e.g. borrow against higher house value).

  **Channel 3 — Income Effects**: works differently for savers vs. borrowers.
  Lower rates → smaller mortgage payments → borrowers spend more (boost);
  but also → lower savings returns → savers spend less (drag). Net effect:
  borrowers tend to spend a **larger share** of new income than savers cut
  spending → rate cuts tend to **boost** overall spending.

  **Channel 4 — Bank Lending Channel**: lower rates → banks offer cheaper
  loans → more demand AND banks more willing to lend (higher collateral values).
  More lending → more money supply → more activity → more inflation.

  **Channel 5 — Exchange Rate Channel**: particularly important for small open
  economies (UK). If UK rates fall but US rates hold, capital flows to dollars
  → pound weakens → imports cost more → inflation rises.

- [MUST KNOW] **Policy lags: the moving target problem**. Monetary policy
  effects on inflation typically take **6 months to 2 years**. Like "a
  sharpshooter aiming at a moving target — must aim ahead of where the target
  will be when the bullet arrives." If today's high inflation is caused by
  something already past and not expected to last, raising rates now would be
  futile — the inflation would fade on its own; you'd merely slow the economy
  unnecessarily.

### Quantitative Easing

- [MUST KNOW] **Bank of Japan hits the lower bound (1991-1995)**: BoJ cut
  rates from **8% to 0.5%** — still not enough. They had hit the **lower
  bound**: the point at which interest rates could fall no further. Classical
  theory: rates can't go below zero because everyone would just hold cash
  (paying 0%).

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Why the lower bound is slightly below zero in practice**: cash
  has real costs — storage, security, logistics. Pension funds need long-dated
  assets, not cash. Some institutions will hold deposits or bonds even at mildly
  negative rates. The real lower bound is **slightly negative, perhaps around
  -1%** in some economies.

- [MUST KNOW] **Quantitative easing (QE) — origin and mechanics**. Japan feared
  negative rates would damage fragile banks and hurt savers (Japan has very high
  savings rates). Alternative: **QE** — also called **asset purchase programmes**.
  Mechanics:
  1. Central bank buys an asset (usually a government bond) from a person
     or company.
  2. It credits the seller's commercial bank account at the central bank with
     new money (adds zeros).
  3. Commercial bank credits the seller's account.
  4. Net result: seller has fewer bonds, more money; central bank has more
     bonds. New money created from scratch — "without the need for a printer."

- [SHOULD KNOW] Before 2008, QE was considered exotic and Japan-specific.
  Economists estimated the world would need QE only **once every 50 years**.
  Then 2008 happened. **Ben Bernanke**, academic expert on the Great Depression
  and Japanese QE, became Fed Chairman in 2007 — just in time. 2008: Fed began
  buying financial assets with newly created money → other central banks
  followed → **QE went global**.

- [MUST KNOW] **Bernanke's famous joke and the QE paradox**:
  > "The problem with QE is that it works well in practice, but not in theory."

  Not a criticism — a recognition that economists know QE lowers long-term
  interest rates and boosts inflation empirically, but don't fully understand
  the mechanism. Analogy: general anaesthetic was used for decades before
  scientists understood how it worked. **Liquidity trap** (the theoretical
  problem): at zero rates, mainstream theory said people already have all the
  money they need; giving them more money-equivalents (bonds that also pay
  zero) would change nothing. Yet QE was working.

- [MUST KNOW] **Two theories of why QE actually works**:
  1. **New Keynesian / forward guidance**: long-term rates are essentially
     the expected average of all future short-term rates. QE signals that rates
     will stay low → lowers long-term rates today. **Michael Woodford**
     (Columbia): you don't even need QE — just a credible promise
     ("**forward guidance**"). QE is useful as a *commitment device* — the
     central bank takes on long-term debt that would cause big losses if it
     raised rates early → self-binding promise.
  2. **Tobin / portfolio rebalancing**: people view bonds and money as
     **imperfect substitutes**. If you swap bonds for money (QE), people now
     have MORE money than they want relative to bonds → they try to pass on
     excess money → prices get pushed up → inflation rises.
  - **Current consensus**: QE works through BOTH channels — signalling AND
    portfolio rebalancing → lower long-term rates → more spending and investment.

- [MUST KNOW] **New Zealand 1989 — the inflation-targeting revolution**.
  New Zealand became the first country to give its central bank an **explicit
  inflation target** set in law — an event that "sent aftershocks around the
  global economy." Background: NZ had double-digit inflation through most of
  the 1970s; politicians kept promising low inflation but hesitating to raise
  rates (unpopular, loses votes) → **inflation bias** → self-fulfilling higher
  inflation. **Ken Rogoff** (economist and chess grandmaster): take rate
  decisions away from politicians and hand to independent central bankers.
  Results: inflation fell to target level within a few years.

- [MUST KNOW] **Bank of England independence 1997**. Within one week of being
  elected, Labour granted the **Bank of England** operational independence →
  created the **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)** with full control over
  interest rates. **Andy Haldane**: before independence, the best predictor
  of UK interest rate moves was "whether Mrs Thatcher had recently suffered a
  bad by-election result." Global inflation track record after independence:
  1970s-80s: averaged >10%; 1990s: ~5%; 2000s: ~3%; 2010s: ~2%. The model
  was so successful it was emulated by "almost every advanced economy in the
  world" within 20 years.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Why QE cannot run forever**: QE is beneficial when inflation
  is below target. Once it pushes inflation above target, further printing
  erodes purchasing power and raises costs. Central banks are **mandated to
  protect the value of money** — they cannot print without limit. The answer
  to "can't we just print more money?" is: yes, but only within the inflation
  target constraint.

### Fiscal Policy

- [MUST KNOW] **Japan's *hakomono* (white elephant) fiscal stimulus**.
  1991-2008: Japanese government spent **$6.3 trillion** on construction-related
  public investments — dams, roads, bridges. Famous example: **$250 million
  bridge** from Okinawa to Kourijima (population: fewer than 400 people).
  Keynes' argument: better for government to pay people to bury money in a hole
  and dig it up again than to leave them unemployed — even absurd spending
  creates economic activity.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Four rationales for government spending**: (1) subsidise
  under-produced goods with positive externalities (e.g. education); (2) build
  large infrastructure with economies of scale; (3) redistribute income;
  (4) **fiscal stimulus** — boost demand when the economy is sluggish.
  Traditional critique: fiscal policy is "**too slow and too political**" —
  infrastructure takes years; politicians incentivised to spend now, defer pain.

- [MUST KNOW] **The fiscal multiplier — Keynes's big idea**. Government
  spending effect can be *greater* than the amount spent, especially when the
  economy is sluggish. Mechanism:
  1. Government pays workers to dig holes
  2. Workers spend some of their income on bread
  3. Baker earns more, spends some of that
  4. Each round adds economic activity
  Each pound of government spending may generate **£1.50 or more** of economic
  output — multiplier > 1. Influenced **FDR's New Deal** (1930s-40s).

- [MUST KNOW] **Three reasons fiscal multiplier may be < 1 (critics)**:
  1. **Monetary policy crowding out**: government spending → inflation risk →
     central bank raises rates → private spending/investment falls.
  2. **Tax financing**: government must eventually fund spending through taxes
     → taxpayers have less to spend.
  3. **Ricardian Equivalence** (named after **David Ricardo**): rational people,
     anticipating future tax rises, rein in spending *today* to prepare →
     completely offsets the stimulus. Most economists think this doesn't fully
     hold in practice (tax payers ≠ spending recipients; people don't actually
     think 20 years ahead).

- [SHOULD KNOW] **When is the fiscal multiplier high vs. low?** Estimates range
  from **less than 1 to well over 2**. Higher when: interest rates are very low
  (monetary policymakers won't offset the fiscal boost), economy is in a
  liquidity trap, spending goes on productive infrastructure. Japan's 1990s
  bridges-to-nowhere may have been less silly than they looked in a high-
  multiplier environment.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Taxation as the mirror of spending**. Taxes reduce disposable
  income → cut demand → slow economy. Cutting taxes puts money in private hands
  → boosts demand → similar multiplier effect. **Progressive taxes** fall
  disproportionately on the rich; **regressive taxes** (e.g. VAT) fall
  disproportionately on the poor.

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Automatic stabilisers — fiscal policy on autopilot**. Budget
  design creates self-correcting mechanisms: economy slows → unemployment rises
  → welfare spending rises automatically → tax receipts fall automatically →
  net fiscal boost delivered without any new legislation. Economy booms →
  opposite. No ministerial action needed.

- [MUST KNOW] **How governments borrow and why it matters**. When spending
  exceeds taxes, government issues **bonds** (IOUs) — bought mainly by
  institutions with long-duration liabilities (pension funds, insurance
  companies). **Argentina default, 26 December 2001**: defaulted on **$95
  billion** → creditors got just 30 cents on the dollar. Consequence: Argentina
  now pays borrowing rates of ~**50%** vs. near-zero for stable governments.
  Serial default history creates a permanent risk premium that compounds
  economic disadvantage.

- [MUST KNOW] **Why the "government debt = household debt" analogy fails**:
  1. **Governments don't die** — can roll debt across generations.
  2. **Investment that grows the tax base**: £1bn motorway generating £2bn GDP
     growth → economy is bigger when debt comes due → debt/GDP ratio *falls*
     even though nominal debt *rose*.
  3. **Growth makes debt manageable**: economy grows at 2%, interest rate is
     1% → can borrow up to 1% of GDP annually and the debt-to-GDP ratio still
     falls naturally.
  4. **Austerity trap**: cut so aggressively that growth slows to zero → the
     debt burden becomes *worse* — the very act of repaying becomes self-
     defeating. Key metric: **debt-to-GDP ratio**, not absolute debt level.

- [MUST KNOW] **IMF fiscal multiplier miscalculation — Greece**. Post-2008,
  many Eurozone countries tried austerity — cut spending, raised taxes to repay
  debt. In Greece: slower growth → tax revenues fell → debt-to-GDP ratio
  *worsened* despite austerity. Then-IMF Managing Director **Christine Lagarde**
  later conceded the IMF had assumed a fiscal multiplier of less than one when
  it was in fact closer to **1.7** — spending cuts shrank the economy far more
  than projected. IMF had pressured Greece to cut ill-advisedly. Symmetric
  logic: **austerity has outsized negative effects** when the multiplier is
  high.

- [MUST KNOW] **100% debt-to-GDP is not a cliff edge**: for the last 100 years,
  UK government debt has been at or above 100% of GDP for **almost half that
  time** — without serious repayment concerns.

- [MUST KNOW] **The limit on printing and the institutional constraint**. El
  Profesor in *Money Heist* argues printing money is equivalent to what the ECB
  did — "a liquidity injection for the real economy." Authors' verdict: partly
  right — money printing can work through multiple channels. But unlimited
  printing produces too much inflation, eroding the value faster than it helps.
  Unlike the bank robbers, the **Bank of England's QE** operates under a
  democratically set **inflation target** — the institutional constraint is what
  separates responsible QE from reckless printing.

## Conclusion: You, the Economist

- [SHOULD KNOW] **Charlene Maines story**: July 2019, 37-year-old charity
  worker from Liverpool attended a **Bank of England Citizens' Panel** in
  Southampton. Pre-event view: economists were "stuck-up, posh and boring"; she
  put on a fake posh accent; economics felt "too complicated" and had deterred
  her from voting in the **2016 Brexit referendum**. Unbeknown to the 25
  participants, the Bank's Governor **Mark Carney** was in attendance. Post-
  event: took evening economics classes, now runs a **charity helping long-term
  unemployed people** back to work, chairs her local political branch, speaks
  publicly about economic policy.

- [MUST KNOW] **The public's relationship with economics**: economists rank
  **second-from-bottom** in Britain's most trusted professions — only politicians
  rank lower. Yet **nearly 50% of people in 2019** reported wanting to increase
  their understanding of economics. The barrier was knowing where to start.

- [MUST KNOW] Core claim of the book: **"You've probably been thinking like an
  economist for your whole life — just maybe you didn't know it."** Economics
  is the formal language for what people already do intuitively. Everyday
  examples throughout the conclusion: the 2020 toilet-paper run = demand-pull
  inflation; lending a friend £20 when they forget their card = money as trust;
  a teenager who knows parents will drive them to school if they miss the bus =
  moral hazard; people queuing for new restaurants partly because others queue =
  herd behaviour.

- [MUST KNOW] **May 2017 poll (pre-UK general election)**: over **half of the
  UK public** didn't feel confident they understood the economic impact of the
  policies they were voting on — a democracy-level problem, not just a personal
  one. The stakes of economic illiteracy: voting for policies that don't work
  for you, storing savings in the wrong place, missing career opportunities.
  Conversely, **economic literacy can make you wealthier, healthier, and
  happier**.

- [MUST KNOW] Economics needs **challenge from all sides** — not just from maths
  experts but from "amateur economists" who notice the irrationality that
  sometimes grips the profession itself (animal spirits). The field needs
  economists who **break the mould, bring fresh ideas, and have different
  experiences** — diverse perspectives are a feature, not a bug.

> "You are the economy and so you have the power to shape it and be shaped by
> it. Along the way, you might just help create a better society for everyone."
> — Patel & Meaning, *Conclusion*
