### The Queen's Question

- [MUST KNOW][N112] **Why economists missed the 2007-8 crisis —
  the specific and general answers**
  - November 2008: **Queen Elizabeth II** visited the London
    School of Economics to open a new building; during height
    of the worst financial crisis since the 1930s, she asked
    assembled economists: "Why did no one see it coming?"
  - The question occupied economists for an entire decade:
    university lecture halls, economic journals, broadsheet
    columns, TV debates; the few who DID see it coming became
    "economic celebrities overnight"
  - **Specific answer**: 2007-8 crisis fundamentally caused
    by banks giving mortgages to people with no hope of
    repaying; economists missed it because they failed to
    "join up the dots" — didn't realize a small number of
    unpaid loans would cascade through the whole economy
    because of how deeply intertwined banks were with other
    sectors
  - **LSE economist Luis Garicano** (to whom the Queen
    addressed the question) later wrote in *The Guardian*:
    > "What I told the Queen is that the reason the situation
    > got out of hand is that those working at every point
    > in the lending chain were eager to continue doing the
    > job they were paid to do."
  - **General answer**: economic crashes are chaotic,
    unpredictable, and have occurred in each of the last
    seven decades; economists have been **wrong more often
    than right** in predicting them
  - Base rate: over an **85-year life**, you're likely to
    live through **8 economic crises** — double the number
    of times the average Briton moves house

- [MUST KNOW][N113] **1929 vs. 1987: financial crises don't
  always cause economic crises**
  - **Black Thursday, 24 October 1929**: New York Stock
    Exchange fell ~10% within first hours of opening;
    followed by Black Monday and Black Tuesday → total
    5-day fall of **25%** (Black Monday "remains one of
    the greatest percentage falls on record")
  - Led to the **Great Depression**: mass unemployment,
    spiralling poverty, soup kitchens; global GDP per capita
    fell **~20% in 1930-2**; Wall Street crash affected
    not just Americans but 526 million Europeans and billions
    more globally despite geographic distance from trading
    floors
  - Flash-forward: **Black Monday, 19 October 1987**: stock
    market crashed 23%, knocked **$1 trillion** off US
    market value; *Variety* ran the identical 1929 headline
    "Wall Street Lays an Egg"
  - BUT: NO recession followed — US GDP growth hit **4%**
    in both 1988 and 1989; 1987 was an economic boom year
    despite the financial crash
  - **Key distinction**: financial crises ≠ economic crises
    - Economic crisis = GDP contraction (if 6+ months:
      **recession**)
    - Only **half** of financial sector crises over the
      past 100 years resulted in a recession
  - Implication: need to understand what determines whether
    a financial shock spreads to the real economy — that is
    the central question of Ch. 9

### Causes and Frequency of Economic Crises

- [SHOULD KNOW][N114] **Economic crises: variety, triggers,
  and ancient history**
  - No two crises are the same: "the fallout from 1929 was
    very different to that of 1987" — and each of the 13
    US stock market crashes since 1929 was different again
  - Common triggers: speculative financial bubbles, bank runs,
    interest rate rises, house price falls, trade wars,
    oil price changes, wars, famines, social unrest,
    pandemics — "or a combination of them all"
  - Pattern of crises: sometimes economy gradually expands
    then gradually contracts; sometimes tanks fast and
    recovers almost immediately — no reliable shape
  - **Oldest recorded financial crisis**: ancient Rome, **33 CE**
    - Land prices fell → landowners couldn't repay loans
      secured against land → lenders stopped loaning money
      → credit freeze across the economy
  - Modern amplification: financial markets are more
    integrated into the rest of the economy than ever before,
    so financial crises are now more likely to cause
    full-blown economic crises than in ancient times

### The South Sea Bubble: A Template for Speculative Crises

- [SHOULD KNOW][N115] **South Sea Bubble (1720): government
  debt, bribery, and speculative mania**
  - **South Sea Company** founded **1711** to trade with
    Spanish colonies in South America — primarily by
    bringing enslaved Africans to work in mines and
    plantations (transatlantic slave trade)
  - UK government context: long-running war with Spain was
    expensive; solution = sell government debt to South Sea
    Company; government would then pay annual interest fees
    to company's shareholders
  - Company **bribed ministers and MPs** to secure the deal;
    by 1720 it had bought and owned the government's entire
    **£30 million of debt**
  - Fundamental economic problems with the scheme:
    - South Seas were Spanish/Portuguese territory → income
      from trade was uncertain and difficult to achieve
    - Transatlantic slave trade had horrifically high
      mortality rates → loss of human "property" → profits
      too unreliable to back stable government finance
    - "While the slave trade could realise large profits
      for individual investors, on the whole it was far too
      risky to function as a secure footing for government
      finance"
  - Despite (or because of?) flawed fundamentals, shares
    soared to nearly **£1,000** (≈ **£200,000 today**);
    investors ranged from aristocrats to lower-middle-class
    Londoners; notable investors included the King's
    mistress **Duchess of Kendal** and **Thomas Pitt,
    Earl of Londonderry**
  - Speculation was "rife" — story continues beyond p200
