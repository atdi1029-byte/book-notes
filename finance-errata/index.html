How to Read This Guide

This document fills in every topic that the four finance guides — ATLAS The Asset Atlas, PLAYBOOK The Portfolio Playbook, INCOME The Income Architect, and FORGE The Metal Forge — reference, imply, or logically need but never fully explain. Each entry teaches the concept directly: what it is, how it works, and why it matters for your portfolio.

Portfolio Playbook

PLAYBOOK

Ch 2 — Cash, TIPS, currency hedging, and crypto: T-bills are the closest thing to a risk-free asset. They mature in weeks to months, their yield resets with Fed policy, and they carry zero credit risk because they’re backed by the US Treasury. You can hold them through SGOV (iShares 0–3 month Treasuries), USFR (WisdomTree floating-rate Treasuries), or buy them directly on TreasuryDirect. TIPS — Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities — are bonds whose principal adjusts with CPI, meaning they protect against inflation in a way nominal bonds can’t. But they have quirks: “phantom income” means you owe taxes on inflation adjustments to principal even though you haven’t received any cash, which is why TIPS belong in tax-advantaged accounts. They also have a deflation floor — the principal can’t drop below par at maturity — and the key metric is real yield rather than nominal yield. When real yield is positive, TIPS guarantee purchasing-power growth; when negative, you’re paying for inflation protection.

When you buy international stocks through a fund like VXUS, you’re implicitly taking a position in foreign currencies. If the dollar strengthens, your international returns shrink even if foreign stocks rise. Hedged ETFs like HEFA (developed markets hedged) and DXJ (Japan hedged) strip out this currency risk. The home-bias debate — whether Americans overweight US stocks because they’re familiar rather than because it’s optimal — is central to how much international exposure to hold. Crypto should be evaluated on the same correlation and drawdown framework applied to every other asset class, with a clear sizing rule: either 0% (skip it entirely) or 1–3% capped, with a rebalancing plan that accounts for 70%+ drawdowns.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 3–4 — Period mismatch and missing portfolios: The comparison tables in Chapters 3 and 4 are hard to use for apples-to-apples comparison because each portfolio uses a different measurement period. One shows data from 1926–2024, another from 1972–2020, another from 1978–2020, and others use vague labels like “30-year” or “10-year.” A portfolio that looks great over 1926–2024 (which includes the post-WWII boom) may look very different over 1972–2020 (which starts with stagflation). The fix: a common-period table — say, 1978–2024 — where every portfolio gets the same ruler.

Several well-known portfolios deserve a spot. NTSX is a “capital-efficient” fund that uses futures to deliver 90/60 exposure (90% stocks, 60% bonds) in a single fund — effectively a leveraged 60/40. RPAR does something similar for a risk-parity portfolio. The Larry Portfolio (named after Larry Swedroe) is a classic small-cap-value-heavy allocation that tilts hard into the factors academic research says have the highest premiums. The Faber Ivy Portfolio replicates the endowment-style approach of Yale and Harvard using five equal-weighted asset classes with a trend-following overlay. These are among the most-discussed portfolios in the investing community.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 5 — 401(k) translation: Most readers’ largest investment account is a 401(k) with a limited menu of 15–25 mutual funds chosen by their employer. You can’t buy VTI or VXUS in most 401(k)s. Here’s the translation: VTI maps to VTSAX or FSKAX, VXUS maps to VTIAX or FTIHX, BND maps to VBTLX. With these equivalents, any model portfolio from Chapter 4 can be built in a real 401(k). Additional instruments worth knowing: SGOV and USFR as T-bill and floating-rate alternatives for cash management, VT as a single-fund global solution for people who want maximum simplicity, and AVDV as the Avantis international small-cap value fund that implements factor tilts.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 6 — Life-stage nuances: Social Security and pensions are bond-like assets that most people ignore when calculating their allocation. A $30,000/year pension, capitalized at 4%, is equivalent to holding $750,000 in bonds. Someone with a generous pension can afford a much more aggressive stock allocation than their age alone would suggest, because they already have a huge implicit bond position.

Goals with horizons under 10 years — saving for a house down payment, a child’s tuition, a wedding — need goal-based buckets with their own allocations, separate from the long-term retirement portfolio. A 25-year-old saving for a home in 3 years shouldn’t have that money in an age-based equity-heavy allocation. The rising equity glide path concept (increasing stock allocation in retirement rather than decreasing it) also needs a caveat: the research shows the effect is small, and it assumes the retiree is psychologically comfortable holding 70–80% stocks at age 80, which most people are not.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 7 — Cross-account rebalancing: Most people have 3–5 accounts: a 401(k), a Roth IRA, a taxable brokerage account, an HSA, and possibly a spouse’s accounts. The critical insight: treat all of these as one unified portfolio and do your rebalancing trades inside the tax-advantaged accounts (401k, Roth, HSA) where buys and sells generate no taxable events. This is free alpha — you maintain your target allocation without creating tax drag. Someone who rebalances each account independently might have the right overall allocation by accident, but they’re almost certainly generating unnecessary capital gains in their taxable account.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 8 — Regime identification: Chapter 8 describes four economic regimes (rising growth/rising inflation, rising growth/falling inflation, etc.) and explains which assets do well in each. But how do you identify which regime you’re currently in? PMI tells you growth direction: above 50 means expansion, below 50 means contraction, and the trend matters more than the level. CPI tells you inflation direction. The yield curve reflects market expectations — an inverted curve (short rates above long rates) signals the market expects rate cuts, usually because it sees a recession coming. The Atlas Layer 14 indicator dashboard is designed for exactly this purpose. Note: 2021–2022 was actually a classic rising-inflation/slowing-growth setup (GDP decelerating from the post-COVID surge while inflation was spiking), not a “rising growth” regime.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 9 — Retirement income tools: Beyond the safe withdrawal rate (3.7% baseline), several tools can dramatically improve retirement outcomes. A TIPS ladder — buying individual TIPS bonds maturing in each year of early retirement — creates an inflation-protected income floor that no stock market crash can touch. SPIAs (Single Premium Immediate Annuities) provide guaranteed lifetime income: you give an insurance company a lump sum and they pay you monthly until you die, no matter how long you live. This is longevity insurance — the only product that eliminates the risk of outliving your money.

Withdrawal sequencing — which account to draw from first — can add years to portfolio longevity. The general rule: taxable first (for lower tax rates on long-term gains), then traditional IRA (ordinary income), then Roth (tax-free, let it grow longest). But Roth conversions during the “gap years” between retirement and age 73 (when RMDs start) can permanently reduce your lifetime tax bill by converting traditional IRA money to Roth while you’re in a low tax bracket. Social Security claiming age (62 vs 67 vs 70) is itself a portfolio lever: each year you delay past 62 increases your benefit by 6–8%, which is effectively a guaranteed real return that no other asset matches.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 10 — Tax tools: Treasury bonds and bills are exempt from state and local income taxes. In a state like California (13.3% top rate) or New York (10.9%), this is a material benefit that changes the tax-equivalent yield calculation significantly. A Treasury yielding 4% in New York is equivalent to a corporate bond yielding roughly 4.5% pre-state-tax.

Municipal bonds (VTEB is the Vanguard tax-exempt bond ETF) are the single most tax-efficient fixed-income instrument for high earners — their interest is exempt from federal income tax and often state tax too. For someone in the 37% federal bracket, a muni yielding 3.5% is equivalent to a taxable bond yielding 5.6%. The HSA (Health Savings Account) is the only account with a triple tax advantage: tax-deductible contributions, tax-free growth, and tax-free withdrawals for medical expenses. After age 65 it functions like a traditional IRA for non-medical spending.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 11 — Return measurement: There are two fundamentally different ways returns are calculated, and confusing them leads to systematically misunderstanding your own performance. Time-weighted return (TWR) measures how the fund performed regardless of when you added or withdrew money — it’s what Morningstar and fund fact sheets report. Money-weighted return (XIRR) measures what you actually earned given your specific pattern of deposits and withdrawals. These can diverge dramatically: if you invested $10,000 in January, the fund dropped 30%, and then you added $50,000 at the bottom, your money-weighted return could be strongly positive even if the fund’s time-weighted return was negative for the year. Most brokerages show one or the other without labeling which kind it is.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 12 — Common mistakes: Three of the most wealth-destroying retail mistakes. Employer-stock concentration: holding too much company stock in your 401(k) means your salary, your benefits, and your retirement savings all depend on one company. Enron employees learned this the hard way when the stock went to zero and their 401(k)s went with it. Margin and leverage: borrowing against your portfolio amplifies returns in both directions, and a 50% drop on 2x leverage wipes you out entirely, not just painfully. “Waiting for a dip” cash drag: the opportunity cost of sitting in cash waiting for a pullback that may not come for years. Studies consistently show that lump-sum investing beats waiting for a dip roughly two-thirds of the time, because markets trend upward and the “perfect entry” you’re waiting for usually doesn’t compensate for the gains you missed while waiting.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 14 — Emergency playbook: “I lost my job and need cash now” is the most stressful financial scenario you’ll face and the one where you most need clear guidance. The liquidation order: emergency fund first, then taxable brokerage, then Roth contributions (which can be withdrawn tax- and penalty-free at any time), then a 401(k) loan as a last resort. Consider whether to sell investments or use a HELOC or margin loan as a short-term bridge. Sequence your spending cuts. Stop contributing to retirement accounts temporarily if needed — the employer match is free money, but beyond that, cash flow matters more than retirement contributions during a crisis. People in crisis make their worst financial decisions, and having a pre-written playbook prevents panic selling at exactly the wrong time.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 15 — Bond depth: Convexity describes how bond prices respond non-linearly to interest rate changes — a 1% rate drop adds more to a bond’s price than a 1% rate rise takes away. This is why long-duration bonds are sometimes described as having “positive convexity” and why they can act as crash insurance. Starting yield is the single best predictor of a bond’s forward return over the next 10 years — much more reliable than any stock-market valuation metric. If you buy a 10-year Treasury at 4.5%, you will almost certainly earn something close to 4.5% annualized over the decade.

Individual Treasury bonds and bond ladders behave differently from bond ETFs in a critical way: an individual bond matures at par (you get your money back), while a bond ETF has no maturity date and continuously rolls into new bonds, meaning it can stay underwater for years in a rising-rate environment. TLT (the 20+ year Treasury ETF) demonstrated this brutally: its 2020–2023 drawdown was roughly −45%, worse than many stock-market crashes. That’s what −45% on a “safe” bond fund actually looks like.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 16 — Home economics: A mortgage is a negative bond: you’re effectively short fixed-income duration. If you have a $400,000 mortgage at 3%, you’re paying a fixed rate and benefiting when rates rise (because your locked-in rate looks better relative to new rates). This changes your overall portfolio’s effective duration and should influence how much you hold in bonds. The pay-down vs invest decision is one of the most common financial questions: if your mortgage rate is 3% and you expect 7–8% from stocks after tax, investing the difference wins on paper. But the guaranteed 3% return from paydown has value, especially for risk-averse investors or those approaching retirement.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 17 — Tax-loss harvesting depth: The wash-sale rule says you can’t claim a loss if you buy a “substantially identical” security within 30 days. The workaround: substitute pairs. You sell VTI at a loss and immediately buy ITOT (iShares total market) — same exposure, different fund, no wash sale. Similarly, VXUS swaps with IXUS for international. These pairs let you harvest losses without ever being out of the market.

Tax-gain harvesting is the opposite strategy and equally powerful: in years when your taxable income is low enough to fall in the 0% long-term capital gains bracket (roughly under $89,250 for married filers in 2024), you can sell appreciated stock, pay zero federal tax on the gain, and immediately repurchase it at a higher cost basis. It’s essentially a free Roth conversion for your taxable account. The Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) adds a 3.8% surcharge on investment income above $250,000 (married), which changes the math for high earners. Donor-advised funds let you donate appreciated stock and take a deduction for the full market value without ever paying capital gains tax on the appreciation. Direct indexing — holding individual stocks instead of an index fund — unlocks continuous tax-loss harvesting at the individual-stock level, particularly valuable for portfolios with large embedded gains.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 18 — Fund costs: The expense ratio is the stated annual fee a fund charges, but tracking difference — the actual gap between an ETF’s net return and its benchmark index — is a more complete measure of cost. Tracking difference captures everything: the expense ratio, securities lending revenue (which can offset or even exceed the ER), sampling error from holding a subset of the index, cash drag from uninvested dividends, and transaction costs from rebalancing. Some funds with higher expense ratios actually have better (tighter) tracking difference than cheaper competitors because they earn more from securities lending or track their index more precisely. Comparing funds only by ER misses the full picture.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 19 — Automation and the spouse test: The biggest risk to any investment plan isn’t a market crash — it’s that the investor stops following it. Auto-invest features (available at Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard, and especially M1 Finance) remove the behavioral risk of “I’ll rebalance next month.” You set your target allocation once and the platform handles contributions and rebalancing automatically.

Equally important: a written investment plan simple enough that a spouse or executor with zero financial knowledge could follow it. “Put 60% in VTI and 40% in BND, rebalance once a year in January” is a complete plan that survives the death or incapacitation of the primary financial planner. This is morbid but essential.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 20 — Day One prerequisites: Before you invest a single dollar, you need an emergency fund of 3–6 months of living expenses in cash or a high-yield savings account. Without this, the first car repair or medical bill forces you to sell investments, potentially at a loss, potentially with tax consequences.

Account sequencing priority: 401(k) up to employer match (free money) → HSA to max (triple tax advantage) → Roth IRA to max (tax-free growth) → back to 401(k) to max → taxable brokerage. Getting this wrong — like maxing out a taxable account before getting the employer match — can cost tens of thousands of dollars over a career in lost matching and tax benefits.

Asset Atlas

ATLAS

L0 — Market plumbing: The Treasury General Account (TGA) is the government’s checking account at the Fed. When it fills up (debt ceiling resolved, big tax receipts), it drains liquidity from the banking system; when it empties (spending exceeds revenue), it adds liquidity back. The bill-vs-coupon issuance mix matters because heavy bill issuance pulls money from the reverse repo facility (adding reserves) while coupon issuance pulls money from the real economy (draining reserves). These mechanics move risk assets on a multi-week timescale.

Global central banks matter enormously. The Bank of Japan (BOJ) held rates negative for years and its July 2024 surprise hike triggered the yen carry trade unwind — the biggest cross-asset shock of the year, sending the Nikkei down 12% in a day and rippling through US equities. The ECB and PBOC have their own cycles that affect global capital flows. Vol-control funds, CTAs (trend-following commodity trading advisors), and risk-parity strategies mechanically buy and sell based on realized volatility — when vol rises, they sell, amplifying the move. Leveraged ETFs (like TQQQ) must rebalance at the close every day, creating predictable buying or selling pressure in the last hour of trading. Vanna (how delta changes with implied volatility) and charm (how delta decays with time) create options-driven flows around OPEX dates. VIX expiration Wednesdays specifically create gamma-driven moves as market makers unwind hedges.

ATLAS

L1 — Foundation additions: The Russell 2000 (IWM) has a well-known problem: roughly 40% of its components are unprofitable companies, which drags down the entire small-cap asset class. IJR (S&P SmallCap 600) and VBR (Vanguard Small-Cap Value) both screen for profitability, filtering out the junk and historically producing better returns. VT (Vanguard Total World Stock) holds every investable stock on earth in a single fund — the simplest possible equity allocation. AVUV (Avantis US Small Cap Value) is a factor ETF that belongs with foundational building blocks, not buried among individual stocks.

ATLAS

L2 — Fixed income depth: Agency mortgage-backed securities (MBS) are one of the largest fixed-income markets in the world, backed by pools of home mortgages and implicitly guaranteed by the US government. MBB and VMBS are the main ETFs. MBS have negative convexity: when rates fall, homeowners refinance and you get your principal back early (just when you want to stay invested); when rates rise, nobody refinances and you’re stuck holding a below-market coupon longer.

The yield curve — how interest rates vary by maturity from 1 month to 30 years — is the single most important chart in fixed income. A normal curve slopes upward (longer = higher yield), an inverted curve slopes downward (the market expects rate cuts, usually because recession is coming), and a flat curve signals uncertainty. The term premium is the extra yield for holding longer maturities. Fed funds futures and SOFR futures let you see what the market expects the Fed to do at each upcoming meeting.

Key instruments: USFR and TFLO (floating-rate Treasury ETFs that reset with Fed policy, so their price barely moves). ZROZ and EDV (ultra-long-duration zero-coupon Treasury strips, effectively 25–30 year duration — the most volatile bonds available, used as crash insurance). EMLC (emerging-market local-currency bonds, which add both credit and currency risk but historically higher yields). Target-maturity bond ladders like iBonds (iShares) and BulletShares (Invesco) hold bonds maturing in a specific year and then return your principal — the maturity certainty of individual bonds with the diversification of a fund.

ATLAS

L3 — Sectors: Independent power producers are the biggest sector story of 2024–2025. Vistra (VST), Constellation Energy (CEG), and NRG Energy own and operate power plants. The AI data center buildout has created enormous demand for electricity, making these some of the best performers of the period.

Other sectors worth knowing: autos (cyclical manufacturing exposed to rates, EV transition, and tariffs), OIH (oil services — the companies that drill, not the ones that own the oil), XME (metals and mining, linking to the Metal Forge), and ITB (homebuilders, one of the most rate-sensitive sectors). Private credit and BDCs (business development companies) have grown into a trillion-dollar market — BIZD is the ETF that holds a basket of publicly traded BDCs, offering exposure to direct lending at a yield typically 8–11%.

ATLAS

L4 — Commodities: The 2024 cocoa spike is the best real-world example of contango and backwardation in recent memory: cocoa futures went from ~$2,500/ton to over $10,000 as West African harvests failed, and the extreme backwardation (spot price far above futures) meant commodity ETFs actually outperformed spot for once. This is the go-to case study for understanding these concepts.

REMX (rare earth and critical minerals ETF) covers the metals essential for EVs, wind turbines, and military hardware — lithium, cobalt, neodymium, dysprosium. China controls the refining for most of them, making this geopolitically important. Physical uranium (Sprott’s SRUUF or the Kazatomprom ADR) is the direct play on nuclear energy’s comeback. KRBN (carbon credits ETF) tracks the price of EU and California emission allowances, creating a financial asset out of climate regulation. The Baltic Dry Index measures shipping rates for bulk commodities and serves as a real-time indicator of global trade demand. The broader commodity supercycle framework: periods of under-investment lead to supply shortages, which lead to price spikes, which lead to over-investment, which leads to oversupply — and the cycle repeats.

ATLAS

L5 — Currencies: Currencies are a major asset class beyond just UUP (the US dollar bullish fund). FXE (euro), FXY (Japanese yen), FXB (British pound), FXF (Swiss franc), and CYB (Chinese yuan) each move on different fundamentals — ECB rate differentials, BOJ intervention, Brexit aftermath, safe-haven flows, and PBOC management respectively. The carry trade concept: borrowing in low-rate currencies (yen, Swiss franc) and lending in high-rate currencies (Brazilian real, Mexican peso, Turkish lira) to earn the rate differential. This is one of the most popular institutional strategies and it periodically blows up spectacularly (as it did in August 2024). Country ETFs: ARGT (Argentina, a frontier-market turnaround story under Milei) and EWQ (France, the largest eurozone pure-play).

ATLAS

L6 — Volatility depth: The short side of volatility is a distinct strategy. SVIX and SVXY are inverse VIX ETFs that profit from the VIX term structure being in contango (which it is ~80% of the time), providing a steady positive return punctuated by catastrophic drawdowns. The February 2018 XIV “Volmageddon” is the essential cautionary tale: XIV lost 96% of its value in a single day when the VIX spiked from 17 to 37, wiping out years of steady gains and billions in investor wealth. Required reading for anyone considering short-vol strategies.

Single-stock leveraged ETFs like NVDL (2x Nvidia daily) and TSLL (2x Tesla daily) amplify both returns and the corrosive effects of daily rebalancing and volatility decay. TMF (3x long 20+ year Treasuries) is the leveraged bond equivalent. Inverse ETFs like SH (inverse S&P 500) and PSQ (inverse Nasdaq) let you bet against the market without shorting. VVIX (the volatility of VIX) and SKEW (which measures the pricing of deep out-of-the-money put options) are second-order indicators that tell you how nervous institutional hedgers are about tail risk. When SKEW is elevated, big money is paying a premium for crash protection even if the VIX itself looks calm.

ATLAS

L7 — Crypto and thematics: Bitcoin and Ethereum spot ETFs (IBIT from BlackRock, FBTC from Fidelity, ETHA for Ethereum) launched in 2024 and accumulated tens of billions in AUM within months, making them among the most successful ETF launches in history. MicroStrategy (MSTR) holds over 200,000 Bitcoin on its balance sheet and trades as a leveraged Bitcoin proxy. WGMI holds Bitcoin mining companies. BLOK is a broader blockchain technology ETF.

Other thematic ETFs: QTUM (quantum computing and AI), GRID (clean energy infrastructure), SRVR/DTCR (data center REITs). YieldMax single-stock income funds deserve a warning: they sell options on individual stocks (TSLY on Tesla, NVDY on Nvidia) to generate eye-catching distribution rates of 30–80%, but the distributions are largely return of capital, the NAV erodes steadily, and the total return typically underperforms just holding the underlying stock. Many retail investors don’t understand this.

In healthcare: CVS (pharmacy + insurance vertical integration), HCA (largest for-profit hospital chain), MCK (McKesson, pharmaceutical distribution). In industrial/consumer: ADP (payroll processing, a labor-market indicator), ORLY and AZO (auto parts retail, recession-resistant), CSX (railroad, economic bellwether), DAL and UAL (airlines), MAR and HLT (hotels), RCL and CCL (cruise lines — the most leveraged consumer discretionary play).

In financials: COF (Capital One, credit card / consumer lending), HOOD (Robinhood, retail trading), USB/PNC/TFC (regional banks, important after the 2023 banking crisis). In energy: EPD, ET, and KMI (midstream pipelines — toll-road businesses that earn fees regardless of commodity prices). In agriculture: CF and NTR (fertilizer producers, food-security plays), ADM and BG (grain traders, the picks-and-shovels of agriculture). ATLAS

L9 — Deep sector ETFs: OIH (oil services — Schlumberger, Halliburton, Baker Hughes; the companies that drill and maintain wells, not the ones that own the oil) is the most leveraged play on oil prices because services spending is the first thing E&P companies cut in a downturn and the first thing they ramp in a recovery. XME (metals and mining) provides diversified exposure to steel, aluminum, copper, and gold miners. XAR and PPA (aerospace and defense) benefit from rising military budgets globally. PPH and IHF (pharmaceuticals and health insurance) are defensive sectors with drug-pricing and regulation risk. IYZ (telecom) is the classic “boring utility-like” sector. BJK (gaming and casinos) captures Macau, Las Vegas, and online gambling. PEJ (leisure and entertainment). IGF (global infrastructure — toll roads, airports, utilities). SEA (shipping) moves with global trade volumes and is one of the most cyclical sectors in existence.

ATLAS

L10 — Advanced fixed income: MBS (mortgage-backed securities) were covered in L2 above. BNDX (international bonds, hedged to USD) provides diversification away from the US Treasury curve. SRLN (senior bank loans) holds floating-rate corporate debt that resets with SOFR — unlike fixed-rate bonds, these benefit from rising rates, making them the mirror image of traditional bonds. JBBB (CLO BBB tranche ETF) and SHYG (short-duration high-yield) offer different risk/reward profiles within credit. Private credit — direct lending by non-bank institutions — has grown into a trillion-dollar market that competes directly with high-yield bonds and bank loans. Callable bonds (bonds the issuer can redeem early) have negative convexity: they underperform in rallies because the issuer calls them away. This is the dominant structure in corporate and municipal bonds.

ATLAS

L11 — Country ETFs: ARGT (Argentina) has been one of the best-performing country ETFs since Milei’s election and market reforms. EWQ (France) is the largest eurozone-only play. EIS (Israel) offers exposure to a tech-heavy economy with unique geopolitical risk. EWN (Netherlands) is dominated by ASML, the semiconductor lithography monopoly. GREK (Greece) has been a turnaround story since the debt crisis. EPHE (Philippines) is a frontier-market play on demographics and remittances. The Nordic economies have distinct characteristics: Norway is an oil-funded sovereign wealth play, Sweden has a strong export sector, Denmark is dominated by Novo Nordisk.

ATLAS

L12 — Commodity instruments: Note: JO (the coffee ETN) was redeemed in June 2023 and no longer exists. Coffee is still investable via futures or the Teucrium suite. Cocoa and sugar both had extreme moves in 2024–2025. Lumber is historically one of the best leading indicators for housing and the broader economy. UGA (gasoline) and BNO (Brent crude) offer different exposure points than standard WTI crude funds. SIL (silver miners) provides leveraged exposure to silver prices through the companies that mine it, similar to how GDX provides leveraged gold exposure.

ATLAS

L13 — Strategy ETFs: PFIX (Simplify Interest Rate Hedge) is designed to profit from rising long-term rates — effectively a put option on bonds, useful when you think inflation will surprise to the upside. SVOL (Simplify Volatility Premium) sells VIX futures to harvest the volatility risk premium, producing high yields but with significant tail risk. BTAL (AGF US Market Neutral Anti-Beta) goes long low-beta stocks and short high-beta stocks, profiting from a “risk-off” environment. KMLM (KFA Mount Lucas Managed Futures) is a trend-following CTA strategy that provides crisis alpha — it tends to make money exactly when stocks crash, as it did in 2022. SWAN (Amplify BlackSwan Growth & Treasury) combines S&P 500 exposure with Treasury options designed to protect against crashes. QAI (IQ Hedge Multi-Strategy) attempts to replicate hedge fund returns in an ETF wrapper.

ATLAS

L14 — Indicator dashboard: ISM Services PMI is arguably more important than manufacturing PMI because the services economy is ~80% of US GDP. Weekly initial jobless claims are the most real-time labor market indicator. JOLTS (Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey) measures the job openings-to-unemployed ratio — the Fed watches this closely. The Sahm Rule: when the 3-month moving average of unemployment rises 0.5% above its 12-month low, a recession has begun — it has correctly called every recession since 1970. Retail sales, consumer sentiment, and inflation expectations surveys. Housing starts (a leading indicator because homes take months to build). LEI (Conference Board Leading Economic Index, a composite of 10 forward-looking indicators).

Valuation gauges: CAPE (cyclically adjusted P/E ratio, the Shiller metric), forward P/E, and the equity risk premium (stock earnings yield minus Treasury yield — when this is near zero, stocks offer no compensation for their extra risk vs bonds). Real yields and breakevens (the market’s inflation expectation derived from TIPS vs nominal Treasuries). The 3-month/10-year yield curve (the most reliable recession predictor). Funding stress indicators: the SOFR–IORB spread and FRA-OIS spread, which measure strain in the banking system’s short-term funding markets. Breadth: percentage of stocks above their 200-day moving average, and new highs minus new lows. Sentiment surveys: AAII (retail investors), NAAIM (active managers), and COT (Commitment of Traders, showing how commercial hedgers vs speculators are positioned in futures). Net Liquidity (Fed balance sheet minus TGA minus reverse repo) should be tracked as a live metric alongside the conceptual definition in Layer 0.

A cash and money-market layer comparing money market funds, SGOV, direct T-bills, and brokered CDs as cash vehicles should be foundational. A derivatives layer covering index futures (ES, NQ, YM), SPX options (the most liquid options market in the world, used for portfolio hedging and income), and how these instruments relate to the underlying ETFs would connect the institutional and retail worlds. Each layer should also have a “figures as of [date]” line.

Income Architect

INCOME

Ch 2 — Annuities: A SPIA (Single Premium Immediate Annuity) is the purest form of income product: you give an insurance company a lump sum and they pay you a fixed amount every month for the rest of your life. You can’t outlive it, you can’t run out, and the payment never changes. A DIA (Deferred Income Annuity) works the same way but starts payments years later, providing longevity insurance at a lower cost because the insurance company keeps your money longer. A QLAC (Qualified Longevity Annuity Contract) is a special DIA purchased inside an IRA that lets you defer up to $200,000 from RMDs until age 85 — useful for people who don’t need their RMD income but are forced to take it.

These are the only instruments in existence that guarantee you won’t outlive your money, which is the central fear of every retiree. CDs and brokered CDs (FDIC-insured, fixed-term, predictable income) are another tool. Royalty trusts (companies that own oil/gas wells or mineral rights and distribute nearly all cash flow) are a classic yield-trap category that income investors repeatedly fall for — the high yields look attractive until production declines and distributions get cut. Interval funds and non-traded BDCs offer higher yields than public markets but lock up your capital and have limited transparency — caution is warranted.

INCOME

Ch 3 — Dividend ETFs and concepts: VYM (Vanguard High Dividend Yield) is the largest dividend ETF by assets and represents a fundamentally different approach than dividend-growth ETFs — VYM buys the highest current yielders rather than the fastest growers, producing more income today at the cost of potentially slower growth. COWZ (Pacer US Cash Cows 100) represents the shareholder-yield concept: selecting companies that return the most capital through dividends and buybacks combined. Buybacks vs dividends as capital return mechanisms is a critical comparison — buybacks are more tax-efficient (they increase your share of the company rather than paying you taxable income), but dividends provide tangible cash flow and impose discipline on management.

The 61-day qualified-dividend holding period rule is a practical tax trap: if you sell a stock fewer than 61 days after the ex-dividend date, the dividend is taxed as ordinary income (up to 37%) rather than the preferential qualified rate (0/15/20%). Individual stock examples showing real dividend histories, payout ratios, and growth rates make the concepts concrete. Stress-test data: S&P 500 dividends fell roughly 20% peak-to-trough in 2009, and about 10% of payers cut or suspended dividends during COVID in 2020. Dividends are real cash, but they’re not guaranteed.

INCOME

Ch 4 — Bond instruments: Target-maturity ETFs (iBonds from iShares, BulletShares from Invesco) hold bonds all maturing in the same year and then liquidate, returning your principal. They combine the diversification of a fund with the maturity certainty of an individual bond — you know when you’ll get your money back, unlike a perpetual bond fund like BND. A TIPS ladder (buying individual TIPS maturing in each year of retirement) creates an inflation-adjusted income floor. USFR (floating-rate Treasuries) resets its yield quarterly, so it barely moves in price regardless of rate changes. I-Bonds (from TreasuryDirect, $10k annual limit) are inflation-protected savings bonds with no market risk.

High-yield and emerging-market bond ETFs (HYG, JNK for US high-yield; EMB for dollar-denominated EM debt) each have their own yield, risk, and default-rate profiles. Callable bonds — bonds the issuer can redeem early, typically when rates fall — have negative convexity: their upside is capped because the issuer calls them away. This is the dominant structure in corporate and municipal bonds, and income investors need to understand why their bond fund might underperform in a rate rally. Agency MBS have the same issue due to mortgage prepayments.

INCOME

Ch 4B — International dividends: SCHY (Schwab International Dividend Equity) is the international equivalent of SCHD, offering high-quality foreign dividend stocks. VIGI (Vanguard International Dividend Appreciation) and IGRO (iShares International Dividend Growth) apply the dividend-growth philosophy internationally.

The foreign tax credit (FTC) is essential knowledge for international dividend investors. When you receive dividends from foreign stocks, the foreign country withholds taxes (typically 15–30%). You can reclaim this on your US tax return as a foreign tax credit, but the credit is limited to the US tax you’d owe on that income. If you hold international funds in a Roth IRA, the foreign withholding is simply lost — there’s no US tax to offset against. The $300/$600 simplified election lets taxpayers with modest foreign income skip the complex Form 1116 and take the credit directly on Form 1040.

INCOME

Ch 7 — Preferreds depth: Baby bonds are $25 par exchange-traded debt instruments — they look like preferred stocks on your brokerage screen but are actually senior unsecured bonds, higher in the capital structure. They trade on stock exchanges, pay interest (not dividends, so no qualified dividend tax treatment), and have a stated maturity date, making them more bond-like than true preferreds. The $1,000-par institutional preferred market is much larger than the retail $25-par market but requires a brokerage that offers bond trading.

AT1/CoCo (Additional Tier 1 / Contingent Convertible) bonds are the instrument class that Credit Suisse wiped out in 2023. When Swiss regulators forced UBS to absorb Credit Suisse, they zeroed out $17 billion in AT1 bonds while equity holders received $3.2 billion — violating the normal hierarchy where equity takes losses first. This is the most important case study in preferreds/hybrid capital since 2008. Most bank preferreds (the kind retail income investors buy) are non-cumulative, meaning if the bank suspends the dividend, it never has to make up the missed payments.

INCOME

Ch 8 — Covered calls (DIY): Selling covered calls on your own stocks requires understanding strike selection (how far out-of-the-money to sell, balancing premium received against upside given up), delta as the probability of getting called away (a 30-delta call has roughly a 30% chance of being in-the-money at expiration), cash-secured puts (selling puts on stocks you want to buy, getting paid while you wait), and the wheel strategy (selling puts until assigned, then selling covered calls until called away, repeating).

SPYI and QQQI (NEOS S&P 500 and Nasdaq Enhanced Options Income) use put spreads instead of pure call-writing. 0DTE (zero days to expiration) covered-call ETFs write daily options for income. YieldMax single-stock income funds (TSLY on Tesla, NVDY on Nvidia) are advertised with distribution rates of 30–80% but most of that is return of capital as the NAV erodes. They’re the poster child for why headline distribution rate does not equal yield.

INCOME

Ch 9 — Closed-end funds: PDI (PIMCO Dynamic Income) and NUV (Nuveen Municipal Value) deserve full profiles with yield, leverage ratio, premium/discount history, and distribution coverage. Two critical analytical tools for CEF buyers: the discount z-score measures how far a CEF’s current discount (or premium) is from its historical average — a z-score below −2 means it’s trading at an unusually wide discount, potentially a buying opportunity. Distribution coverage and UNII (Undistributed Net Investment Income) measure whether the fund is actually earning enough to sustain its distribution. When UNII goes negative, the fund is paying out more than it earns and will eventually cut — this has destroyed wealth in funds like ARR, NLY, and numerous muni CEFs.

INCOME

Ch 9B — Private credit: Non-traded BDCs like BCRED (Blackstone Private Credit) have attracted hundreds of billions from retail investors seeking higher yields than public markets. They invest in direct loans to middle-market companies that can’t or won’t access public bond markets, paying yields of 8–12%. CLO ETFs like JAAA (highest-rated tranche) and JBBB (investment-grade but lower-rated tranche) package pools of leveraged loans into structured tranches — JAAA has never suffered a principal loss in any vintage, while lower tranches carry meaningful default risk.

The 2025 private-credit scare around First Brands and Tricolor Automotive is the stress test to study. These were portfolio companies of major private-credit funds that ran into trouble, raising questions about whether the “private credit is safer than high-yield” narrative was overstated. The key issue: private credit marks its positions to model rather than market, so losses can be hidden until they’re catastrophic. The smooth NAV isn’t the same as low risk.

INCOME

Ch 10 — Yield measurement: Three different yield metrics exist and confusing them leads to bad decisions. SEC 30-day yield is a standardized, forward-looking measure based on the fund’s current holdings and their stated coupons, net of expenses. Trailing twelve-month (TTM) yield is backward-looking — what the fund actually paid over the last year. Distribution rate is the annualized most recent distribution divided by price. These three can differ by 100+ basis points for the same fund: a bond ETF that recently took capital gains might show a high TTM yield that won’t repeat, while its SEC yield reflects the actual current earning power. Comparing SCHD’s 3.5% TTM yield to JEPI’s 7.2% distribution rate is comparing apples to oranges.

INCOME

Ch 11 — Tax depth: The 0% qualified-dividend and long-term capital gains bracket applies to taxable income up to about $89,250 (married filing jointly, 2024). For early retirees living on modest income, this means they can receive qualified dividends and sell appreciated stock tax-free at the federal level.

IRMAA (Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount) is a stealth tax on Medicare premiums. If your modified adjusted gross income exceeds $206,000 (married, 2024), your Medicare Part B and D premiums increase by $1,000 to $7,000+ per year depending on the bracket. A single Roth conversion or capital gain can push you into a higher IRMAA bracket, and the surcharge applies for the entire year based on income from two years prior. The taxation of Social Security benefits uses a “provisional income” formula that creates effective marginal rates of 22.2% to 40.7% — higher than the nominal bracket — in the phase-in zone. State income taxes on dividends and interest vary wildly: nine states have no income tax, while California and New York tax investment income at rates exceeding 10%.

INCOME

Ch 12 — Regime context: The stagflation regime (rising inflation + slowing growth) is the hardest environment for income investors because bond prices fall (rates rising), stock dividends get pressured (slowing economy), and real returns turn negative (inflation outpacing income). The 1970s and 2022 are the two modern examples. This is the regime where the “live on dividends” strategy is most stressed. Atlas Layer 14 is the shared indicator set for identifying which regime you’re currently in.

INCOME

Ch 14 — Portfolio costs: Model portfolios should show their weighted expense ratio — the blended annual cost of owning the portfolio. A portfolio that’s 30% JEPI (0.35% ER), 20% SCHD (0.06%), 15% HYG (0.49%), and 10% PDI (1.95% effective cost including leverage) has a very different total cost than one built from Vanguard index funds.

Fee layering for CEFs and BDCs is particularly opaque: a BDC might charge a 1.5% base management fee plus a 20% incentive fee on returns above a hurdle, plus fund-level borrowing costs of 3–5%. The all-in cost can be 4–8% annually, which is hidden because the distributions are reported net of fees. Know the cost of earning the yield.

INCOME

Ch 15 — RMDs and Roth conversions: Required Minimum Distributions start at age 73 (under current law) and force you to withdraw a percentage of your traditional IRA each year whether you need the money or not. If your IRA has grown large, the forced withdrawals push you into higher tax brackets, trigger IRMAA surcharges, and increase the taxable portion of your Social Security. This is a structural conflict with the “never touch principal, live on natural yield” philosophy — RMDs force you to liquidate the very holdings generating that yield.

Roth conversions during the gap years (between retirement and age 73) can permanently solve this by moving IRA money to a Roth while your taxable income is low. Bracket management — converting just enough each year to “fill up” the 12% or 22% bracket without spilling into the next one — is a core retirement tax strategy.

INCOME

Ch 16 — Social Security depth: Spousal benefits allow a lower-earning spouse to claim up to 50% of the higher earner’s benefit at the higher earner’s full retirement age. Survivor benefits provide the surviving spouse with 100% of the deceased spouse’s benefit, replacing their own smaller benefit. These rules fundamentally change the optimal claiming strategy for married couples.

The earnings test before full retirement age (FRA) reduces benefits by $1 for every $2 earned above ~$22,320 (2024) if you claim early and keep working. The withheld benefits are added back after FRA, but most people don’t know this. The Roth-conversion window — converting traditional IRA money to Roth during the years between retirement and when you claim SS — exploits the lowest-income period of your life for permanent tax savings. If you retire at 62, don’t claim SS until 70, and have no other income, you can convert large amounts at the 10–12% bracket rate. This is one of the most powerful retirement planning tools available.

Ch 12 — ETF and equity profiles: PSLV (Sprott Physical Silver Trust) holds allocated silver bars in Canada and avoids the futures-roll costs of SLV, making it the preferred physical-silver vehicle for many investors — but it’s a PFIC (passive foreign investment company) that requires a QEF election to avoid punitive tax treatment. PALL (Aberdeen Physical Palladium) is the only physical palladium ETF. GLDM (SPDR Gold MiniShares) has a lower expense ratio than GLD (0.10% vs 0.40%) and no options market, making it better for buy-and-hold but worse for hedging. SILJ (ETFMG Prime Junior Silver Miners) offers leveraged exposure to silver through smaller, higher-risk mining companies. URNM (Sprott Uranium Miners) provides diversified exposure to the nuclear fuel supply chain.

Individual stocks: Newmont (the world’s largest gold miner, recently acquired Newcrest), Barrick (now Barrick Mining, ticker B, the second-largest), Agnico Eagle (the third-largest, with exclusively Tier-1 jurisdictions), Royal Gold (buys royalty and streaming agreements that give it the right to purchase gold at a fixed cost below market, creating an asset-light, high-margin model). BHP, Rio Tinto, and Vale are the three largest diversified miners in the world — essential context for the copper, iron ore, aluminum, and nickel markets. SCCO (Southern Copper) is the world’s largest publicly traded copper company. Ivanhoe Mines is the highest-profile development-stage miner. Cameco dominates uranium production. Albemarle is the largest Western lithium producer. MP Materials operates the only active rare-earth mine in the United States.

FORGE

Ch 8 — Industrial metal equities: Alcoa and Century Aluminum (CENX) are the main US aluminum producers. Glencore is the world’s largest diversified commodity trader/miner, covering copper, zinc, nickel, cobalt, and thermal coal. Alphamin and MLX are the dominant tin producers (tin is increasingly critical for semiconductor soldering). Teck Resources and Boliden provide diversified base metal exposure across copper, zinc, and lead.

Simandou, the massive iron ore deposit in Guinea, is expected to come online and significantly increase global iron ore supply. This matters because iron ore is dominated by three companies (BHP, Rio Tinto, Vale) and any supply shock changes the pricing dynamics for steel globally.

FORGE

Ch 10/18 — Mining business model: Hedge books are one of the most consequential business decisions a gold miner can make. Barrick’s late-1990s hedge book is the case study: the company sold forward millions of ounces at $300–400/oz, locking in revenue but giving up all upside. When gold eventually rallied above $1,000, Barrick was still delivering at the hedged price, costing shareholders billions in lost profits. It took years and billions in cash to close the hedge book. This is why most gold miners today operate unhedged.

Co-product vs byproduct AISC (All-In Sustaining Cost) accounting is how miners manipulate their reported cost per ounce. A copper-gold mine can allocate 100% of its costs to copper (making gold a zero-cost byproduct with infinite margin) or split costs by revenue contribution (co-product). Same mine, same costs, radically different reported gold AISC. Comparing AISC across companies without checking which method is used is comparing fiction. Streaming obligations (from Franco-Nevada, Royal Gold, and Wheaton Precious Metals) give the streamer the right to buy future gold production at a fixed price well below market. For the miner, streaming provides upfront capital but creates a debt-like obligation that reduces the effective gold price received on every ounce produced for the life of the mine.

FORGE

Ch 14 — Valuation: Mining stocks are notoriously difficult to value. Free-cash-flow yield at spot price answers the most basic question: if gold stays at today’s price, how much cash does this miner generate relative to its market cap? Reserve price decks vs spot is a subtler issue: mining companies book their reserves using conservative price assumptions ($1,400–1,700 gold) far below current spot ($5,000+). This means reported reserve ounces are dramatically understated — the economic cutoff grade at $5,000 gold is much lower than at $1,700, which means more rock is economic, which means the mine has more reserves than the financial statements show. EV/EBITDA is the standard valuation metric for base-metal miners, where cash-flow multiples are more useful than price/earnings because of heavy capital expenditure cycles.

FORGE

Ch 21 — Signals: The real-yield model — the idea that gold moves inversely to real interest rates — was the dominant gold framework for two decades and it has broken since 2022. Real yields (10-year TIPS yield) went above 2%, which historically would have crushed gold, but gold tripled instead. The primary driver shifted from Western ETF flows (which respond to real yields) to central bank purchases and Asian physical demand (which respond to de-dollarization and geopolitical risk). The real-yield framework is no longer primary.

Gold making all-time highs in non-USD currencies (euro, yen, yuan, rupee) before making ATHs in dollars is historically a leading signal — it means gold is strengthening everywhere, not just because the dollar is weakening. COMEX registered vs eligible inventory measures deliverable supply: registered gold is available for delivery on futures contracts, while eligible is stored in COMEX vaults but not committed. When registered inventory drops while open interest stays high, it signals potential delivery stress. EFP (Exchange for Physical) basis — the spread between futures and physical — normally stays tight but blew out dramatically during the COVID panic and again in early 2025 when tariff fears drove London-to-New-York gold shipments.

FORGE

Ch 28 — Drill results: CuEq and AuEq (copper-equivalent and gold-equivalent) grade reporting is a promotional trick used by junior miners to inflate their headline grades. A project with 0.3% copper and 0.2 g/t gold can be presented as “0.7% CuEq” by converting the gold value into copper equivalents at favorable metal prices. The conversion depends on which metal prices and recovery assumptions you use, and juniors typically choose the most flattering ones. This is marketing, not geology.

S-K 1300 (the SEC’s mining disclosure standard, effective 2021) modernized US mining disclosure to be more comparable with international standards (NI 43-101 in Canada, JORC in Australia) but has differences in how “measured” and “indicated” resources are defined. A US-listed miner reports under S-K 1300 while a Canadian-listed miner reports under NI 43-101, and the same deposit can look meaningfully different depending on which standard is used.

FORGE

Ch 32 — The Lassonde Curve: The lifecycle of a mining project follows a predictable pattern universally known as the Lassonde Curve, named after Pierre Lassonde (co-founder of Franco-Nevada). Early exploration: the stock spikes on discovery. Feasibility studies: the stock drifts as the market waits. Permitting and construction: the “orphan period” where enthusiasm fades and the stock can languish for years. Finally, production: the stock re-rates to cash-flow value. Understanding where a mining stock sits on the Lassonde Curve tells you what kind of risk you’re taking and what catalyst moves it next.

FORGE

Ch 29 — Tax: Canadian-listed miners (Barrick, Agnico, Franco-Nevada, many juniors) withhold 25% on dividends by default, reduced to 15% under the US-Canada tax treaty. In a taxable account, this withholding is recoverable as a foreign tax credit. In an IRA, it is simply lost — the US-Canada treaty does not exempt retirement accounts. A Canadian miner paying a 3% dividend effectively yields only 2.55% in an IRA.

NIIT (the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax) applies to all investment income including collectibles gains, meaning gold ETF profits face a potential 28% + 3.8% = 31.8% combined federal rate. State sales tax on physical bullion varies widely: some states exempt it entirely (Texas, Florida), others charge full sales tax (Vermont, New Mexico), and many have minimum-purchase exemptions ($1,000+). PFIC/QEF elections for Sprott trusts (PHYS, PSLV, SPPP) are required to avoid the PFIC penalty regime, which taxes gains at the highest ordinary rate plus an interest charge. PSLV requires an annual tax filing election that most retail investors don’t know about.

FORGE

Ch 22 — Physical gold: Costco gold bars have become a surprisingly significant retail demand channel — reportedly $100–200 million per month in 1-ounce gold bars and silver coins, making Costco one of the largest retail bullion dealers in the United States. This democratization of physical gold ownership reflects the cultural shift toward tangible assets.

Self-directed gold IRA mechanics: you can hold physical gold (coins, bars meeting purity requirements) inside an IRA, but it requires a specialized custodian, approved depository storage (you can’t keep it at home), and transaction fees that are typically much higher than buying a gold ETF. This makes sense for large allocations, distrust of counterparties, or estate planning — but not for small allocations where fees eat the return, or for people who want liquidity.

Ch 36 — Steel: U.S. Steel is one of the most historically significant American industrial companies and the subject of a blocked Nippon Steel acquisition (President Biden blocked it on national security grounds in early 2025). Its legacy integrated mills compete against EAF (electric arc furnace) producers like Nucor and Steel Dynamics, and the political dimension of foreign ownership adds a layer of risk unique to this company.

Cleveland-Cliffs (CLF) acquired Stelco, a major Canadian steelmaker, in 2024, continuing its consolidation strategy after buying ArcelorMittal USA and AK Steel. Section 232 steel tariffs are now at 50% (doubled from the original 25% in 2018), fundamentally changing the import economics and domestic pricing power.
