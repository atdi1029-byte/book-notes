How to Read This Guide

This document lists every content gap across all four finance guides: ATLAS The Asset Atlas, PLAYBOOK The Portfolio Playbook, INCOME The Income Architect, and FORGE The Metal Forge. Each item is tagged by guide. These are topics that were promised, implied, or logically belong but were never covered. Each entry explains what the concept is, why it matters, and what a reader misses without it.

Content Gaps: Portfolio Playbook

PLAYBOOK

Ch 2 — Cash, TIPS, currency hedging, and crypto: Cash and T-bills are a core building block of every portfolio but get no standalone section. A reader doesn’t learn that T-bills are the closest thing to a risk-free asset, that their yield resets with Fed policy, or how to hold them efficiently (SGOV, USFR, or direct TreasuryDirect purchases). TIPS — Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities — appear in most of the guide’s Day One portfolios but only as a row in a table. TIPS are bonds whose principal adjusts with CPI, meaning they protect against inflation in a way nominal bonds can’t. But they have quirks the guide never explains: “phantom income” (you owe taxes on inflation adjustments to principal even though you haven’t received any cash), a deflation floor (the principal can’t drop below par at maturity), and real yield as the key metric rather than nominal yield. Without this, a reader puts TIPS in their portfolio without understanding what they own.

Currency hedging is also absent. When you buy international stocks through a fund like VXUS, you’re implicitly taking a position in foreign currencies — if the dollar strengthens, your international returns shrink even if foreign stocks rise. Hedged ETFs like HEFA (developed markets hedged) and DXJ (Japan hedged) strip out this currency risk. The home-bias debate — whether Americans overweight US stocks because they’re familiar rather than because it’s optimal — is central to how much international exposure to hold, and the guide doesn’t address it. Finally, crypto should be evaluated on the same correlation and drawdown framework the guide applies to every other asset class, with a clear sizing rule: either 0% (skip it entirely) or 1–3% capped, with a rebalancing plan that accounts for 70%+ drawdowns.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 3–4 — Period mismatch and missing portfolios: The comparison tables that form the backbone of Chapters 3 and 4 are nearly useless for apples-to-apples comparison because each portfolio uses a different measurement period. One shows data from 1926–2024, another from 1972–2020, another from 1978–2020, and others use vague labels like “30-year” or “10-year.” A portfolio that looks great over 1926–2024 (which includes the post-WWII boom) may look very different over 1972–2020 (which starts with stagflation). The fix is a common-period table — say, 1978–2024 — where every portfolio gets the same ruler.

Several well-known portfolios are also missing. NTSX is a “capital-efficient” fund that uses futures to deliver 90/60 exposure (90% stocks, 60% bonds) in a single fund — effectively a leveraged 60/40. RPAR does something similar for a risk-parity portfolio. The Larry Portfolio (named after Larry Swedroe) is a classic small-cap-value-heavy allocation that tilts hard into the factors academic research says have the highest premiums. The Faber Ivy Portfolio replicates the endowment-style approach of Yale and Harvard using five equal-weighted asset classes with a trend-following overlay. These are among the most-discussed portfolios in the investing community and their absence is conspicuous.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 5 — 401(k) translation: The guide tells you exactly which ETFs to buy, but most readers’ largest investment account is a 401(k) with a limited menu of 15–25 mutual funds chosen by their employer. You can’t buy VTI or VXUS in most 401(k)s. A “closest index fund on your menu” section would be the most actionable page in the entire guide: mapping each recommended ETF to its mutual-fund equivalent (VTI → VTSAX or FSKAX, VXUS → VTIAX or FTIHX, BND → VBTLX). The reader could then take any model portfolio from Chapter 4 and actually build it in their real account. Also missing: SGOV and USFR as T-bill and floating-rate alternatives for cash management, VT as a single-fund global solution for people who want maximum simplicity, and AVDV as the Avantis international small-cap value fund that implements the factor tilts the guide recommends.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 6 — Life-stage gaps: Social Security and pensions are bond-like assets that most people ignore when calculating their allocation. A $30,000/year pension, capitalized at 4%, is equivalent to holding $750,000 in bonds. Someone with a generous pension can afford a much more aggressive stock allocation than their age alone would suggest, because they already have a huge implicit bond position. The guide never frames it this way.

Goals with horizons under 10 years — saving for a house down payment, a child’s tuition, a wedding — need goal-based buckets with their own allocations, separate from the long-term retirement portfolio. A 25-year-old saving for a home in 3 years shouldn’t have that money in an age-based equity-heavy allocation. The rising equity glide path concept (increasing stock allocation in retirement rather than decreasing it) also needs a caveat: the research shows the effect is small, and it assumes the retiree is psychologically comfortable holding 70–80% stocks at age 80, which most people are not.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 7 — Cross-account rebalancing: The chapter discusses rebalancing within a single account but never addresses the reality that most people have 3–5 accounts: a 401(k), a Roth IRA, a taxable brokerage account, an HSA, and possibly a spouse’s accounts. The critical insight the guide misses: you should treat all of these as one unified portfolio and do your rebalancing trades inside the tax-advantaged accounts (401k, Roth, HSA) where buys and sells generate no taxable events. This is free alpha — you maintain your target allocation without creating tax drag. A reader who rebalances each account independently might have the right overall allocation by accident, but they’re almost certainly generating unnecessary capital gains in their taxable account.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 8 — Regime identification: The chapter describes four economic regimes (rising growth/rising inflation, rising growth/falling inflation, etc.) and explains which assets do well in each. But it never tells you how to identify which regime you’re currently in. This is like giving someone a map with no “you are here” marker. The Atlas has an entire Layer 14 indicator dashboard designed for exactly this purpose — PMI for growth direction, CPI for inflation direction, the yield curve for market expectations — but the Playbook never links to it. The chapter also misidentifies 2021–2022 as a “rising growth” regime when GDP growth was actually decelerating from the post-COVID surge while inflation was spiking — a classic rising-inflation/slowing-growth setup, not what the guide claims.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 9 — Retirement income tools: The chapter covers the safe withdrawal rate (3.7% baseline) but misses several tools that retirement researchers consider essential. A TIPS ladder — buying individual TIPS bonds maturing in each year of early retirement — creates an inflation-protected income floor that no stock market crash can touch. SPIAs (Single Premium Immediate Annuities) provide guaranteed lifetime income: you give an insurance company a lump sum and they pay you monthly until you die, no matter how long you live. This is longevity insurance — the only product that eliminates the risk of outliving your money.

Withdrawal sequencing — which account to draw from first — can add years to portfolio longevity. The general rule: taxable first (for lower tax rates on long-term gains), then traditional IRA (ordinary income), then Roth (tax-free, let it grow longest). But Roth conversions during the “gap years” between retirement and age 73 (when RMDs start) can permanently reduce your lifetime tax bill by converting traditional IRA money to Roth while you’re in a low tax bracket. Social Security claiming age (62 vs 67 vs 70) is itself a portfolio lever: each year you delay past 62 increases your benefit by 6–8%, which is effectively a guaranteed real return that no other asset matches.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 10 — Tax tools: Treasury bonds and bills are exempt from state and local income taxes. In a state like California (13.3% top rate) or New York (10.9%), this is a material benefit that changes the tax-equivalent yield calculation significantly. A Treasury yielding 4% in New York is equivalent to a corporate bond yielding roughly 4.5% pre-state-tax. The guide never mentions this.

Municipal bonds (VTEB is the Vanguard tax-exempt bond ETF) are completely absent despite being the single most tax-efficient fixed-income instrument for high earners — their interest is exempt from federal income tax and often state tax too. For someone in the 37% federal bracket, a muni yielding 3.5% is equivalent to a taxable bond yielding 5.6%. The HSA (Health Savings Account) also deserves its own section: it’s the only account with a triple tax advantage (tax-deductible contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals for medical expenses), and after age 65 it functions like a traditional IRA for non-medical spending.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 11 — Return measurement: The chapter discusses tracking portfolio performance but never explains the critical difference between the two ways returns are calculated. Time-weighted return (TWR) measures how the fund performed regardless of when you added or withdrew money — it’s what Morningstar and fund fact sheets report. Money-weighted return (XIRR) measures what you actually earned given your specific pattern of deposits and withdrawals. These can diverge dramatically: if you invested $10,000 in January, the fund dropped 30%, and then you added $50,000 at the bottom, your money-weighted return could be strongly positive even if the fund’s time-weighted return was negative for the year. Most investors use whichever number their brokerage shows without knowing which kind it is, leading them to systematically misunderstand their own performance.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 12 — Missing common mistakes: Three of the most wealth-destroying retail mistakes are absent. Employer-stock concentration: holding too much company stock in your 401(k) means your salary, your benefits, and your retirement savings all depend on one company. Enron employees learned this the hard way when the stock went to zero and their 401(k)s went with it. Margin and leverage: borrowing against your portfolio amplifies returns in both directions, and a 50% drop on 2x leverage wipes you out entirely, not just painfully. “Waiting for a dip” cash drag: the opportunity cost of sitting in cash waiting for a pullback that may not come for years. Studies consistently show that lump-sum investing beats waiting for a dip roughly two-thirds of the time, because markets trend upward and the “perfect entry” you’re waiting for usually doesn’t compensate for the gains you missed while waiting.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 14 — Emergency scenario: “I lost my job and need cash now” is the most stressful financial scenario a reader will face and the one where they most need clear guidance. The chapter covers what to do during market crashes but not personal financial emergencies. The guide should address: which account to tap first (emergency fund → taxable brokerage → Roth contributions → 401k loan, roughly in that order), whether to sell investments or use a HELOC or margin loan as a short-term bridge, how to sequence spending cuts, and when to stop contributing to retirement accounts temporarily. People in crisis make their worst financial decisions, and having a pre-written playbook would prevent panic selling at exactly the wrong time.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 15 — Bond depth: Several important bond concepts are missing. Convexity describes how bond prices respond non-linearly to interest rate changes — a 1% rate drop adds more to a bond’s price than a 1% rate rise takes away. This is why long-duration bonds are sometimes described as having “positive convexity” and why they can act as crash insurance. Starting yield is the single best predictor of a bond’s forward return over the next 10 years — much more reliable than any stock-market valuation metric. If you buy a 10-year Treasury at 4.5%, you will almost certainly earn something close to 4.5% annualized over the decade.

Individual Treasury bonds and bond ladders behave differently from bond ETFs in a critical way: an individual bond matures at par (you get your money back), while a bond ETF has no maturity date and continuously rolls into new bonds, meaning it can stay underwater for years in a rising-rate environment. TLT (the 20+ year Treasury ETF) demonstrated this brutally: its 2020–2023 drawdown was roughly −45%, worse than many stock-market crashes. The guide discusses duration but never shows what −45% on a “safe” bond fund actually looks like.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 16 — Home economics: A mortgage is a negative bond: you’re effectively short fixed-income duration. If you have a $400,000 mortgage at 3%, you’re paying a fixed rate and benefiting when rates rise (because your locked-in rate looks better relative to new rates). This changes your overall portfolio’s effective duration and should influence how much you hold in bonds. The pay-down vs invest decision is one of the most common financial questions people face: if your mortgage rate is 3% and you expect 7–8% from stocks after tax, investing the difference wins on paper. But the guaranteed 3% return from paydown has value, especially for risk-averse investors or those approaching retirement. The guide never frames this trade-off.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 17 — Tax-loss harvesting depth: The wash-sale rule (you can’t claim a loss if you buy a “substantially identical” security within 30 days) is mentioned but the practical workaround isn’t provided: substitute pairs. You sell VTI at a loss and immediately buy ITOT (iShares total market) — same exposure, different fund, no wash sale. Similarly, VXUS ↔ IXUS for international. These pairs let you harvest losses without ever being out of the market.

Tax-gain harvesting is the opposite strategy and equally powerful: in years when your taxable income is low enough to fall in the 0% long-term capital gains bracket (roughly under $89,250 for married filers in 2024), you can sell appreciated stock, pay zero federal tax on the gain, and immediately repurchase it at a higher cost basis. It’s essentially a free Roth conversion for your taxable account. The Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) adds a 3.8% surcharge on investment income above $250,000 (married), which changes the math on harvesting for high earners. Donor-advised funds let you donate appreciated stock and take a deduction for the full market value without ever paying capital gains tax on the appreciation. Direct indexing — holding individual stocks instead of an index fund — unlocks continuous tax-loss harvesting at the individual-stock level, which is particularly valuable for portfolios with large embedded gains that can’t be sold without a massive tax hit.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 18 — Fund costs: The expense ratio is the stated annual fee a fund charges, but tracking difference — the actual gap between an ETF’s net return and its benchmark index — is a more complete measure of cost. Tracking difference captures everything: the expense ratio, securities lending revenue (which can offset or even exceed the ER), sampling error from holding a subset of the index, cash drag from uninvested dividends, and transaction costs from rebalancing. Some funds with higher expense ratios actually have better (tighter) tracking difference than cheaper competitors because they earn more from securities lending or track their index more precisely. A reader who compares funds only by ER is missing the full picture.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 19 — Automation and the spouse test: The biggest risk to any investment plan isn’t a market crash — it’s that the investor stops following it. Auto-invest features (available at Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard, and especially M1 Finance) remove the behavioral risk of “I’ll rebalance next month.” You set your target allocation once and the platform handles contributions and rebalancing automatically. The guide recommends manual rebalancing without mentioning that automation exists and solves the discipline problem.

Also missing: the concept of a written investment plan simple enough that a spouse or executor with zero financial knowledge could follow it. “Put 60% in VTI and 40% in BND, rebalance once a year in January” is a complete plan that survives the death or incapacitation of the primary financial planner. This is morbid but essential — and the guide never raises it.

PLAYBOOK

Ch 20 — Day One prerequisites: The “Day One” chapter tells readers to start investing immediately but skips the single most important prerequisite: an emergency fund of 3–6 months of living expenses in cash or a high-yield savings account. Without this, the first car repair or medical bill forces you to sell investments, potentially at a loss, potentially with tax consequences. This isn’t just missing from Chapter 20 — it’s absent from the entire guide.

Account sequencing priority is also missing: the standard flowchart is 401(k) up to employer match (free money) → HSA to max (triple tax advantage) → Roth IRA to max (tax-free growth) → back to 401(k) to max → taxable brokerage. Getting this wrong — like maxing out a taxable account before getting the employer match — can cost tens of thousands of dollars over a career in lost matching and tax benefits.

Content Gaps: Asset Atlas

ATLAS

L0 — Market plumbing: Layer 0 covers how markets work under the hood, but several critical pieces of the plumbing are missing. The Treasury General Account (TGA) is the government’s checking account at the Fed — when it fills up (debt ceiling resolved, big tax receipts), it drains liquidity from the banking system; when it empties (spending exceeds revenue), it adds liquidity back. The bill-vs-coupon issuance mix matters because heavy bill issuance pulls money from the reverse repo facility (adding reserves) while coupon issuance pulls money from the real economy (draining reserves). These mechanics move risk assets on a multi-week timescale and the guide doesn’t explain them.

Global central banks are absent. The Bank of Japan (BOJ) held rates negative for years and its July 2024 surprise hike triggered the yen carry trade unwind — the biggest cross-asset shock of the year, sending the Nikkei down 12% in a day and rippling through US equities. The ECB and PBOC have their own cycles that affect global capital flows, and none of this appears in the guide. Vol-control funds, CTAs (trend-following commodity trading advisors), and risk-parity strategies mechanically buy and sell based on realized volatility — when vol rises, they sell, amplifying the move. Leveraged ETFs (like TQQQ) must rebalance at the close every day, creating predictable buying or selling pressure in the last hour of trading. Vanna (how delta changes with implied volatility) and charm (how delta decays with time) create options-driven flows around OPEX dates. VIX expiration Wednesdays specifically create gamma-driven moves as market makers unwind hedges. These are the mechanical forces that move markets on a day-to-day basis.

ATLAS

L1 — Foundation gaps: The Russell 2000 (IWM) has a well-known problem: roughly 40% of its components are unprofitable companies. This drags down the entire small-cap asset class. IJR (S&P SmallCap 600) and VBR (Vanguard Small-Cap Value) both screen for profitability, which filters out the junk and has historically produced better returns. These are the most commonly recommended fix for the small-cap problem and deserve their own cards. VT (Vanguard Total World Stock) holds every investable stock on earth in a single fund and is the simplest possible equity allocation — one fund, done. For a guide about building blocks, this is a notable absence. AVUV (Avantis US Small Cap Value) is currently buried in Layer 15 among individual stocks, but it’s a factor ETF that belongs with the other foundational building blocks in Layer 1.

ATLAS

L2 — Fixed income depth: Agency mortgage-backed securities (MBS) are one of the largest fixed-income markets in the world, backed by pools of home mortgages and implicitly guaranteed by the US government. MBB and VMBS are the main ETFs. MBS have negative convexity: when rates fall, homeowners refinance and you get your principal back early (just when you want to stay invested); when rates rise, nobody refinances and you’re stuck holding a below-market coupon longer. This unique risk profile deserves its own card and a convexity explanation.

The yield curve — how interest rates vary by maturity from 1 month to 30 years — is the single most important chart in fixed income, but the Atlas has no section dedicated to it. It should cover: what a normal, inverted, and flat curve look like and what each signals; the term premium (extra yield for holding longer maturities); and Fed funds futures / SOFR futures, which let you see what the market expects the Fed to do at each upcoming meeting.

Missing instruments: USFR and TFLO (floating-rate Treasury ETFs that reset with Fed policy, so their price barely moves); ZROZ and EDV (ultra-long-duration zero-coupon Treasury strips, effectively 25–30 year duration — the most volatile bonds available, used as crash insurance); EMLC (emerging-market local-currency bonds, which add both credit and currency risk but historically higher yields); and target-maturity bond ladders like iBonds (iShares) and BulletShares (Invesco), which hold bonds maturing in a specific year and then return your principal — giving you the maturity certainty of individual bonds with the diversification of a fund.

ATLAS

L3 — Sectors: Independent power producers are the biggest missing sector story. Vistra (VST), Constellation Energy (CEG), and NRG Energy own and operate power plants. The AI data center buildout has created enormous demand for electricity, making these stocks some of the best performers of 2024–2025. The AI-power thesis is mentioned in four separate Atlas cards, but none provides a ticker or explains the investment vehicle. It’s as if the guide describes the thesis and then forgets to tell you how to invest in it.

Other missing sectors: autos (cyclical manufacturing exposed to rates, EV transition, and tariffs), OIH (oil services — the companies that drill, not the ones that own the oil), XME (metals and mining, linking to the Metal Forge), and ITB (homebuilders, one of the most rate-sensitive sectors). Private credit and BDCs (business development companies) have grown into a trillion-dollar market — BIZD is the ETF that holds a basket of publicly traded BDCs, offering exposure to direct lending at a yield typically 8–11%. This entire alternative lending market has no card.

ATLAS

L4 — Commodities: The 2024 cocoa spike is the best real-world example of contango and backwardation in recent memory: cocoa futures went from ~$2,500/ton to over $10,000 as West African harvests failed, and the extreme backwardation (spot price far above futures) meant commodity ETFs actually outperformed spot for once. This should be the go-to case study for explaining these concepts, and it’s missing.

REMX (rare earth and critical minerals ETF) covers the metals essential for EVs, wind turbines, and military hardware — lithium, cobalt, neodymium, dysprosium. These have become geopolitically important because China controls the refining for most of them. Physical uranium (Sprott’s SRUUF or the Kazatomprom ADR) is the direct play on nuclear energy’s comeback. KRBN (carbon credits ETF) tracks the price of EU and California emission allowances, creating a financial asset out of climate regulation. The Baltic Dry Index measures shipping rates for bulk commodities and serves as a real-time indicator of global trade demand — when it spikes, physical goods are moving; when it collapses, trade is slowing. The broader commodity supercycle and capex-cycle framework (periods of under-investment lead to supply shortages, which lead to price spikes, which lead to over-investment, which leads to oversupply) is the organizing principle for the entire commodity layer and it’s never articulated.

ATLAS

L5 — Currencies: The currency layer currently only covers UUP (the US dollar bullish fund), but currencies are a major asset class. FXE (euro), FXY (Japanese yen), FXB (British pound), FXF (Swiss franc), and CYB (Chinese yuan) each move on different fundamentals — ECB rate differentials, BOJ intervention, Brexit aftermath, safe-haven flows, and PBOC management respectively. An emerging-market currency basket captures the carry trade concept: borrowing in low-rate currencies (yen, Swiss franc) and lending in high-rate currencies (Brazilian real, Mexican peso, Turkish lira) to earn the rate differential. This is one of the most popular institutional strategies and it periodically blows up spectacularly (as it did in August 2024). Country ETFs ARGT (Argentina, a frontier-market turnaround story under Milei) and EWQ (France, the largest eurozone pure-play) are also absent.

ATLAS

L6 — Volatility depth: The volatility layer covers the long side (buying protection via VIX products) but ignores the short side. SVIX and SVXY are inverse VIX ETFs that profit from the VIX term structure being in contango (which it is ~80% of the time), providing a steady positive return punctuated by catastrophic drawdowns. The February 2018 XIV “Volmageddon” is the essential cautionary tale: XIV (a predecessor to these funds) lost 96% of its value in a single day when the VIX spiked from 17 to 37, wiping out years of steady gains and billions in investor wealth. This is required reading for anyone considering short-vol strategies.

Single-stock leveraged ETFs like NVDL (2x Nvidia daily) and TSLL (2x Tesla daily) have exploded in popularity but amplify both returns and the corrosive effects of daily rebalancing and volatility decay. TMF (3x long 20+ year Treasuries) is the leveraged bond equivalent. Inverse ETFs like SH (inverse S&P 500) and PSQ (inverse Nasdaq) let you bet against the market without shorting. VVIX (the volatility of VIX) and SKEW (which measures the pricing of deep out-of-the-money put options) are second-order indicators that tell you how nervous institutional hedgers are about tail risk. When SKEW is elevated, big money is paying a premium for crash protection even if the VIX itself looks calm.

ATLAS

L7 — Thematic (biggest hole in the Atlas): Crypto is the single largest gap. Bitcoin and Ethereum spot ETFs (IBIT from BlackRock, FBTC from Fidelity, ETHA for Ethereum) launched in 2024 and accumulated tens of billions in AUM within months, making them among the most successful ETF launches in history. MicroStrategy (MSTR) holds over 200,000 Bitcoin on its balance sheet and trades as a leveraged Bitcoin proxy. WGMI holds Bitcoin mining companies. BLOK is a broader blockchain technology ETF. These are instruments that millions of investors own and the Atlas — a guide to “every asset” — has zero cards for any of them.

Cannabis ETFs (MJ, MSOS) and metaverse ETFs (META, METV) are named in the thematic layer’s introduction but have no cards. QTUM (quantum computing and AI), GRID (clean energy infrastructure), and SRVR/DTCR (data center REITs) represent investable thematic trends with no coverage. YieldMax single-stock income funds deserve a warning card: they sell options on individual stocks (TSLY on Tesla, NVDY on Nvidia) to generate eye-catching distribution rates of 30–80%, but the distributions are largely return of capital, the NAV erodes steadily, and the total return typically underperforms just holding the underlying stock. Many retail investors don’t understand this, making it exactly the kind of product the Atlas should explain.

ATLAS

L8/8B/15 — Missing stocks: The individual stock layers have significant gaps across multiple sectors. In tech infrastructure: IBM (legacy enterprise, AI pivot), ANET (Arista Networks, data center networking), GEV (GE Vernova, power/energy spinoff), SNPS and CDNS (Synopsys and Cadence, the EDA duopoly that designs every chip), DELL/SMCI/VRT (server hardware and data center cooling — the AI infrastructure picks-and- shovels play). In healthcare: CVS (pharmacy + insurance vertical integration), HCA (largest for-profit hospital chain), MCK (McKesson, pharmaceutical distribution). In industrial/ consumer: ADP (payroll processing, a labor- market indicator), ORLY and AZO (auto parts retail, recession-resistant), CSX (railroad, economic bellwether), DAL and UAL (airlines), MAR and HLT (hotels), RCL and CCL (cruise lines — the most leveraged consumer discretionary play).

In financials: COF (Capital One, credit card / consumer lending), HOOD (Robinhood, retail trading), USB/PNC/TFC (regional banks, important after the 2023 banking crisis). In energy: EPD, ET, and KMI (midstream pipelines — toll-road businesses that earn fees regardless of commodity prices). In agriculture: CF and NTR (fertilizer producers, food-security plays), ADM and BG (grain traders, the picks-and-shovels of agriculture). The ADR gaps are equally significant: BABA and PDD (Chinese e-commerce), TM (Toyota, the world’s largest automaker), SAP (European enterprise software), SHEL and BP (European oil majors), BHP/RIO/VALE (the three largest diversified miners in the world), NVS and AZN (European pharma), and LVMH (luxury goods, the largest company in Europe). These aren’t obscure names — they’re among the most widely held stocks globally.

ATLAS

L9 — Deep sector ETFs: Layer 9 covers niche sector ETFs, but several important ones are missing. OIH (oil services — Schlumberger, Halliburton, Baker Hughes; the companies that drill and maintain wells, not the ones that own the oil) is the most leveraged play on oil prices because services spending is the first thing E&P companies cut in a downturn and the first thing they ramp in a recovery. XME (metals and mining) provides diversified exposure to steel, aluminum, copper, and gold miners. XAR and PPA (aerospace and defense) benefit from rising military budgets globally. PPH and IHF (pharmaceuticals and health insurance) are defensive sectors with drug-pricing and regulation risk. IYZ (telecom) is the classic “boring utility-like” sector. BJK (gaming and casinos) captures Macau, Las Vegas, and online gambling. PEJ (leisure and entertainment). IGF (global infrastructure — toll roads, airports, utilities). SEA (shipping) moves with global trade volumes and is one of the most cyclical sectors in existence.

ATLAS

L10 — Advanced fixed income: The advanced bond layer is missing several instruments that institutional investors use daily. MBS (mortgage-backed securities) were covered in the L2 gap above. BNDX (international bonds, hedged to USD) provides diversification away from the US Treasury curve. SRLN (senior bank loans) holds floating-rate corporate debt that resets with SOFR — unlike fixed-rate bonds, these benefit from rising rates, making them the mirror image of traditional bonds. JBBB (CLO BBB tranche ETF) and SHYG (short- duration high-yield) offer different risk/reward profiles within credit. Private credit — direct lending by non-bank institutions — has grown into a trillion-dollar market that competes directly with high-yield bonds and bank loans. Callable bonds (bonds the issuer can redeem early) and their convexity characteristics (negative convexity means they underperform in rallies because the issuer calls them away) are foundational concepts for anyone holding corporate or municipal bonds.

ATLAS

L11 — Missing countries: The global map layer is missing several investable country ETFs that represent distinct economic stories. ARGT (Argentina) has been one of the best-performing country ETFs since Milei’s election and market reforms. EWQ (France) is the largest eurozone-only play. EIS (Israel) offers exposure to a tech-heavy economy with unique geopolitical risk. EWN (Netherlands) is dominated by ASML, the semiconductor lithography monopoly. GREK (Greece) has been a turnaround story since the debt crisis. EPHE (Philippines) is a frontier-market play on demographics and remittances. The Nordic economies (Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Finland) have distinct characteristics — Norway is an oil-funded sovereign wealth play, Sweden has a strong export sector, Denmark is dominated by Novo Nordisk — and none have cards.

ATLAS

L12 — Commodity gaps: JO (the coffee ETN) was redeemed in June 2023 and the card is now dead — it points to a security that no longer exists. Coffee is still investable via futures or the Teucrium suite. Cocoa and sugar both had extreme moves in 2024–2025 and have no representation. Lumber is historically one of the best leading indicators for housing and the broader economy. UGA (gasoline) and BNO (Brent crude) offer different exposure points than the standard WTI crude funds. SIL (silver miners) provides leveraged exposure to silver prices through the companies that mine it, similar to how GDX provides leveraged gold exposure.

ATLAS

L13 — Strategy ETFs: Several important strategy and alternative ETFs are missing. PFIX (Simplify Interest Rate Hedge) is designed to profit from rising long-term rates — effectively a put option on bonds, useful as a hedge when you think inflation will surprise to the upside. SVOL (Simplify Volatility Premium) sells VIX futures to harvest the volatility risk premium, producing high yields but with significant tail risk. BTAL (AGF US Market Neutral Anti-Beta) goes long low-beta stocks and short high-beta stocks, profiting from a “risk-off” environment. KMLM (KFA Mount Lucas Managed Futures) is a trend-following CTA strategy that provides crisis alpha — it tends to make money exactly when stocks crash, as it did in 2022. SWAN (Amplify BlackSwan Growth & Treasury) combines S&P 500 exposure with Treasury options designed to protect against crashes. QAI (IQ Hedge Multi-Strategy) attempts to replicate hedge fund returns in an ETF wrapper.

ATLAS

L14 — Indicator dashboard: Layer 14 is the regime-identification toolkit that the Playbook, Income Architect, and Metal Forge all need but none currently link to. Its gaps are significant. ISM Services PMI (the services economy is ~80% of US GDP, making this arguably more important than manufacturing PMI). Weekly initial jobless claims (the most real-time labor market indicator). JOLTS (Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey, which measures the job openings-to-unemployed ratio — the Fed watches this closely). The Sahm Rule (when the 3-month moving average of unemployment rises 0.5% above its 12-month low, a recession has begun — it has correctly called every recession since 1970). Retail sales, consumer sentiment, and inflation expectations surveys. Housing starts (a leading indicator because homes take months to build). LEI (Conference Board Leading Economic Index, a composite of 10 forward-looking indicators).

Valuation gauges: CAPE (cyclically adjusted P/E ratio, the Shiller metric), forward P/E, and the equity risk premium (stock earnings yield minus Treasury yield — when this is near zero, stocks offer no compensation for their extra risk vs bonds). Real yields and breakevens (the market’s inflation expectation derived from TIPS vs nominal Treasuries). The 3-month/10-year yield curve (the most reliable recession predictor). Funding stress indicators: the SOFR–IORB spread and FRA-OIS spread, which measure strain in the banking system’s short-term funding markets. Breadth indicators beyond the simple advance/decline line: percentage of stocks above their 200-day moving average, and new highs minus new lows. Sentiment surveys: AAII (retail investors), NAAIM (active managers), and COT (Commitment of Traders, showing how commercial hedgers vs speculators are positioned in futures). Net Liquidity (Fed balance sheet minus TGA minus reverse repo) is defined conceptually in Layer 0 but never appears on the indicator dashboard as a trackable metric.

ATLAS

Global Atlas gaps: A glossary and ticker index would make the Atlas dramatically more usable — with 500+ assets, a reader who encounters an unfamiliar ticker has no way to search for it. A cash and money-market layer (comparing money market funds, SGOV, direct T-bills, and brokered CDs as cash vehicles) should be foundational. A derivatives layer covering index futures (ES, NQ, YM), SPX options (the most liquid options market in the world, used for portfolio hedging and income), and how these instruments relate to the underlying ETFs would connect the institutional and retail worlds. Finally, each layer should have a “figures as of [date]” line so the reader knows exactly how current the data is.

Content Gaps: Income Architect

INCOME

Ch 2 — Annuities (the largest structural hole): A guide about generating income from a portfolio that doesn’t cover annuities is like a guide about transportation that skips trains. A SPIA (Single Premium Immediate Annuity) is the purest form of income product: you give an insurance company a lump sum and they pay you a fixed amount every month for the rest of your life. You can’t outlive it, you can’t run out, and the payment never changes. A DIA (Deferred Income Annuity) works the same way but starts payments years later, providing longevity insurance at a lower cost because the insurance company keeps your money longer. A QLAC (Qualified Longevity Annuity Contract) is a special DIA purchased inside an IRA that lets you defer up to $200,000 from RMDs until age 85 — useful for people who don’t need their RMD income but are forced to take it.

These aren’t exotic products — they’re the only instruments in existence that guarantee you won’t outlive your money, which is the central fear of every retiree. CDs and brokered CDs (FDIC-insured, fixed-term, predictable income) are also missing. Royalty trusts (companies that own oil/gas wells or mineral rights and distribute nearly all cash flow) are a classic yield-trap category that income investors repeatedly fall for — the high yields look attractive until production declines and distributions get cut. Interval funds and non-traded BDCs offer higher yields than public markets but lock up your capital and have limited transparency — products the guide should explain and caution against.

INCOME

Ch 3 — Dividend ETFs and concepts: VYM (Vanguard High Dividend Yield) is the largest dividend ETF by assets and represents a fundamentally different approach than the dividend-growth ETFs the guide covers — VYM buys the highest current yielders rather than the fastest growers, producing more income today at the cost of potentially slower growth. COWZ (Pacer US Cash Cows 100) represents the shareholder-yield concept: selecting companies that return the most capital through dividends and buybacks combined. Buybacks vs dividends as capital return mechanisms is a critical comparison the guide skips — buybacks are more tax-efficient (they increase your share of the company rather than paying you taxable income), but dividends provide tangible cash flow and impose discipline on management.

The 61-day qualified-dividend holding period rule is a practical tax trap: if you sell a stock fewer than 61 days after the ex-dividend date, the dividend is taxed as ordinary income (up to 37%) rather than the preferential qualified rate (0/15/20%). Individual stock examples showing real dividend histories, payout ratios, and growth rates would make the guide far more concrete. The 2009 evidence — S&P 500 dividends fell roughly 20% peak-to-trough, and about 10% of payers cut or suspended dividends during COVID in 2020 — is essential stress-test data for the guide’s “dividends are real cash” argument. They are real cash, but they’re not guaranteed, and the guide should show the worst cases.

INCOME

Ch 4 — Bond instruments: Target-maturity ETFs (iBonds from iShares, BulletShares from Invesco) are bond ETFs that hold bonds all maturing in the same year and then liquidate, returning your principal. They combine the diversification of a fund with the maturity certainty of an individual bond — you know when you’ll get your money back, unlike a perpetual bond fund like BND. A TIPS ladder (buying individual TIPS maturing in each year of retirement) creates an inflation-adjusted income floor. USFR (floating-rate Treasuries) resets its yield quarterly, so it barely moves in price regardless of rate changes. I-Bonds (from TreasuryDirect, $10k annual limit) are inflation-protected savings bonds with no market risk — the most conservative inflation hedge available to retail investors.

High-yield and emerging-market bond ETFs (HYG, JNK for US high-yield; EMB for dollar-denominated EM debt) are named in prose but never get their own cards with yield, risk, and default-rate context. Callable bonds — bonds the issuer can redeem early, typically when rates fall — have negative convexity: their upside is capped because the issuer calls them away. This is the dominant structure in corporate and municipal bonds, and income investors need to understand why their bond fund might underperform in a rate rally. Agency MBS have the same issue due to mortgage prepayments.

INCOME

Ch 4B — International dividends: SCHY (Schwab International Dividend Equity) is the international equivalent of SCHD and offers high-quality foreign dividend stocks with a different portfolio construction than the existing recommendations. VIGI (Vanguard International Dividend Appreciation) and IGRO (iShares International Dividend Growth) apply the dividend-growth philosophy internationally.

The foreign tax credit (FTC) limitation and the $300/$600 simplified election are practical tax mechanics every international dividend investor needs to understand. When you receive dividends from foreign stocks, the foreign country withholds taxes (typically 15–30%). You can reclaim this on your US tax return as a foreign tax credit, but the credit is limited to the US tax you’d owe on that income. If you hold international funds in a Roth IRA, the foreign withholding is simply lost — there’s no US tax to offset against. The $300/$600 simplified election lets taxpayers with modest foreign income skip the complex Form 1116 and take the credit directly on Form 1040 — a valuable simplification the guide doesn’t mention.

INCOME

Ch 7 — Preferreds depth: Baby bonds are $25 par exchange-traded debt instruments — they look like preferred stocks on your brokerage screen but are actually senior unsecured bonds, higher in the capital structure. They trade on stock exchanges, pay interest (not dividends, so no qualified dividend tax treatment), and have a stated maturity date, making them more bond-like than true preferreds. The $1,000-par institutional preferred market is much larger than the retail $25-par market but requires a brokerage that offers bond trading.

AT1/CoCo (Additional Tier 1 / Contingent Convertible) bonds are the instrument class that Credit Suisse wiped out in 2023. When Swiss regulators forced UBS to absorb Credit Suisse, they zeroed out $17 billion in AT1 bonds while equity holders received $3.2 billion — violating the normal hierarchy where equity takes losses first. This is the most important case study in preferreds/hybrid capital since 2008 and belongs in this chapter. Most bank preferreds (the kind retail income investors buy) are non-cumulative, meaning if the bank suspends the dividend, it never has to make up the missed payments. This is a critical risk that the guide doesn’t disclose.

INCOME

Ch 8 — Covered calls (DIY section missing): The table of contents promises a “rolling your own” covered call section, but it doesn’t exist. A reader interested in selling options on their own stocks needs to understand strike selection (how far out-of-the-money to sell, balancing premium received against upside given up), delta as the probability of getting called away (a 30-delta call has roughly a 30% chance of being in-the-money at expiration), cash-secured puts (selling puts on stocks you want to buy, getting paid while you wait), and the wheel strategy (selling puts until assigned, then selling covered calls until called away, repeating).

SPYI and QQQI (NEOS S&P 500 and Nasdaq Enhanced Options Income) use a different approach than the existing covered-call ETFs, employing put spreads instead of pure call-writing. 0DTE (zero days to expiration) covered-call ETFs represent a new category that writes daily options for income. YieldMax single-stock income funds (TSLY on Tesla, NVDY on Nvidia) are advertised with distribution rates of 30–80% but most of that is return of capital as the NAV erodes. They belong in Chapter 10 as a “distribution rate ≠ yield” warning — the poster child for why headline yield is misleading.

INCOME

Ch 9 — Closed-end funds: PDI (PIMCO Dynamic Income) and NUV (Nuveen Municipal Value) are named in the chapter’s prose but never get their own profile cards with the yield, leverage ratio, premium/discount history, and distribution coverage that a CEF buyer needs. More importantly, two critical analytical tools are missing. The discount z-score measures how far a CEF’s current discount (or premium) is from its historical average — a z-score below −2 means it’s trading at an unusually wide discount, potentially a buying opportunity. Distribution coverage and UNII (Undistributed Net Investment Income) measure whether the fund is actually earning enough to sustain its distribution. When UNII goes negative, the fund is paying out more than it earns and will eventually cut — this has destroyed wealth in funds like ARR, NLY, and numerous muni CEFs.

INCOME

Ch 9B — Private credit: Non-traded BDCs like BCRED (Blackstone Private Credit) have attracted hundreds of billions from retail investors seeking higher yields than public markets. They invest in direct loans to middle-market companies that can’t or won’t access public bond markets, paying yields of 8–12%. CLO ETFs like JAAA (highest-rated tranche) and JBBB (investment-grade but lower-rated tranche) package pools of leveraged loans into structured tranches with different risk/reward profiles — JAAA has never suffered a principal loss in any vintage, while lower tranches carry meaningful default risk.

The 2025 private-credit scare around First Brands and Tricolor Automotive is the stress test the guide needs. These were portfolio companies of major private-credit funds that ran into trouble, raising questions about whether the “private credit is safer than high-yield” narrative was overstated. The key issue: private credit marks its positions to model rather than market, so losses can be hidden until they’re catastrophic. An income investor allocating to BCRED or similar products needs to understand that the smooth NAV isn’t the same as low risk.

INCOME

Ch 10 — Yield measurement: The guide uses yield numbers throughout but never defines which kind. SEC 30-day yield is a standardized, forward-looking measure based on the fund’s current holdings and their stated coupons, net of expenses. Trailing twelve-month (TTM) yield is backward-looking — what the fund actually paid over the last year. Distribution rate is the annualized most recent distribution divided by price. These three numbers can differ by 100+ basis points for the same fund: a bond ETF that recently took capital gains might show a high TTM yield that won’t repeat, while its SEC yield reflects the actual current earning power. A reader comparing SCHD’s 3.5% TTM yield to JEPI’s 7.2% distribution rate is comparing apples to oranges, and the guide never says so.

INCOME

Ch 11 — Tax depth: The 0% qualified-dividend and long-term capital gains bracket applies to taxable income up to about $89,250 (married filing jointly, 2024). For early retirees living on modest income, this means they can receive qualified dividends and sell appreciated stock tax-free at the federal level. This is enormously powerful and the guide doesn’t mention it.

IRMAA (Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount) is a stealth tax on Medicare premiums that catches retirees who don’t plan for it. If your modified adjusted gross income exceeds $206,000 (married, 2024), your Medicare Part B and D premiums increase by $1,000 to $7,000+ per year depending on the bracket. A single Roth conversion or capital gain can push you into a higher IRMAA bracket, and the surcharge applies for the entire year based on income from two years prior. The taxation of Social Security benefits uses a “provisional income” formula that creates effective marginal rates of 22.2% to 40.7% — higher than the nominal bracket — in the phase-in zone. State income taxes on dividends and interest vary wildly: nine states have no income tax, while California and New York tax investment income at rates exceeding 10%.

INCOME

Ch 12 — Regime context: The stagflation regime (rising inflation + slowing growth) is the hardest environment for income investors because bond prices fall (rates rising), stock dividends get pressured (slowing economy), and real returns turn negative (inflation outpacing income). The 1970s and 2022 are the two modern examples. The guide discusses regimes but doesn’t cover stagflation specifically, which is the regime where the “live on dividends” strategy is most stressed. It should also point to Atlas Layer 14 as the shared indicator set for identifying which regime you’re currently in.

INCOME

Ch 14 — Portfolio costs: The model portfolios show projected income but not their weighted expense ratio — the blended annual cost of owning the portfolio. A portfolio that’s 30% JEPI (0.35% ER), 20% SCHD (0.06%), 15% HYG (0.49%), and 10% PDI (1.95% effective cost including leverage) has a very different total cost than one built from Vanguard index funds. The reader needs to see this number to make an informed decision.

Fee layering for CEFs and BDCs is particularly opaque: a BDC might charge a 1.5% base management fee plus a 20% incentive fee on returns above a hurdle, plus fund-level borrowing costs of 3–5%. The all-in cost can be 4–8% annually, which is hidden because the distributions are reported net of fees. The guide shows the yield but never the cost of earning it.

INCOME

Ch 15 — RMDs and Roth conversions: Required Minimum Distributions start at age 73 (under current law) and force you to withdraw a percentage of your traditional IRA each year whether you need the money or not. The guide recommends holding income-producing assets in IRAs for tax efficiency — but RMDs force you to liquidate those exact holdings. If your IRA has grown large, the forced withdrawals push you into higher tax brackets, trigger IRMAA surcharges, and increase the taxable portion of your Social Security. This is a structural conflict with the “never touch principal, live on natural yield” philosophy that the guide advocates.

Roth conversions during the gap years (between retirement and age 73) can permanently solve this by moving IRA money to a Roth while your taxable income is low. Bracket management — converting just enough each year to “fill up” the 12% or 22% bracket without spilling into the next one — is a core retirement tax strategy that the guide completely omits.

INCOME

Ch 16 — Social Security depth: Spousal benefits allow a lower-earning spouse to claim up to 50% of the higher earner’s benefit at the higher earner’s full retirement age. Survivor benefits provide the surviving spouse with 100% of the deceased spouse’s benefit, replacing their own smaller benefit. These rules fundamentally change the optimal claiming strategy for married couples and the guide doesn’t cover them.

The earnings test before full retirement age (FRA) reduces benefits by $1 for every $2 earned above ~$22,320 (2024) if you claim early and keep working. The withheld benefits are added back after FRA, but most people don’t know this and either avoid claiming early (when they should) or are shocked by the reduction. The Roth-conversion window — converting traditional IRA money to Roth during the years between retirement and when you claim SS — exploits the lowest-income period of your life for permanent tax savings. If you retire at 62, don’t claim SS until 70, and have no other income, you can convert large amounts at the 10–12% bracket rate. This is one of the most powerful retirement planning tools and the guide’s Social Security chapter ignores it entirely.

INCOME

Global Income Architect gaps: Chapters labeled 4B and 9B should be renumbered as proper sequential chapters. The letter suffixes signal that these were inserted after the original structure was built, and they make the table of contents look inconsistent. Each chapter should also have a brief sources section citing the data behind its income projections and yield assumptions.

PSLV (Sprott Physical Silver Trust) holds allocated silver bars in Canada and avoids the futures-roll costs of SLV, making it the preferred physical-silver vehicle for many investors — but it’s a PFIC (passive foreign investment company) that requires a QEF election to avoid punitive tax treatment, a critical detail covered nowhere in the guide. PALL (Aberdeen Physical Palladium) is the only physical palladium ETF. SILJ (ETFMG Prime Junior Silver Miners) offers leveraged exposure to silver through smaller, higher-risk mining companies. URNM (Sprott Uranium Miners) provides diversified exposure to the nuclear fuel supply chain.

Individual stocks that are named repeatedly but never profiled: Newmont (the world’s largest gold miner, recently acquired Newcrest), Barrick (now Barrick Mining, ticker B, the second-largest gold miner), Agnico Eagle (the third-largest, with exclusively Tier-1 jurisdictions), Royal Gold (described as “one of three dominant streamers” but the guide never explains what it actually does — it buys royalty and streaming agreements that give it the right to purchase gold at a fixed cost below market, creating an asset-light, high-margin model). BHP, Rio Tinto, and Vale are the three largest diversified miners in the world and essential context for understanding the copper, iron ore, aluminum, and nickel markets the guide discusses. SCCO (Southern Copper) is the world’s largest publicly traded copper company. Ivanhoe Mines is the highest-profile development-stage miner. Cameco dominates uranium production. Albemarle is the largest Western lithium producer. MP Materials operates the only active rare-earth mine in the United States.

FORGE

Ch 8 — Industrial metal equities: The chapter discusses industrial metals (aluminum, tin, copper, zinc) but doesn’t tell you how to invest in them through equities. Alcoa and Century Aluminum (CENX) are the main US aluminum producers. Glencore is the world’s largest diversified commodity trader/miner, covering copper, zinc, nickel, cobalt, and thermal coal. Alphamin and MLX are the dominant tin producers (tin is increasingly critical for semiconductor soldering). Teck Resources and Boliden provide diversified base metal exposure across copper, zinc, and lead.

Simandou, the massive iron ore deposit in Guinea, is expected to come online and significantly increase global iron ore supply. This matters because iron ore is dominated by three companies (BHP, Rio Tinto, Vale) and any supply shock changes the pricing dynamics for steel globally. The guide discusses iron ore as a commodity but doesn’t mention the single largest upcoming supply event.

FORGE

Ch 10/18 — Mining business model: Hedge books are one of the most consequential business decisions a gold miner can make. Barrick’s late-1990s hedge book is the case study: the company sold forward millions of ounces at $300–400/oz, locking in revenue but giving up all upside. When gold eventually rallied above $1,000, Barrick was still delivering at the hedged price, costing shareholders billions in lost profits. It took years and billions in cash to close the hedge book. This is why most gold miners today operate unhedged and why investors care intensely about any miner’s hedging activity.

Co-product vs byproduct AISC (All-In Sustaining Cost) accounting is how miners manipulate their reported cost per ounce. A copper-gold mine can allocate 100% of its costs to copper (making gold a zero-cost byproduct with infinite margin) or split costs by revenue contribution (co-product). Same mine, same costs, radically different reported gold AISC. Investors comparing AISC across companies without checking which method is used are comparing fiction. Streaming obligations (like those from Franco-Nevada, Royal Gold, and Wheaton Precious Metals) give the streamer the right to buy future gold production at a fixed price well below market. For the miner, streaming provides upfront capital but creates a debt-like obligation that reduces the effective gold price received on every ounce produced for the life of the mine.

FORGE

Ch 14 — Valuation: Mining stocks are notoriously difficult to value, and the guide discusses qualitative factors without providing a valuation framework. Free-cash-flow yield at spot price answers the most basic question: if gold stays at today’s price, how much cash does this miner generate relative to its market cap? Reserve price decks vs spot is a subtler issue: mining companies book their reserves using conservative price assumptions ($1,400–1,700 gold) far below current spot ($5,000+). This means reported reserve ounces are dramatically understated — the economic cutoff grade at $5,000 gold is much lower than at $1,700, which means more rock is economic, which means the mine has more reserves than the financial statements show. EV/EBITDA is the standard valuation metric for base-metal miners, where cash-flow multiples are more useful than price/earnings because of heavy capital expenditure cycles.

FORGE

Ch 21 — Signals: The real-yield model — the idea that gold moves inversely to real interest rates — was the dominant gold framework for two decades and it has broken since 2022. Real yields (10-year TIPS yield) went above 2%, which historically would have crushed gold, but gold tripled instead. The primary driver shifted from Western ETF flows (which respond to real yields) to central bank purchases and Asian physical demand (which respond to de-dollarization and geopolitical risk). The guide still presents the real-yield framework as primary without noting that it no longer works.

Gold making all-time highs in non-USD currencies (euro, yen, yuan, rupee) before making ATHs in dollars is historically a leading signal — it means gold is strengthening everywhere, not just because the dollar is weakening. COMEX registered vs eligible inventory is a measure of deliverable supply: registered gold is available for delivery on futures contracts, while eligible is stored in COMEX vaults but not committed. When registered inventory drops while open interest stays high, it signals potential delivery stress. EFP (Exchange for Physical) basis trading — the spread between futures and physical — normally stays tight but blew out dramatically during the COVID panic and again in early 2025 when tariff fears drove London-to-New-York gold shipments.

FORGE

Ch 28 — Drill results: CuEq and AuEq (copper-equivalent and gold-equivalent) grade reporting is a promotional trick used by junior miners to inflate their headline grades. A project with 0.3% copper and 0.2 g/t gold can be presented as “0.7% CuEq” by converting the gold value into copper equivalents at favorable metal prices. The problem: the conversion depends on which metal prices and recovery assumptions you use, and juniors typically choose the most flattering ones. A reader evaluating drill results needs to know this is marketing, not geology.

S-K 1300 (the SEC’s mining disclosure standard, effective 2021) should be discussed alongside the existing coverage of NI 43-101 (Canadian) and JORC (Australian). S-K 1300 modernized US mining disclosure to be more comparable with international standards but has differences in how “measured” and “indicated” resources are defined. A US-listed miner reports under S-K 1300 while a Canadian-listed miner reports under NI 43-101, and the same deposit can look meaningfully different depending on which standard is used.

FORGE

Ch 32 — Lassonde curve: The chapter describes the lifecycle of a mining project — early exploration (stock spikes on discovery), feasibility studies (stock drifts as the market waits), permitting and construction (the “orphan period” where enthusiasm fades), and finally production (stock re-rates to cash-flow value). This pattern is universally known in mining circles as the Lassonde Curve, named after Pierre Lassonde (co-founder of Franco-Nevada), and the guide never names it. This is like describing supply and demand without calling it supply and demand. Any mining investor searching for this concept will look for the Lassonde Curve by name and won’t find it in the guide.

FORGE

Ch 29 — Tax: Canadian-listed miners (Barrick, Agnico, Franco- Nevada, many juniors) withhold 25% on dividends by default, reduced to 15% under the US-Canada tax treaty. In a taxable account, this withholding is recoverable as a foreign tax credit. In an IRA, it is simply lost — the US-Canada treaty does not exempt retirement accounts (unlike several other treaties). This means a Canadian miner paying a 3% dividend effectively yields only 2.55% in an IRA, which changes the optimal account placement.

NIIT (the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax) applies to all investment income including collectibles gains, meaning gold ETF profits face a potential 28% + 3.8% = 31.8% combined federal rate. State sales tax on physical bullion varies widely: some states exempt it entirely (Texas, Florida), others charge full sales tax (Vermont, New Mexico), and many have minimum-purchase exemptions ($1,000+). PFIC/QEF elections for Sprott trusts (PHYS, PSLV, SPPP) are required to avoid the PFIC penalty regime, which taxes gains at the highest ordinary rate plus an interest charge. The guide recommends PSLV without mentioning that it requires an annual tax filing election that most retail investors don’t know about.

FORGE

Ch 22 — Physical gold: Costco gold bars have become a surprisingly significant retail demand channel — the company reportedly sells $100–200 million per month in 1-ounce gold bars and silver coins, making it one of the largest retail bullion dealers in the United States. This democratization of physical gold ownership is a new phenomenon and reflects the cultural shift the guide discusses but doesn’t connect to a real distribution channel.

Self-directed gold IRA mechanics are also missing. You can hold physical gold (coins, bars meeting purity requirements) inside an IRA, but it requires a specialized custodian, approved depository storage (you can’t keep it at home), and transaction fees that are typically much higher than buying a gold ETF. The guide should explain when this makes sense (large allocations, distrust of counterparties, estate planning) and when it doesn’t (small allocations where fees eat the return, people who want liquidity).

FORGE

Ch 36 — Steel: U.S. Steel is one of the most historically significant American industrial companies and the subject of a blocked Nippon Steel acquisition (President Biden blocked it on national security grounds in early 2025). The guide’s steel chapter should have a U.S. Steel card covering its legacy integrated mills, competitive position vs EAF (electric arc furnace) producers like Nucor and Steel Dynamics, and the political dimension of foreign ownership.

Cleveland-Cliffs (CLF) acquired Stelco, a major Canadian steelmaker, in 2024, continuing its consolidation strategy after buying ArcelorMittal USA and AK Steel. Section 232 steel tariffs are now at 50% (doubled from the original 25% in 2018), fundamentally changing the import economics and domestic pricing power. The chapter still references the 2018 tariff rate, which understates the level of protection domestic producers currently enjoy.
