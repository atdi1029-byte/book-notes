If You Only Remember 10 Things

  1. Speculative manias are not aberrations but inherent, recurring features of capitalism that have not changed in character since the Dutch Tulipomania of 1636.
  2. The financial memory lasts no more than twenty years — long enough for a new generation to arrive believing it has discovered something genuinely new.
  3. Every celebrated financial "innovation" is merely leverage — debt secured by inadequate assets — dressed in new clothing.
  4. Money is reflexively associated with intelligence: the richer the person, the deeper the presumed genius, shielding the wealthy from the scrutiny that might save them.
  5. Speculation buys up the intelligence of those involved — no one wants to believe their enrichment is accidental, so rising prices silence critical thinking.
  6. Those who express doubt during a boom are attacked as envious, ignorant, or treasonous; only after the crash does anyone recall their warnings.
  7. The crash always comes as a bang, never a whimper — both true believers and those riding the wave rush for the exits simultaneously.
  8. After every crash, blame falls on external causes (budget deficits, program trading, hurricanes) while the speculation itself is never examined.
  9. Financial genius is before the fall — association with great wealth creates the illusion of brilliance that vanishes the moment the money does.
  10. Regulation cannot prevent speculative manias; the only defense is personal skepticism and the recognition that when excitement pervades a market, it is time for caution, not participation.

Foreword to the 1993 Edition

Galbraith opens the 1993 edition with a confession that his optimism about the social utility of warning people has dimmed. He had originally hoped that business executives and ordinary citizens might be reminded of how reliably people are separated from their money during speculative manias. Three years later, he is less certain. Recurrent speculative insanity and the financial devastation it causes are, he is now persuaded, inherent in the system. Perhaps it is better that this be recognized and accepted rather than resisted with futile warnings that nobody heeds until after the crash.

The main players of the 1980s boom had by 1993 met their predictable fates. Michael Milken, "perhaps the most spectacular figure of the last boom and certainly the best paid," was serving time in a minimum-security prison. Donald Trump was reported to have a negative net worth. The Reichman brothers and Robert Campeau, Canada's gifts to financial excess, were indubitably broke, though they had at least erected monuments — the Canary Wharf development in London being "perhaps the most awesome recent example of speculative dementia."

Recurrent speculative insanity and the associated financial deprivation and larger devastation are, I am persuaded, inherent in the system. Perhaps it is better that this be recognized and accepted. — Galbraith, Foreword

The 1980s speculative orgy in real estate was financed largely through the savings and loan associations at the expense of the American taxpayer. Salomon Brothers estimated that empty commercial real estate would take an average of twelve years to absorb — twenty-six years in Boston, forty-six in New York, and fifty-six in San Antonio, "the leader, so to speak, in this provision for the future." The effects extended far beyond real estate: R.H. Macy struggled under the heavy load of debt from leveraged buyouts, and across the country enterprises were similarly afflicted by debts incurred during "the years of financial pillage and devastation."

The recession that began in the summer of 1990 was almost certainly caused and prolonged by the speculative collapse. Public confidence was shaken, corporate investment curtailed, banks forced to restrict lending, workers discharged. Had there been no speculative excess, the political history of 1992 would have been far different — the boom and collapse ended George H.W. Bush's presidency. Without the recession, his reelection would have been "certain, a cinch." With Herbert Hoover, Bush stands as one of two Presidents destroyed by Wall Street. In politics, as in other matters, one must beware one's friends.

Chapter 1: The Speculative Episode

The free-enterprise economy is given to recurrent episodes of speculation — great events and small, involving bank notes, securities, real estate, art, and other assets — stretching across years and centuries. What has not been sufficiently analyzed, Galbraith argues, are the features common to these episodes, the things that signal their certain return and have the considerable practical value of aiding understanding and prediction. Regulation and orthodox economic knowledge are not what protect the individual when euphoria returns. There is protection only in a clear perception of the characteristics common to these flights into what must conservatively be described as mass insanity.

The obvious mechanics of speculation are manifestly clear. Some artifact or development — tulips in Holland, gold in Louisiana, real estate in Florida, the free-enterprise vision of Ronald Reagan — captures the financial mind. The price goes up. The increase attracts new buyers; the new buyers assure a further increase. Yet more are attracted; yet more buy; the increase continues. The speculation building on itself provides its own momentum — a self-reinforcing loop where rising prices are simultaneously the cause and the evidence of continued prosperity.

Anyone taken as an individual is tolerably sensible and reasonable — as a member of a crowd, he at once becomes a blockhead. — Friedrich von Schiller, as quoted by Bernard Baruch

Two groups of participants drive the process, and their interaction ensures the inevitable crash. The first group are the true believers — those persuaded that some new price-enhancing circumstance is in control and expect the market to go up permanently. They are adjusting to a new situation, "a new world of greatly, even infinitely increasing returns." The second group, generally fewer in number, are the riders — those who perceive or believe themselves to perceive the speculative mood and plan to ride the wave before getting out ahead of the crash. Their particular genius, they are convinced, will allow them to "get the maximum reward from the increase as it continues" and be out before the eventual fall.

Built into this situation is the eventual and inevitable collapse. It cannot come gently or gradually — when it comes, it bears the grim face of disaster. Both groups are programmed for sudden escape. Something, "it matters little what — although it will always be much debated," triggers the ultimate reversal. The riders decide now is the time to get out. The true believers find their illusion destroyed abruptly. Both respond to the newly revealed reality by selling or trying to sell. And thus the rule, supported by centuries of experience: the speculative episode always ends not with a whimper but with a bang.

Less understood is the mass psychology that sustains the euphoria and suppresses dissent. To save themselves, individuals must resist two compelling forces: the powerful personal interest that develops in the euphoric belief, and the pressure of public and seemingly superior financial opinion brought to bear on behalf of that belief. Schiller's dictum applies with full force — the crowd converts the individual from reasonably good sense to the stupidity against which "the very Gods Themselves contend in vain."

The vested interest in error is an extremely plausible phenomenon. Those involved with the speculation are experiencing an increase in wealth — getting rich or being further enriched. No one wishes to believe this is fortuitous or undeserved; all wish to think it is the result of their own superior insight or intuition. The very increase in values thus captures the thoughts and minds of those being rewarded. Speculation buys up, in a very practical way, the intelligence of those involved. Even Walter Bagehot, the astute financial writer and early editor of The Economist, observed that "all people are most credulous when they are most happy."

Reinforcing this vested interest is the condemnation that reputable public and financial opinion directs at those who express doubt. Paul M. Warburg, one of the most respected bankers of his time and a founding parent of the Federal Reserve System, warned in the winter of 1929 of "unrestrained speculation" and predicted a disastrous collapse. The reaction was bitter, even vicious — he was held to be obsolete, accused of "sandbagging American prosperity," and suspected of being short in the market. There was more than a shadow of anti-Semitism in the response. Roger Babson similarly foresaw a crash in September 1929, predicting a 60- to 80-point drop in the Dow. Barron's dismissed him for his "notorious inaccuracy," and even Irving Fisher, the most innovative economist of the day, spoke out sharply against him.

Galbraith cites his own experience as further proof. In 1955, he testified before the Senate Banking Committee about a speculative buildup. In the days that followed, the postman brought letters condemning him, including threats of CIA action and prayers for his "richly deserved demise." When he broke his leg skiing shortly after, speculators wrote that their prayers had been answered. In 1986, he wrote an article for The New York Times about the speculative buildup — the editors rejected it as too alarming. The Atlantic published it instead. Before the October 1987 crash, the response was sparse and hostile: "Galbraith doesn't like to see people making money." After October 19, "almost everyone I met told me that he had read and admired the article." The critic must wait until after the crash for any approval, not to say applause.

Chapter 2: The Common Denominators

The first factor contributing to speculative euphoria, and perhaps the most important, is the extreme brevity of the financial memory. Financial disaster is quickly forgotten. When the same or closely similar circumstances occur again, sometimes in only a few years, they are hailed by a new, often youthful, and always supremely self-confident generation as a brilliantly innovative discovery. There can be few fields of human endeavor in which history counts for so little as in the world of finance. Past experience, to the extent that it is part of memory at all, is dismissed as the primitive refuge of those who do not have the insight to appreciate the incredible wonders of the present.

There can be few fields of human endeavor in which history counts for so little as in the world of finance. Past experience, to the extent that it is part of memory at all, is dismissed as the primitive refuge of those who do not have the insight to appreciate the incredible wonders of the present. — Galbraith

The second factor is the specious association of money and intelligence. In all free-enterprise attitudes there is a strong tendency to believe that the more money a person possesses or controls, the deeper and more compelling his economic and social perception. Money is the measure of capitalist achievement — the more money, the greater the intelligence that supports it. This view is reinforced by the air of self-confidence and self-approval commonly assumed by the affluent, who rudely inform ordinary people: "I'm afraid that you simply don't understand financial matters." In fact, such reverence for the possession of money indicates the shortness of memory, the ignorance of history, and the consequent capacity for self- and popular delusion.

The association extends with particular force to the leadership of great financial institutions — large banking, investment-banking, insurance, and brokerage houses. The larger the capital assets and income flow controlled, the deeper the presumed financial and social perception. In practice, individuals at the top of these institutions are often there precisely because theirs was the most predictable mind and, bureaucratically, the least inimical of the contending talent. They are endowed with authority that encourages acquiescence from subordinates and applause from acolytes, and are thus "admirably protected in what may be a serious commitment to error."

Another factor compounds the delusion: those with money to lend are, by long force of habit and tradition, accorded deference by borrowers. This deference is readily transmuted by the recipient into an assurance of personal mental superiority — "I must be wise." Self-scrutiny, the greatest support to minimal good sense, is thereby put at risk. In the 1970s, the greatest of the New York banks, praising their own success in recycling Arab oil revenues, made durably unfortunate loans to Latin America, Africa, and Poland. It was intellectually questionable men in intimate and protected association with large assets who fed money through the ridiculous Penn Square Bank in Oklahoma City to the outstretched hands in the neighboring oil patch, and on to the manifold disasters of the great Texas oil and real estate speculations, and who initiated the terrible savings and loan debacle.

In the chapters that follow, Galbraith shows how the investing public is fascinated and captured by the great financial mind. That fascination derives from the scale of the financial operations and the feeling that, with so much money involved, the mental resources behind them cannot be less. Only after the speculative collapse does the truth emerge. What was thought to be unusual acuity turns out to be only a fortuitous and unfortunate association with the assets. The rule will be reiterated throughout: financial genius is before the fall.

Uniformly in all speculative events there is the thought that something is genuinely new in the world. It can be the arrival of tulips in Western Europe, the seeming wonders of the joint-stock company, the accommodation of the markets to the Reagan-era vision of free enterprise, or the miracle of high-risk junk bonds supporting corporate raiders and leveraged-buyout specialists. In all speculative episodes there is an element of pride in discovering what is seemingly new and greatly rewarding. This perception of novelty rewards the ego of the participant even as it is expected to reward his pocketbook. And for a while it does.

Yet experience establishes a firm rule: financial operations do not lend themselves to innovation. What is recurrently so described and celebrated is, without exception, a small variation on an established design that owes its distinctive character to the brevity of the financial memory. The world of finance hails the invention of the wheel over and over again, often in a slightly more unstable version. All financial innovation involves, in one form or another, the creation of debt secured in greater or lesser adequacy by real assets. This was true when banks first discovered they could print notes in excess of their hard-money deposits — there seemed to be no limit to the debt that could be leveraged on a given volume of cash. The limit became apparent only when alarming news caused too many depositors to want their money at the same time. All crises have involved debt that has become dangerously out of scale in relation to the underlying means of payment.

The world of finance hails the invention of the wheel over and over again, often in a slightly more unstable version. — Galbraith

The final common feature of the speculative episode is what happens after the inevitable crash. There will be anger, recrimination, and scapegoating — the individuals previously most admired for their financial imagination and acuity will become objects of condemnation. There will be scrutiny of the previously praised financial instruments — paper money, implausible securities issues, insider trading, program trading. What will not be discussed is the speculation itself or the aberrant optimism that lay behind it. Two reasons protect the speculative mood from blame: first, too many people were involved to attribute error to an entire community without challenging the presumption that intelligence is associated with money; second, free-enterprise theology holds markets are inherently perfect and rational, so the cause must be found external to the market itself. After 1987, the deficit in the federal budget became the decisive factor. After 1929, it was a weakening in industrial production. The speculation is never blamed.

Chapter 3: The Tulipomania; John Law and the Banque Royale

Speculative episodes go back to the days of the Florentines and the Venetians — active securities markets existed in Genoa, Florence, and Venice as early as the 14th century. But the first modern speculative explosion occurred in the stable, wide-horizoned land of the Dutch in the 1630s, and it remains to this day one of the most remarkable. It was not in stocks or real estate or in the superb Dutch paintings; it was in tulip bulbs, and it has come down over the last 350 years with a name of its own — the Tulipomania.

The tulip — Tulipa of the lily family Liliaceae, of which there are around 160 species — first came to Western Europe from the eastern Mediterranean in the 16th century; a cargo arriving in Antwerp from Constantinople in 1562 is thought to have been especially important. Enormous prestige was soon attached to the possession and cultivation of the plant. Speculation settled on the more esoteric blooms. Appreciation of the flowers rapidly gave way to a deeper appreciation of the increase in the price that their beauty and rarity were commanding. By the mid-1630s, the increase seemed to be without limit. The rush to invest engulfed the whole of Holland — no person of minimal sensitivity of mind felt that he could be left behind.

Prices were extravagant. By 1636, a bulb of no previously apparent worth might be exchanged for "a new carriage, two grey horses and a complete harness." A Semper Augustus bulb was worth some 3,000 florins — an unimaginable $25,000 to $50,000 in modern terms. Bulbs now changed hands several times at steadily increasing and wonderfully rewarding prices while still unseen in the ground. Charles Mackay, in Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds, tells the story of a young sailor who, rewarded with a fine red herring for bringing word of goods from the Levant, found and ate what he took to be an onion — a Semper Augustus bulb worth 3,000 florins, consumed contentedly along with the fish.

Nobles, citizens, farmers, mechanics, seamen, footmen, maid-servants, even chimney-sweeps and old clotheswomen, dabbled in tulips. People of all grades converted their property into cash, and invested it in flowers. — Charles Mackay

Mackay's account of the mood is vivid: regular marts established on the Stock Exchange of Amsterdam and in Rotterdam, Harlaem, Leyden, and other towns. Everyone imagined the passion for tulips would last forever and that the wealthy from every part of the world would send to Holland and pay whatever prices were asked. In the smaller towns, the principal tavern was usually selected as the "show-place," where high and low traded in tulips and confirmed their bargains over sumptuous dinners attended by two or three hundred persons. Money was borrowed for purchase; the small bulbs leveraged large loans.

In 1637 came the end. The wise and the nervous began to detach, no one knows for what reason; others saw them go; the rush to sell became a panic; the prices dropped as if over a precipice. Those who had purchased by pledging property for credit were suddenly bereft or bankrupt. "Substantial merchants were reduced almost to beggary, and many a representative of a noble line saw the fortunes of his house ruined beyond redemption." Courts refused to enforce contracts, calling it a gambling operation. The state emerged as the recourse of last resort. The collapse had a chilling effect on Dutch economic life — an appreciable depression followed. One mitigating result: tulip cultivation continued and wide markets for flowers and bulbs eventually developed.

Missing from the history of the Tulipomania are the names of the major players — a deficiency that does not afflict the next of the classic episodes, which is dominated by John Law, one of the central figures in all financial history. Law was born in 1671 in Edinburgh, the son of a goldsmith who was actively engaged in holding and lending money. The younger Law moved to London at an early age where, along with enjoying a markedly sybaritic existence, he gave some attention to banking and finance. This education was drastically interrupted in 1694 when, in a duel in what is now Bloomsbury, he was "unduly successful" in bringing his opponent down with a sword "to his belly." Arrested for murder, he escaped prison and made his way to the Continent, where he evidently made his living as a highly resourceful gambler who had worked out the odds in a contemporary version of craps.

Law observed the operations of the great and successful Bank of Amsterdam and conceived the idea of a land bank that would issue notes secured by real estate. He arrived in Paris in 1716 at a propitious moment: Louis XIV had died the year before, leaving the Regent Philippe II, Duc d'Orléans — "a man who combined a negligible intellect with deeply committed self-indulgence" — and a bankrupt treasury drained by the Sun King's wars and extravagance. On May 2, 1716, Law was accorded the right to establish the Banque Royale with a capital of six million livres, with authorization to issue notes to pay government expenses and absorb government debts.

To support the note issue, Law organized the Mississippi Company (Compagnie d'Occident) to pursue gold deposits "presumed to exist" in Louisiana. There was no evidence of the gold, but this was no time for doubters. Shares were offered to the public and the response was sensational. The old bourse in the Rue Quincampoix saw the most intense, even riotous operations in all the history of financial greed. The scheme was essentially circular: proceeds from stock sales went not to search for gold but to pay government debts; the notes that paid the debt came back to buy more stock, which lifted prices higher. All the notes were presumed backed by coin in the Banque Royale, but the amount of coin was soon minuscule in relation to the volume of paper. Here was leverage in a particularly wondrous form.

In 1720 came the end. The precipitating factor was the Prince de Conti, annoyed by his inability to buy stock, who sent his notes to the Banque Royale to be exchanged for gold. A run developed — people seeking to convert notes into gold, not into Mississippi Company stock. To restore confidence, a battalion of Paris mendicants was recruited, equipped with shovels, and marched through the streets as though on the way to mine gold in Louisiana. Weeks later, many were seen back at their old haunts. On one July day in 1720, fifteen people lost their lives in the crush in front of the Banque Royale. Notes were declared no longer convertible; values collapsed. Citizens who had been millionaires — an indispensable term given to us by these years — were now impoverished.

Law's fate exemplifies the core pattern. Associated with wealth, he was genius — intelligence derived from association with money. He had been made Comptroller General of France and the Duc d'Arkansas, "a title one wishes might have survived." When the wealth dissolved, he was a fugitive mercilessly reviled. Protected by the Regent, he left France, spent four years in England, then moved to Venice, where according to the Duc de Saint-Simon, he "lived, in decent poverty, a quiet and virtuous life, died there in the Catholic faith, piously receiving the Sacraments of the Church." In the aftermath, the French economy was depressed, "a tiny minority enriched by the total ruin of all the rest of the people." Blame fell on Law and his Banque Royale — not on the speculation and its gulled participants. French banks would be regarded with suspicion for a century.

Chapter 4: The Bubble

As the great speculation was coming to its unpredicted but wholly predictable climax in Paris in 1720, so was another in London — insanity born of optimism and self-serving illusion, a tale of two cities. The British version lacked the French style. It was, by comparison, a rather ordinary boom and collapse in securities prices augmented by a comprehensive exercise in official bribery, corruption, and chicane. The "discovery" that justified the boom was the joint-stock company — already more than a hundred years old but suddenly, with the brevity of financial memory, the new wonder of finance.

In the early years of the 18th century there had been notably imaginative stock promotions: a company to build and market a typewriter (considerably ahead of its time), a project for a machine gun that could fire both round and square bullets depending on whether the enemy was Christian or Turk, a project for a mechanical piano. Sir Isaac Newton observed: "I can measure the motions of bodies, but I cannot measure human folly." Nor could he do so as regards his own — he was to lose £20,000, now a million dollars and much more, in the speculative orgy to come.

I can measure the motions of bodies, but I cannot measure human folly. — Sir Isaac Newton

The South Sea Company was born in 1711 at the instigation of Robert Harley, Earl of Oxford, and one John Blunt, a scrivener. Its origins closely resembled those of the Banque Royale and the Mississippi Company — it similarly provided a seeming solution to floating government debt from the War of the Spanish Succession. In return, the Company received 6% interest and the right to "the sole trade and traffick" with the Americas. The inconvenient fact that Spain claimed monopoly over this same trade was thoughtfully overlooked, though Spain briefly allowed exactly one voyage per year, subject to a share in its profits. It would be hard, in fact, to imagine a commercial project that was more questionable.

But this was not a time for questions. Further issues of stock were authorized in exchange for assumptions of public debt, and by early 1720 the whole public debt was assumed. The legislation was facilitated by the endowment of gifts of South Sea stock to key ministers of the government, and by the happy circumstance that several directors sat in Parliament. In 1720, the British public responded powerfully to the seeming opportunity. The stock went from around £128 in January to £330 in March, £550 in May, £890 in June, and to around £1,000 later in the summer. As ever, the sight of some becoming effortlessly affluent brought the rush to participate that further powered the upward thrust.

Nor was the South Sea Company the only opportunity. Its success spawned at least a hundred imitators and hitchhikers hoping to take part in the boom: companies to develop perpetual motion, to insure horses, to improve the art of making soap, to trade in hair, to transmute quicksilver into malleable fine metal, as well as the immortal enterprise "for carrying on an undertaking of great advantage, but nobody to know what it is." In July 1720, the government called a halt — the Bubble Act was passed prohibiting these other promotions, less to protect the innocent than to secure the speculative monopoly of the South Sea Company itself.

By this time the end of the Company was in sight. The stock went into a tail-spin — partly the result of inspired profit-taking by insiders. By September it was down to £175, by December to £124. Heroic efforts to sustain and revive confidence failed, including an appeal to the newly formed Bank of England. Eventually shares leveled off at around £140, approximately one-seventh of their peak value. As before, and as ever: once the crash comes, it overrides all efforts to reverse the disaster.

The search for scapegoats was fierce, even brutal. Blunt, by now Sir John Blunt, narrowly escaped death when an assailant sought to shoot him down in a London street. He later saved himself by turning in to the government his fellow conspirators. Directors were expelled from Parliament, their money and estates confiscated. Robert Knight, the company's treasurer, departed suddenly for the Continent and lived in exile for twenty-one years. James Craggs, an influential elder statesman, committed suicide. Others went to jail.

Charles Mackay pointed out the truth with singular acuity: "Nobody seemed to imagine that the nation itself was as culpable as the South-Sea company. Nobody blamed the credulity and avarice of the people — the degrading lust of gain...or the infatuation which had made the multitude run their heads with such frantic eagerness into the net held out for them by scheming projectors. These things were never mentioned." Nor in the aftermath of modern speculation are they ever mentioned.

Chapter 5: The American Tradition

The financial memory is brief, but subjective public attitudes can be more durable. John Law created a suspicion of banks in France that endured for a century or longer. The South Sea Bubble warned Britain against joint-stock companies; the Bubble Act restricted the formation of limited companies for many succeeding decades. However, by 1824 these enterprises had regained sufficient respectability for another wave of London stock promotions, inspired in part by prospects in South America and including a company committed to draining the Red Sea "with a view to recovering the treasure abandoned by the Egyptians after the crossing of the Jews."

Whatever the London excesses, in the last century the speculative imagination was at work in its most ardent form in the United States. This was because of a special American commitment to the seeming magic of money creation and its presumptively wondrous economic effects. The commitment began in colonial times. The Southern colonies — Maryland, Virginia, and Carolina — issued notes against the security of tobacco. In Maryland, tobacco-backed notes served as currency for nearly two centuries, longer by a considerable margin than the gold standard was to last.

More dramatically, in 1690 Sir William Phips led an expedition from the Massachusetts Bay Colony to the fortress of Quebec. It was intended that captured treasure would pay the costs; alas, the fortress did not fall, and there was no hard money in the colonial treasury to pay the returning troops. It then seemed a minor step to issue paper notes promising eventual payment in gold or silver — a seemingly innovative and wonderful financial instrument that was, of course, debt backed by markedly fewer solid assets than were available should all the notes be presented at once. The wonder spread to other colonies; notes were issued with abandon. Rhode Island was an extreme case — there, the notes became worthless.

Benjamin Franklin supported paper money, influenced perhaps by his business as a printer of the notes. Not all colonies succumbed — Pennsylvania, New York, New Jersey, Delaware, and Maryland exercised admirable restraint. Eventually, in 1751, Parliament forbade the paper issues in New England and later in the other colonies, provoking sharp anger. The Continental notes later financed the Revolution — soldiers paid in Continentals found their buying power rapidly diminishing (shoes at $5,000 a pair, a full outfit of clothing over $1,000,000). Thus was American independence purchased, and it is not clear that it could have been bought in any other way.

The new republic's financial policy was initially conservative — the Constitution forbade paper money. The First Bank of the United States was created to enforce discipline on state-chartered banks by refusing to accept the notes of those that did not pay out in specie on demand. The conservative Northeast approved; the South and West most certainly did not. In 1810, under attack for its financial rigor, the charter was not renewed. With the stimulus of the War of 1812, state banks were chartered with abandon — "every location large enough to have a church, a tavern, or a blacksmith shop was deemed a suitable place for setting up a bank." These banks issued notes, and other, more surprising enterprises imitated them: "Even barbers and bartenders competed with the banks in this respect." The assets behind these notes were, it need hardly be said, minuscule and evanescent. Leverage once again.

The Second Bank of the United States was chartered in 1816, but it too involved itself enthusiastically in real estate loans. The boom collapsed in 1819 — the first of the speculative episodes with resulting collapse that would characterize American economic history for the rest of the century. The word panic entered the financial language. Later, in eager search of milder reference, crisis, depression, recession, and now growth adjustment came successively to denote the economic aftermath. The Second Bank was blamed for the collapse (it had begun calling in notes), and Andrew Jackson rallied public opinion against it, aided by Nicholas Biddle's arrogant enforcement. The charter was not renewed. Nearly a century would pass before a central bank would again be tolerated in the United States.

The 1837 crash was a speculative bubble in real estate, manufacturing, and public lands, financed by proliferating banks and massive British investment in canals and turnpikes. Its aftermath introduced debt repudiation — Mississippi, Louisiana, Maryland, Pennsylvania, Indiana, and Michigan all repudiated their debts to foreign creditors. Only the pathological weakness of the financial memory allows us to believe that the modern experience of Third World debt — Argentina, Brazil, Mexico — is in any way a new phenomenon.

The pattern continued through the 1857 panic, and then through the devastating 1873 crash, when Jay Cooke & Company, heavily involved in railroad financing, failed in September. Two large banks went under. The New York Stock Exchange was closed for ten days. Banks suspended payment in coin. A group of Michigan banks cooperated in sharing the same gold reserves, transferring them from one institution to the next just ahead of the examiner. On some occasions, the top layer of gold coins in the container was given a more impressive height by a larger layer of ten-penny nails below. In the more conservative precincts of New England, a bank was closed up with $500,000 in notes outstanding and a specie reserve of $86.48 in hand.

Joseph Schumpeter later conceptualized boom and bust as the predictable business cycle, normalizing mania as a detail in a larger process where depression benignly purged the system and restored sanity. University courses accepted the alternation between high and low as routine. The 1907 panic was memorable for J.P. Morgan's intervention — calling on public and private funds to rescue the endangered Trust Company of America and asking New York clergymen to preach sermons of encouragement. "A crash can come to an end without divine intervention." After World War I, a speculative surge in farm land left farmers with debt that in the Great Depression exceeded the aggregate value of all rural property. But overshadowing all previous episodes was the great stock-market boom of the later 1920s.

Chapter 6: 1929

In the larger history of economics and finance, no year stands out as does 1929. It is richly evocative in the public memory, partly because the speculative debacle was of special magnitude, and more because it ushered in for the United States and the industrial world the most extreme and enduring crisis that capitalism had ever experienced. 1929 is also remembered because there were then evident all the elements of the euphoric episode and especially the powerful commitment to presumed financial innovation — the rediscovered wonders of leverage, the parade of publicly celebrated genius, optimism built on optimism driving prices up, and the eventual discovery of severe mental and moral deficiencies in those once thought endowed with genius.

The justifying mood was the political, social, and economic order associated with the Republican administration of Calvin Coolidge and his Treasury Secretary, Andrew W. Mellon. Then came the presidency of the more experienced Herbert Hoover. It was a mood to be repeated with the advent of Ronald Reagan a little less than sixty years later — and this recurrence was not entirely an accident. Most of those who manage investment operations or have sizable amounts of money to invest are Republican in their politics. They naturally believe in the politicians they support and the economic advantage flowing therefrom. It is especially easy for those seemingly so blessed to be persuaded of the approximately infinite opportunities for enrichment inherent in a Republican age. So in 1929; so again before the crash in 1987.

The first manifestation of the euphoric mood of the 1920s was seen not on Wall Street but in Florida — the great real estate boom of the middle of that decade. Lots could be purchased for a cash payment of around 10 percent — leverage again. Prices could be expected to double in a matter of weeks. Charles Ponzi of Boston, whose name is durably associated with investment operations that paid early investors from the money coming in from later ones, had turned to the real estate business. He developed a subdivision said to be "near Jacksonville" — it was approximately 65 miles away. Choice "beachfront" lots could, by a flexible approach to measurement, be 10 or 15 miles from the water. In 1926 the inevitable collapse came — blamed not on the speculation but on two especially vicious Caribbean hurricanes. Miami bank clearings fell from $1,066,528,000 in 1925 to $143,364,000 in 1928.

By 1928, the speculative mood had shifted to lower Manhattan. Stock prices had been rising since 1924 and took clear leave of reality in 1928 and particularly in 1929. In the spring of 1929, the Federal Reserve Board announced that it might tighten interest rates to arrest the boom — seen as economic sabotage. Charles E. Mitchell, head of the National City Bank, stepped in to counter the threat: "We feel that we have an obligation which is paramount to any Federal Reserve warning, or anything else, to avert any dangerous crisis in the money market." His bank would lend money as necessary to offset any restraint by the Federal Reserve. The effect was more than satisfactory: in three summer months, the increase in prices outran all of the quite impressive increase that had occurred during the entire previous year.

We feel that we have an obligation which is paramount to any Federal Reserve warning, or anything else, to avert any dangerous crisis in the money market. — Charles E. Mitchell, head of National City Bank, 1929

Leverage was magnificently available. In its most commonplace form, it allowed the purchase of stock on a 10 percent margin — 10 percent from the aspiring owner, 90 percent from the obliging lender. Interest rates climbed to 7 to 12 percent and once as high as 15. The closed-end investment trusts were the era's great innovation. United Founders Corporation, rescued with a $500 infusion from a friend in 1921, borrowed money and sold securities for an eventual total of around a billion dollars — assets worth a billion from an original investment of $500, "the most notable exercise of leverage of all time," the Michigan banks and their ten-penny nails possibly excepted.

Goldman, Sachs launched the Goldman Sachs Trading Corporation in late 1928 with the sole purpose of holding and speculating in common stocks. The Trading Corporation launched Shenandoah Corporation, which launched Blue Ridge Corporation, repeating the process — leveraged gains flowing upward through the chain. Unrecognized was the way this process would work in reverse, the fixed obligations commanding diminishing market values. Goldman Sachs Trading Corporation shares were issued at $104, rose to $222.50, and in the late spring of 1932 stood at $1.75.

The most celebrated men of the time were those riding and furthering the boom: Arthur W. Cutten, Bernard E. "Sell 'Em Ben" Smith, M.J. Meehan, Albert H. Wiggin of the Chase, Ivar Kreuger the Swedish match king, and Richard Whitney, the most eminent broker and soon president of the NYSE. Supporting them was a convocation of economics professors. The most prominent was Irving Fisher of Yale, the most innovative economist of his time. Heavily invested in the market, he surrendered to the basic speculative impulse — to believe whatever best serves the good fortune you are experiencing. In the autumn of 1929, he gained enduring fame for the widely reported conclusion that "stock prices have reached what looks like a permanently high plateau."

Beginning on October 21 came the end. The market opened badly the week of October 21; things turned worse on Wednesday; Thursday was the first of the days of disaster. At noon on Thursday, the great bankers — Thomas Lamont of Morgan's, Mitchell, and Wiggin — met at the House of Morgan and resolved to act. Richard Whitney appeared on the floor of the Exchange making stabilizing purchases. The confidence evaporated over the weekend. Tuesday, October 29, was the most devastating day in the history of the Exchange. Nothing arrested the rush to sell or the likelihood of being sold out. Rumors circulated that the great bankers were themselves getting out — which may well have been the case.

In the aftermath, there were two predictable developments. Mitchell and Wiggin were sacked. Mitchell spent a decade fighting income tax evasion charges — he had unloaded depreciated stock on his wife to take a capital-loss deduction. Wiggin, also a large operator heavily short in the stock of his own bank, was denied his pension. Cutten suffered acute amnesia. Meehan absent-mindedly went abroad. Whitney went to Sing Sing for embezzlement. Ivar Kreuger, once a world-class financier, went out one day in Paris in 1932, bought a pistol, and shot himself — he had been discovered to have counterfeited Italian government bonds. Fisher lost millions and was rescued by Yale. Joseph P. Kennedy and Bernard Baruch shared the rewards and lasting esteem that came from having gotten out early. Predictable also was the evasion: the market had merely reflected external influences, not the speculation. Some useful steps were taken — creation of the SEC, restraints on holding-company pyramiding, control of margin requirements — but attention was always on the instruments, never on the tendency to speculation itself.

Chapter 7: October Redux

The financial memory should be assumed to last, at a maximum, no more than twenty years. This is normally the time required for a new generation to enter the scene, impressed with its own innovative genius, and bemused by the two further influences that are greatly seductive of error: the ease with which any individual, on becoming affluent, attributes his good fortune to his own superior acumen, and the companion tendency of the many who live in more modest circumstances to presume an exceptional mental aptitude in those who are identified with wealth. Only in the financial world is there such an efficient design for concealing what will be revealed as self- and general delusion.

The crash of 1929 ushered in the Great Depression, for which the stock-market debacle should rightly be held sharply responsible — demand for consumers' goods weakened, business confidence was shaken, investment fell, and business failures rose. Economists, then as later, sought to exculpate the market, holding deeper factors responsible. This was evasion bordering on nonsense. The market crash broke into an exceptionally fragile financial, banking, and general economic structure. But the 1929 crash, along with John Law and the South Sea Bubble, remained more vividly in memory than the typical 20-year cycle would predict. The result was a somewhat longer period of "doubt, caution, and comparative sanity."

By the mid-1950s, Americans were ceasing to regard the stock market with the post-1929 misgiving. The 1960s were years of good performance — low unemployment, steady growth, low inflation — and youthful market operators, notably the Go-Go boys, were believed to have a new and highly innovative approach to investment. Relatively mild setbacks in 1962 and 1969 partially corrected this error.

The most notable manifestation of the new speculative mood occurred in Switzerland under American auspices. Investors Overseas Services (IOS) was the brainchild of Bernard Cornfeld and Edward Cowett — the first a former social worker, the second an unquestionably accomplished lawyer. IOS was the guiding force for a large group of mutual funds, of mutual funds investing in other mutual funds (including the Fund of Funds, which invested in the IOS itself), and of firms to sell and manage mutual funds. But most of all it was a vast sales organization in which salesmen recruited other salesmen who recruited yet other salesmen for commissions. The pyramid in Germany was eventually some six stories high, and only a fraction of the original investment found its way into the securities it was meant to buy — the rest went into all those commissions. Cornfeld's compelling sales appeal: "Do you sincerely want to be rich?"

Do you sincerely want to be rich? — Bernard Cornfeld's compelling appeal to investors

IOS attracted eminent names to its board: James Roosevelt (FDR's son), Sir Eric Wyndham White (longtime GATT secretary-general), and a former German vice-chancellor. In 1969, declining sales and securities prices worked back through the leveraged structure with reverse effect. Cornfeld yielded the remaining assets to Robert Vesco, the fugitive financier who eventually found refuge in Cuba. Galbraith had been invited to join the board by James Roosevelt but declined, guided by a general rule against corporate boards reinforced by specific misgivings from Swiss bankers.

In these same years there was the enthusiastic reception given to Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs), and the self-congratulatory enthusiasm of the great international banks — Citibank, Manufacturers Hanover, Continental Illinois — as they received the sizable deposits of the OPEC oil-producing countries and sent them on in the form of loans to Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Poland, and other eager recipients. This recycling was considered innovative and imaginative — an operation at the highest level of financial respectability. Its legacy was either default or an oppressive burden of debt for the countries so favored, gravely threatening their living standards and the stability of their governments. Much of the borrowed money was recycled, without local benefit, back to Switzerland or New York.

The 1980s brought the full reassertion of speculative mania. All elements were in place, as the age of Calvin Coolidge had been hailed, so now the age of Ronald Reagan. Leverage returned in the form of corporate takeovers and leveraged buyouts, with the requisite new financial instrument — junk bonds, whose novelty resided only in their deeply valid name. A new generation of young men on Wall Street exhibited the necessarily abbreviated memory. Dennis Levine and Ivan Boesky, the most famed, were on their way to disgrace and prison before the crash. Milken and Drexel Burnham Lambert encountered difficult days after. Fortune magazine concluded of Drexel: "Did Drexel do itself in? Or was it done in? The truth is that this was a case of suicide — and murder."

The crash of October 19, 1987 was predictable and well in the established pattern. Galbraith had dealt with it and its parallels with 1929 in early 1987 in the Atlantic, speaking of a "day of reckoning...when the market goes down seemingly without limit." He predicted it would be less devastating than 1929 because of structural changes — a welfare system, farm-income supports, trade-union support to wages, deposit insurance for banks and S&Ls, and a broad Keynesian commitment by government to sustain economic activity. These things, all absent after 1929, had lent a resilience to the economy.

The aftermath of 1987 saw an especially notable exercise in evasion. Former Secretaries of the Treasury, professional public spokesmen, and corporate executives sponsored a New York Times advertisement attributing the crash to the federal budget deficit — a deficit that had already persisted unchanged for the preceding six years of the Reagan administration. Next came studies by the NYSE, the SEC, and a presidential task force chaired by Nicholas F. Brady. The Brady Commission did not entirely overlook the preceding speculation — five or six early pages affirmed a bull market had existed. But it then superbly emphasized subordinate factors — program trading, portfolio insurance, specialist misbehavior. The SEC report was devoted entirely to market performance and "strategies" during the crash, with no mention of the circumstances that induced it. Speculation and its aftermath are recurrent, inherent, unfortunate characteristics of markets extending over centuries — but this went mostly unmentioned.

Chapter 8: Reprise

There are few references in life so common as that to the lessons of history, and few warnings so regularly ignored. Those who know history are not doomed to repeat it; those who do not know it are. The circumstances that induce the recurrent lapses into financial dementia have not changed in any truly operative fashion since the Tulipomania of 1636-1637. Individuals and institutions are captured by the wondrous satisfaction from accruing wealth. The associated illusion of insight is protected by the public impression that intelligence marches in close step with the possession of money. Out of that belief comes the bidding up of values. The upward movement confirms the commitment to personal and group wisdom. And so on to the moment of mass disillusion and the crash — which never comes gently, and is always accompanied by a desperate and largely unsuccessful effort to get out.

Inherent in this sequence are the elements by which it is comprehensively misunderstood. Those who are involved never wish to attribute stupidity to themselves. Markets are also theologically sacrosanct — the market can be subject to monopoly, imperfect competition, or errors of information, but apart from these it is intrinsically perfect. Yet clearly the speculative episode, with increases provoking increases, is within the market itself. And so is the culminating crash. Such a thought being theologically unacceptable, it is necessary to search for external influences — the downturn in the summer of 1929, the budget deficit of the 1980s, the "market mechanisms" that brought the crash of 1987. In the absence of these factors, the market presumably would have remained guiltless. There is nothing in economic life so willfully misunderstood as the great speculative episode.

There is nothing in economic life so willfully misunderstood as the great speculative episode. — Galbraith

The final question is what, if anything, should be done. Recurrent descent into insanity is not a wholly attractive feature of capitalism. The human cost is not negligible, nor is the economic and social effect. After 1929, the damage was very great and contributed visibly to the depression. After 1987, the heavy residue of debt from leveraged buyouts crowded out productive investment, and individuals and pension funds suffered from losses on junk bonds.

Yet beyond a better perception of the speculative tendency and process itself, there is probably not a great deal that can be done. Regulation outlawing financial incredulity or mass euphoria is not a practical possibility. If applied generally to such human condition, the result would be an impressive, perhaps oppressive, and certainly ineffective body of law. The only remedy, in fact, is an enhanced skepticism that would resolutely associate too evident optimism with probable foolishness and that would not associate intelligence with the acquisition, the deployment, or the administration of large sums of money.

Galbraith offers two unfailing rules. First: there is the possibility, even the likelihood, of self-approving and extravagantly error-prone behavior on the part of those closely associated with money. Let the individual investor and the pension and institutional-fund manager be guided by this. Second: when a mood of excitement pervades a market or surrounds an investment prospect, when there is a claim of unique opportunity based on special foresight, all sensible people should circle the wagons; it is the time for caution. Perhaps there is opportunity. Maybe there is treasure on the floor of the Red Sea. A rich history provides proof, however, that, as often or more often, there is only delusion and self-delusion.

No one concluding such an essay can expect to escape the questions: When will come the next great speculative episode, and in what venue — real estate, securities, art, antique automobiles? To these there are no answers, and anyone who presumes to answer does not know he doesn't know. But one thing is certain: there will be another of these episodes and yet more beyond. Fools, as it has long been said, are indeed separated, soon or eventually, from their money. So, alas, are those who, responding to a general mood of optimism, are captured by a sense of their own financial acumen. Thus it has been for centuries; thus in the long future it will also be.

Trading & Investing Lessons

The 20-Year Memory Cycle Is a Tradeable Pattern

Galbraith identifies approximately twenty years as the maximum span of the financial memory. Each new generation enters the market convinced of its own unique insight, dismissing the warnings of their predecessors as primitive. For the trader, this means that the current crop of innovators claiming to have discovered something genuinely new should be treated with extreme skepticism, particularly when their innovation involves leverage in a new costume. The traders who got out early — Kennedy, Baruch — earned lasting esteem precisely because they recognized patterns their peers refused to see.

Leverage Is Always the Tell

Every speculative episode Galbraith catalogs involves leverage — debt secured by inadequate assets. Tulip bulbs leveraged large loans. The Banque Royale's notes vastly exceeded its coin reserves. 1920s stocks were bought on 10% margin. 1980s leveraged buyouts loaded companies with junk bond debt. For the trader, rapidly expanding leverage in any asset class is the single most reliable warning signal that a speculative episode is underway. When margin debt hits new highs relative to GDP, when new financial instruments proliferate that allow more leverage, it is time for caution.

The Crowd Attacks the Skeptic — Use That as Confirmation

Warburg was vilified, Babson was dismissed, Fisher turned against those who warned, and Galbraith himself received death threats. When skepticism about a market is met with fury rather than argument — when the doubter is accused of envy, ignorance, or treachery — that emotional reaction is itself a signal. Markets that can tolerate criticism are not yet in euphoria; markets that punish it are. A trader who is attacked for expressing caution should take the attack as confirmation, not as a reason to change his view.

The Bang, Not the Whimper

Galbraith's rule that crashes come suddenly — not gradually — has immediate implications for position management. Stop-losses and trailing exits must be in place before the event, because the speed of the reversal leaves no time for orderly liquidation. Both true believers and riders rush for the exits simultaneously, creating selling pressure that overwhelms any support. The trader who plans to "get out near the top" is the rider in Galbraith's framework — and Galbraith's assessment of the rider's chance of success is mordant.

Watch What Gets Blamed After the Crash

After every crash, blame falls on everything except the speculation itself — budget deficits, program trading, hurricanes, regulatory failure. For the trader, this pattern of evasion signals that the next speculative episode is already being prepared. When post-crash analysis focuses on instruments and external causes rather than on the psychology of the boom, the financial community is revealing that it has not learned and will repeat the cycle. The time between the crash and the next mania is inversely proportional to the quality of the post-mortem.

Financial Genius Is Before the Fall

The most celebrated investment minds in every era were those riding the boom, not those standing apart. Mitchell, Wiggin, Milken, the Goldman Sachs Trading Corporation operators — all were deemed geniuses at the peak. For the modern trader, this means treating celebrity fund managers, viral investment influencers, and media-anointed market wizards with particular skepticism during periods of rising prices. The acclaim they receive is a function of their association with rising values, not of any demonstrable skill that will survive a reversal.

Key Takeaways

Speculative Manias Are Structural, Not Aberrant

They are built into the free-enterprise system and have recurred with remarkably consistent features for nearly four centuries. Expecting them to stop is as unrealistic as expecting human nature to change.

The Financial Memory Has a Half-Life of Twenty Years

Long enough for a new generation to enter the scene convinced of its own innovative genius. The specific objects of speculation change — tulips, land, stocks, junk bonds — but the psychology is identical.

All Financial Innovation Is Disguised Leverage

From bank notes in excess of reserves to investment trusts layered on investment trusts to leveraged buyouts financed by junk bonds, the fundamental mechanism is always debt secured by insufficient assets. The world of finance "hails the invention of the wheel over and over again, often in a slightly more unstable version."

Money Silences Criticism

The association of money with intelligence creates a feedback loop: the wealthy are presumed wise, their wealth confirms their wisdom, and anyone who questions them is dismissed as envious or ignorant. This mechanism protects the speculative episode from examination until after the crash.

Crashes Are Sudden, Not Gradual

The speculative episode always ends with a bang because both groups of participants — true believers and riders — are programmed for sudden escape. The trigger matters little; the dynamics of simultaneous exit are what make crashes violent.

The Speculation Is Never Blamed

After every crash, external causes are found — budget deficits, market mechanics, weather — because blaming the speculation would require acknowledging mass stupidity and challenging the theological perfection of markets. This evasion ensures the pattern will repeat.

Regulation Cannot Prevent Mania

You cannot outlaw foolishness or financial incredulity. Regulation addresses instruments (margin requirements, securities laws) but cannot touch the tendency to speculation itself. The only defense is personal skepticism.

Celebrity Wisdom Is a Lagging Indicator

"Financial genius is before the fall." Those most celebrated during a boom — Mitchell, Wiggin, Fisher, Milken — are precisely those who will suffer most in the aftermath. Acclaim during rising prices is evidence of nothing except association with rising prices.

Contrarian Ideas

Markets Are Not Inherently Rational

Free-enterprise doctrine holds that markets are intrinsically perfect, reflecting rational assessments of value apart from occasional external distortions. Galbraith insists the opposite: the speculative episode, with increases provoking increases and the culminating crash, is within the market itself. The internal dynamics of euphoria are the cause, not the reflection, of the instability.

Financial Innovation Is Mostly an Illusion

Conventional wisdom celebrates financial innovation as progress. Galbraith argues that virtually all innovation in finance is merely leverage in a new costume — debt creation secured by inadequate assets, rediscovered by each generation thanks to the brevity of the financial memory. The electric light was an invention; the junk bond was not.

Wealth Does Not Signal Intelligence

Society reflexively associates money with brains. Galbraith argues this is perhaps the most dangerous delusion in economic life. The rich are often indifferent to legal constraints, may have inherited, and frequently owe their position to bureaucratic mediocrity rather than insight. Their air of authority rests on deference from subordinates and borrowers, not on tested wisdom.

Post-Crash Regulation Misses the Point

After every crash, reforms target instruments — margin requirements, securities laws, program trading restrictions. Galbraith argues this addresses symptoms while ignoring the disease. Nothing can be done about the tendency to speculation itself, because it is rooted in human psychology rather than institutional failure. The SEC, the Bubble Act, even the abolition of banks were all reactions that left the fundamental impulse untouched.

Proposals & Frameworks

The Anatomy of a Speculative Episode (Galbraith's Invariant Pattern)

  1. The new thing: Something seemingly new captures the financial imagination — tulips, joint-stock companies, paper money, junk bonds, crypto
  2. Pride of discovery: Participants believe they have discovered this innovation ahead of the mob
  3. Self-reinforcing prices: Rising prices attract buyers whose purchases cause further increases
  4. Leverage amplification: Debt is created in excess of the underlying assets, multiplying both gains and vulnerability
  5. Genius by association: Those closest to the rising wealth are deemed geniuses; skeptics are condemned
  6. The sudden crash: Something triggers reversal; both true believers and riders rush for the exits; the collapse is always violent
  7. The scapegoat search: Previously admired figures are condemned; previously praised instruments are scrutinized
  8. The great evasion: External causes are found; the speculation itself is never blamed; markets are held theologically blameless
  9. Memory fades: Within twenty years, the cycle repeats with a new generation and a new "innovation"

Galbraith's Two Rules for Investors

  1. Assume that those closely associated with money are likely to be self-approving and extravagantly error-prone — do not defer to them
  2. When excitement pervades a market and there are claims of unique opportunity based on special foresight, circle the wagons — it is the time for caution, not participation

The Five Shields Against Reality

Galbraith identifies five mechanisms that protect the speculative episode from rational examination:

  1. Vested interest in error: profits capture the intelligence of participants
  2. Money-intelligence fallacy: wealth confers presumed wisdom
  3. Lender deference: bankers' respectful treatment of borrowers becomes self-assurance
  4. Brevity of memory: the 20-year cycle erases the lessons
  5. Market theology: free-enterprise doctrine holds markets inherently rational, so crashes must have external causes

What Didn't Fit

Galbraith's Personal War with the Speculators

Galbraith's 1955 testimony before the Senate Banking Committee produced an extraordinary reaction. The postman staggered in with condemning letters, the most extreme threatening CIA action, the mildest praying for his "richly deserved demise." When he broke his leg skiing days later and news-men reported the fact, letters arrived from speculators saying their prayers had been answered. He posted the most compelling communications in a seminar room at Harvard as an instruction to the young. The market recovered, and his mail returned to normal — a vivid personal demonstration that the euphoric episode protects itself by attacking critics with religious fervor.

The Japanese Accounting Dodge

When Japanese stocks took a large and wholly unexpected dive in March 1990, with major indices falling nearly a quarter, a leading investment house concerned with Japan reported that there was talk of changing the accounting rules so that "an institution that loses money in stocks can keep that fact confidential." A Washington Post dispatch added that it had been "a matter of received wisdom...that the Japanese stock market, manipulated by the government and big investment houses, can only go up." This perfectly illustrates Galbraith's point about the evasion that follows collapse — rather than examining why stocks fell, the impulse was to hide the losses entirely.

The Campeau-Trump Parallel

Robert Campeau's acquisition of Bloomingdale's and other great retail houses loaded them with crushing debt. Fortune called it "THE BIGGEST LOONIEST DEAL EVER." Howard Davidowitz, a New York retailing consultant, said of Campeau in MacLean's: "he was the guy at the head of the table who was pounding his fist and yelling, 'Do the damn deal.' No one could have stopped him." Galbraith's point is characteristically directed at the banks: "Almost no mention has been made of the deeply defective judgment of the banks that financed these men." The press and public reacted to the transformation of Campeau and Trump from "aggressively avowed geniuses" to "objects of condemnation" with selective outrage — condemning the borrowers while ignoring the lenders who enabled them.

The Bubble Act's Real Purpose

The Bubble Act of July 1720, passed by the British government to prohibit new stock promotions, is conventionally presented as protective legislation for innocent investors. Galbraith suggests a less charitable reading: the Act was passed less to protect the foolish and the innocent than "to secure the speculative monopoly of the South Sea Company itself." The directors who sat in Parliament had a direct interest in eliminating competitors for the speculative capital flowing into London. Legislative reform after a crash, then as now, may serve the interests of those already in position rather than those it claims to protect.

How Continental Currency Bought a Revolution

The Continental notes that financed the American Revolution were perhaps the most consequential exercise in paper money in history. Washington's soldiers were paid in Continentals that rapidly lost their value — shoes cost $5,000 a pair, a full outfit of clothing over $1,000,000. The cost of the war was borne by those who received and held the Continentals as their buying power diminished. Galbraith notes that American independence was thereby purchased, "and it is not clear that it could have been bought in any other way." The irony is that the new republic's Constitution then forbade the very instrument that had secured its freedom — a pattern of using leverage in extremis and then recoiling from it that would repeat throughout American financial history.

About the Author

John Kenneth Galbraith was born in 1908 in Ontario, Canada, and became one of the most influential economists and public intellectuals of the 20th century. He earned a PhD at the University of California in 1934 and took a fellowship at Cambridge, where he first encountered Keynesian economics. He taught at both Harvard and Princeton, served as deputy head of the Office of Price Administration during World War II (charged with preventing inflation from crippling the war effort), and was US Ambassador to India during the Kennedy administration.

Galbraith wrote more than forty books on an array of economic topics, including The Affluent Society (1958), The New Industrial State (1967), and The Great Crash, 1929 (1955), which remained continuously in print for decades — its sales reliably revived by each new speculative episode. He was known for his literary prose style, sardonic wit, and willingness to challenge conventional economic orthodoxy, particularly the assumption that markets are inherently rational and self-correcting. He died in 2006 at the age of 97.

Galbraith's unique qualification to write this book was not merely academic expertise but personal experience across multiple speculative cycles. He testified before Congress about speculation in 1955, predicted the 1987 crash in print, and observed from a front-row seat as the celebrated geniuses of each era — from the investment trust operators of 1929 to the junk bond promoters of the 1980s — rose to adulation and fell to disgrace in the pattern he describes. His confession that even he was a "beneficiary of the speculative aberration in its association with more than occasional insanity" — since each crisis revived interest in his books — is characteristically self-aware.

Why This Book Matters

Written in 1990 and revised in 1993, A Short History of Financial Euphoria preceded the dot-com bubble, the subprime mortgage crisis, the crypto manias, and every subsequent episode of speculative excess by years or decades. Yet every one of those episodes followed Galbraith's pattern with eerie fidelity: a "new" innovation (the internet, mortgage-backed securities, blockchain), leveraged participation by a new generation convinced of its own insight, savage condemnation of skeptics, a violent crash, and an aftermath in which external causes were blamed while the speculation itself went unexamined. The book's predictive accuracy over thirty-plus years is its most powerful vindication.

At seventy-eight pages, this is perhaps the most efficient book ever written about financial markets. Galbraith distills four centuries of speculative history into a framework so simple it seems obvious — which is precisely his point. The pattern IS obvious; its power comes from the fact that each generation refuses to see it. For any trader or investor, the book serves as an inoculation: not against the temptation to speculate (Galbraith is too wise to promise that) but against the delusion that this time is different, that the new financial instrument is genuinely new, and that the celebrated genius of the hour is anything more than a man fortunately associated with rising prices.

The book's deepest value is its insistence that the problem is not institutional but psychological. No regulation, no reform, no watchdog agency can prevent speculative manias because they arise from the deepest instincts of human nature — the desire to believe that wealth confirms intelligence, the brevity of memory, the vested interest in error. Galbraith's solution — personal skepticism and the refusal to associate money with brains — is modest precisely because it is honest. He does not promise that wisdom will prevail. He promises only that those who recognize the pattern have a slim chance of escaping its consequences. That slim chance, in the world of finance, is the best anyone has ever offered.
