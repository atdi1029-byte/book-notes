# A Short History of Financial Euphoria — Summary

**Author:** John Kenneth Galbraith
**Published:** 1990 (revised 1993)
**Pages:** 78

## Core Thesis

Speculative manias are not aberrations but inherent features of free-enterprise capitalism. They recur with remarkable consistency because of two unchanging human traits: (1) the extreme brevity of financial memory, which ensures each new generation believes it has discovered something genuinely new, and (2) the specious association of money with intelligence, which shields the wealthy from scrutiny and silences skeptics. The pattern has not changed since the Dutch Tulipomania of 1636.

## The Invariant Pattern

Every speculative episode follows the same sequence:
1. Something seemingly new captures the financial imagination
2. Prices rise, attracting participants who attribute gains to their own insight
3. Leverage amplifies returns (all financial "innovation" is ultimately leverage — debt against limited assets)
4. Skeptics are condemned as lacking vision or envious
5. The crash comes suddenly — always a bang, never a whimper
6. Scapegoats are found; the speculation itself is never blamed
7. External causes are invented (budget deficits, program trading, weather)
8. Memory fades within ~20 years; the cycle repeats

## Key Concepts

- **Financial genius is before the fall** — association with money creates presumed intelligence that vanishes with the money
- **The 20-year memory cycle** — financial disasters are forgotten within a generation, allowing the next mania
- **Leverage as false innovation** — every celebrated financial "invention" (bank notes, holding companies, junk bonds) is merely debt secured by insufficient assets
- **Vested interest in error** — those profiting from speculation have powerful incentives to silence doubters and deny reality
- **Theological market perfection** — free-enterprise doctrine holds markets are inherently rational, so crashes must have external causes

## Historical Episodes Covered

1. **Tulipomania** (1630s Holland) — flower bulbs as speculative instrument
2. **John Law / Banque Royale** (1716-1720 France) — paper money and the Mississippi Company
3. **South Sea Bubble** (1720 England) — joint-stock company mania
4. **American banking panics** (1819, 1837, 1857, 1873, 1907) — paper money and bank note leverage
5. **Florida land boom** (1924-1926) — 10% down payments, Ponzi's involvement
6. **1929 crash** — investment trusts, margin buying, celebrated geniuses
7. **1960s Go-Go boys / IOS** — Bernard Cornfeld's mutual fund pyramid
8. **1987 crash** — junk bonds, leveraged buyouts, Reagan-era optimism

## Galbraith's Verdict

Regulation cannot prevent speculative manias — you cannot outlaw foolishness. The only defense is personal skepticism: assume that those associated with money are likely to be error-prone, and when excitement pervades a market with claims of unique opportunity, "all sensible people should circle the wagons." But even this defense is unlikely to hold against the sweeping power of financial euphoria.
