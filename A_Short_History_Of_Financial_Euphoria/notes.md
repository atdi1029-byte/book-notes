# A Short History of Financial Euphoria — John Kenneth Galbraith
## Notes

### FOREWORD TO THE 1993 EDITION

N001 [MUST KNOW] Galbraith's central thesis updated: "Recurrent speculative insanity and the associated financial deprivation and larger devastation are, I am persuaded, inherent in the system." He is less certain warnings help — perhaps it's better to simply recognize and accept this reality.

N002 [SHOULD KNOW] The 1980s speculative players met their fate: Michael Milken in prison, Donald Trump with negative net worth, the Reichman brothers and Robert Campeau "indubitably broke." The euphoria's monuments (Canary Wharf) outlast the speculators.

N003 [SHOULD KNOW] The 1980s real estate speculation was financed through S&Ls at taxpayer expense. Salomon Brothers estimated 12 years average before empty commercial real estate absorbed — 26 years in Boston, 40-46 in New York, 56 in San Antonio.

N004 [MUST KNOW] The speculative collapse deepened the 1990 recession: public confidence shaken, corporate investment curtailed, banks forced to restrict lending, workers discharged. The boom and collapse ended George H.W. Bush's presidency — without it, his reelection was "certain, a cinch."

N005 [NICE TO KNOW] Historical parallel: John Law presided over the great French boom and went into exile. Some South Sea Bubble officials suffered similarly. Bush, like Reagan, would have a civilized retirement. "Much, much more remains the same."

### CHAPTER 1: THE SPECULATIVE EPISODE

N006 [MUST KNOW] The speculative episode is driven by self-reinforcing momentum: price increases attract new buyers, whose purchases cause further increases. "The speculation building on itself provides its own momentum."

N007 [MUST KNOW] Two groups of speculative participants: (1) True believers — persuaded some new price-enhancing circumstance is permanent, expect prices to go up indefinitely. (2) Riders — perceive the speculative mood, believe their genius will let them get out before the fall. Both groups are "programmed for sudden efforts at escape" when the reversal comes.

N008 [MUST KNOW] The crash always comes as a bang, never a whimper: "the speculative episode always ends not with a whimper but with a bang." Both groups rush for the exits simultaneously — true believers see their illusion destroyed, riders decide it's time to get out.

N009 [MUST KNOW] The euphoric episode is protected by two forces: (1) the powerful personal financial interest of those getting rich (who want to justify their fortune as deserved), and (2) the pressure of public opinion that condemns doubters. "The euphoric episode is protected and sustained by the will of those who are involved, in order to justify the circumstances that are making them rich."

N010 [MUST KNOW] Vested interest in error: "Speculation buys up, in a very practical way, the intelligence of those involved." No one wants to believe their enrichment is fortuitous — all attribute it to their own superior insight.

N011 [MUST KNOW] Those who express doubt are savagely attacked. Paul M. Warburg warned of "unrestrained speculation" in winter 1929 — reaction was "bitter, even vicious," with anti-Semitic overtones. Roger Babson predicted a 60-80 point Dow crash in September 1929 — Barron's dismissed him, Hornblower and Weeks refused to be "stampeded," even Irving Fisher spoke against him.

N012 [SHOULD KNOW] Galbraith's personal experience: testified before Senate Banking Committee in 1955 about speculative buildup. Received death threats, prayers for his demise. When he broke his leg skiing, speculators wrote saying "their prayers had been answered."

N013 [SHOULD KNOW] In 1986, Galbraith wrote for the New York Times about the speculative buildup. The Times editors rejected it as "too alarming." The Atlantic published it. Before the October 1987 crash, response was "sparse and unfavorable" — "Galbraith doesn't like to see people making money." After October 19, "almost everyone I met told me that he had read and admired the article."

N014 [NICE TO KNOW] Walter Bagehot, early editor of The Economist, observed: "all people are most credulous when they are most happy."

### CHAPTER 2: THE COMMON DENOMINATORS

N015 [MUST KNOW] Factor 1 — Extreme brevity of financial memory: "There can be few fields of human endeavor in which history counts for so little as in the world of finance." Financial disaster is quickly forgotten. When similar circumstances recur, they are hailed as "a brilliantly innovative discovery" by a new, self-confident generation. Past experience is "dismissed as the primitive refuge of those who do not have the insight to appreciate the incredible wonders of the present."

N016 [MUST KNOW] Factor 2 — The specious association of money and intelligence: The more money someone has, the deeper the presumed intelligence. "Money is the measure of capitalist achievement. The more money, the greater the achievement and the intelligence that supports it." This is reinforced by the air of self-confidence of the affluent, who tell ordinary people "I'm afraid that you simply don't understand financial matters."

N017 [MUST KNOW] This association extends to financial institutions: "The larger the capital assets and income flow controlled, the deeper the presumed financial, economic, and social perception." Leaders of great financial institutions are often there precisely because they were the most predictable and bureaucratically inoffensive — yet they are endowed with authority that "encourages acquiescence from their subordinates and applause from their acolytes."

N018 [MUST KNOW] Lenders' deference to borrowers reinforces delusion: banks traditionally treat borrowers with deference ("I must be wise"), which "transmutes by the recipient into an assurance of personal mental superiority." Self-scrutiny — the greatest support to minimal good sense — is at risk.

N019 [SHOULD KNOW] 1970s example: greatest New York banks, praising their own success in "recycling" Arab oil revenues, made durably unfortunate loans to Latin America, Africa, and Poland. "Intellectually questionable men in intimate and protected association with large assets" fed money through Penn Square Bank in Oklahoma City to outstretched hands in the oil patch.

N020 [MUST KNOW] The rule reiterated: "financial genius is before the fall." Only after collapse does the truth emerge — what was thought to be unusual acuity turns out to be "only a fortuitous and unfortunate association with the assets." The result: opprobrium, disgrace, exile, suicide, or "moderately uncomfortable confinement."

N021 [MUST KNOW] Common feature — Something seemingly new: every speculative episode involves the belief that something is genuinely new. Tulips in the 17th century, the joint-stock company, Reagan-era deregulation, junk bonds. Each is celebrated as an innovation when it's actually "a small variation on an established design" owing its character to "the aforementioned brevity of the financial memory."

N022 [MUST KNOW] The core financial "innovation" is always leverage: "All financial innovation involves, in one form or another, the creation of debt secured in greater or lesser adequacy by real assets." Banks discovered they could issue notes in excess of hard-money deposits. "All crises have involved debt that, in one fashion or another, has become dangerously out of scale in relation to the underlying means of payment."

N023 [SHOULD KNOW] 1920s holding companies: owners issued bonds and preferred stock to buy other stocks. As the latter appreciated, all gains accrued to owners. "This was proclaimed one of the financial miracles of that age. It was, in fact, leverage in, at most, a slightly different guise."

N024 [SHOULD KNOW] Junk bonds in the 1980s: Michael Milken of Drexel Burnham Lambert earned $550 million in 1987 and was hailed as an Edisonian innovator. But "the discovery that high-risk bonds leveraged on limited assets should have a higher interest rate hardly stands on a par as an invention with the electric light. Again the wheel, here in an especially fragile version."

N025 [MUST KNOW] After the crash — anger, recrimination, scapegoating: previously admired financiers are now vilified. There will be scrutiny of financial instruments — "paper money; implausible securities issues; insider trading; market rigging; more recently, program and index trading." But what will NOT be discussed is "the speculation itself or the aberrant optimism that lay behind it."

N026 [MUST KNOW] Two reasons the speculative mood escapes blame after crashes: (1) Too many people were involved — attributing error to an entire community challenges the presumption that intelligence is associated with money. (2) Free-enterprise theology holds markets are inherently perfect and rational — so the cause must be found in something external to the market itself (budget deficits, program trading, etc.).

N027 [SHOULD KNOW] 1987 crash aftermath: intense speculation in months before the crash was undeniable, yet "little or no importance was attributed to this speculation. Instead, the deficit in the federal budget became the decisive factor." Studies by NYSE, SEC, and a presidential commission "all passed over or minimized the speculation as a conditioning cause."

### CHAPTER 3: THE CLASSIC CASES, I — TULIPOMANIA; JOHN LAW AND THE BANQUE ROYALE

N028 [MUST KNOW] The Tulipomania (1630s Holland): First great speculative explosion in history. Tulips arrived in Western Europe from Constantinople (cargo in Antwerp, 1562). The tulip became an object of enormous prestige, then speculation. By mid-1630s, price increases seemed without limit. "No person of minimal sensitivity of mind felt that he could be left behind."

N029 [SHOULD KNOW] Price specifics: by 1636, a single bulb of no previously apparent worth could be exchanged for "a new carriage, two grey horses and a complete harness." A Semper Augustus bulb was worth 3,000 florins ($25,000-$50,000 in modern terms). Bulbs changed hands "several times at steadily increasing and wonderfully rewarding prices while still unseen in the ground."

N030 [NICE TO KNOW] The sailor's onion: A sailor who brought news of goods from the Levant was rewarded with a fine red herring breakfast. The merchant, involved in tulip speculation, found a Semper Augustus bulb worth 3,000 florins missing. The sailor was found "contentedly finishing the onion, as he had supposed it to be, along with the fish."

N031 [SHOULD KNOW] Mackay's account of the mania: "Nobles, citizens, farmers, mechanics, seamen, footmen, maid-servants, even chimney-sweeps and old clotheswomen, dabbled in tulips. People of all grades converted their property into cash, and invested it in flowers. Houses and lands were offered for sale at ruinously low prices."

N032 [MUST KNOW] The 1637 collapse: "The wise and the nervous began to detach, no one knows for what reason; others saw them go; the rush to sell became a panic; the prices dropped as if over a precipice." Buyers who had pledged property for credit were "suddenly bereft or bankrupt." Courts refused to enforce contracts, calling it gambling. The state — as with failing banks later — emerged as recourse of last resort.

N033 [SHOULD KNOW] Aftermath: collapse had "a chilling effect on Dutch economic life" — an appreciable depression followed. One mitigating result: tulip cultivation continued and wide markets for flowers and bulbs eventually developed.

N034 [MUST KNOW] John Law and the Banque Royale (1716-1720): Law was born 1671 in Edinburgh into finance. Killed a man in a duel in 1694, escaped prison, lived as a gambler on the Continent. Observed Amsterdam's Bank and conceived of a land bank issuing notes secured by real estate.

N035 [SHOULD KNOW] Law's opportunity: Louis XIV had died in 1715, leaving the Regent Philippe II, Duc d'Orléans — "a man who combined a negligible intellect with deeply committed self-indulgence" — and a bankrupt treasury from the Sun King's wars and extravagance.

N036 [MUST KNOW] The scheme: On May 2, 1716, Law established the Banque Royale (capital of 6 million livres) with authorization to issue notes to pay government expenses and absorb government debts. Then organized the Mississippi Company (Compagnie d'Occident) to pursue gold deposits "presumed to exist" in Louisiana. "There was no evidence of the gold, but this, as ever in such episodes, was no time for doubters or doubting."

N037 [SHOULD KNOW] The frenzy: The old bourse in the Rue Quincampoix saw "the most intense, even riotous, operations in all the history of financial greed." Women offered themselves for the right to buy shares. (Galbraith notes the 1980s parallel: Drexel Burnham Lambert's "Predators' Balls" at the Beverly Hills Hotel.)

N038 [MUST KNOW] The circularity of leverage: Proceeds from Mississippi Company stock sales went not to search for gold but to pay government debts. The notes that paid the debt came back to buy more stock, which lifted both old and new issues higher. "All the notes in this highly literal circulation were, it was presumed, backed by coin in the Banque Royale, but the amount of the coin that so sustained the notes was soon minuscule in relation to the volume of paper."

N039 [MUST KNOW] The 1720 collapse: Precipitating factor was the Prince de Conti, annoyed at his inability to buy stock, sending his notes to the Banque Royale to be exchanged for gold. A run developed — "people seeking to convert their notes not into the stock of the Mississippi Company but into gold." On one July day in 1720, 15 people lost their lives in the crush in front of the Banque Royale. Notes declared no longer convertible; values collapsed. Citizens who had been millionaires "were now impoverished."

N040 [SHOULD KNOW] Mendicant theater: To restore confidence, a battalion of Paris mendicants was recruited, equipped with shovels, and marched through Paris "as though on the way to mine the metal in Louisiana." Weeks later, many were seen back at their old haunts.

N041 [MUST KNOW] Law's fate exemplifies the pattern: When associated with the Banque Royale's wealth, Law was genius — "intelligence, as ever, derived from association with money." When the wealth dissolved, he was "a fugitive mercilessly reviled." He eventually died in Venice, "in decent poverty, a quiet and virtuous life."

N042 [SHOULD KNOW] French aftermath: economy depressed, "a tiny minority enriched by the total ruin of all the rest of the people" (Saint-Simon). Blame fell on Law and his Banque Royale, not on the speculation and its participants. French banks would be regarded with suspicion for a century.

### CHAPTER 4: THE CLASSIC CASES, II — THE BUBBLE (SOUTH SEA)

N043 [SHOULD KNOW] The South Sea Bubble (1720) paralleled the Mississippi scheme but "lacked the French style." It was an ordinary boom and collapse augmented by "a comprehensive exercise in official bribery, corruption, and chicane."

N044 [MUST KNOW] The "discovery" was the joint-stock company — already a hundred years old but suddenly "the new wonder of finance." Absurd early-18th century stock promotions included: a company to build a typewriter, a machine gun firing round and square bullets depending on whether the enemy was Christian or Turk, a mechanical piano, and the immortal "an undertaking of great advantage, but nobody to know what it is."

N045 [SHOULD KNOW] Isaac Newton's involvement: "I can measure the motions of bodies, but I cannot measure human folly." He lost £20,000 (now a million+ dollars) in the speculative orgy.

N046 [MUST KNOW] South Sea Company (founded 1711): Inspired by Robert Harley, Earl of Oxford, and John Blunt, a scrivener. Like the Banque Royale/Mississippi Company, it offered to absorb government debt (from the War of the Spanish Succession) in exchange for 6% interest and monopoly trading rights to South America. Spain claimed monopoly over this trade — Spain allowed exactly one voyage per year, subject to a share of its profits.

N047 [SHOULD KNOW] Stock price trajectory: from ~£128 in January 1720 to £330 in March, £550 in May, £890 in June, ~£1,000 in summer. Directors facilitated this by issuing stock to key ministers and generously awarding stock to themselves.

N048 [SHOULD KNOW] The Bubble Act (July 1720): government passed legislation prohibiting other stock promotions — less to protect the innocent than to "secure the speculative monopoly of the South Sea Company itself."

N049 [MUST KNOW] The collapse: by September stock was down to £175, by December £124. Heroic efforts to revive confidence failed — even an appeal to the newly formed Bank of England. "Once the crash comes, it overrides all efforts to reverse the disaster."

N050 [SHOULD KNOW] Scapegoat hunt was "fierce, even brutal." Blunt narrowly escaped assassination. He saved himself by turning in fellow conspirators. Directors expelled from Parliament, money and estates confiscated. Robert Knight (treasurer) fled and lived in exile 21 years. James Craggs committed suicide. Richard Whitney went to jail. "As in the aftermath of the tulips and of John Law, the economic life of the City of London and that of the country as a whole were notably depressed."

N051 [MUST KNOW] Mackay's crucial observation about the aftermath: "Nobody seemed to imagine that the nation itself was as culpable as the South-Sea company. Nobody blamed the credulity and avarice of the people — the degrading lust of gain...or the infatuation which had made the multitude run their heads with such frantic eagerness into the net held out for them by scheming projectors. These things were never mentioned."

### CHAPTER 5: THE AMERICAN TRADITION

N052 [SHOULD KNOW] Lasting institutional damage from classic cases: John Law created suspicion of banks in France for a century. The Bubble Act restricted limited companies (corporations) in Britain for decades. But by 1824, memory had faded enough for new London stock promotions, including a company to drain the Red Sea to recover "the treasure abandoned by the Egyptians after the crossing of the Jews."

N053 [MUST KNOW] American speculative tradition rooted in "a special American commitment to the seeming magic of money creation." Colonial paper money was the first expression — Southern colonies issued notes against tobacco, Massachusetts issued paper notes after Sir William Phips failed to capture Quebec (1690), needing to pay returning troops with no hard money.

N054 [SHOULD KNOW] Paper money worked for two decades in Massachusetts, then spread to other colonies. Rhode Island was an extreme case — notes became worthless. But Pennsylvania, New York, New Jersey, Delaware, and Maryland exercised restraint. Benjamin Franklin supported paper money (influenced by his business as a printer of notes). Parliament forbade colonial paper issues in 1751.

N055 [SHOULD KNOW] Continental currency financed the Revolution — soldiers paid in Continentals that rapidly lost buying power (shoes at $5,000 a pair, full outfit of clothing over $1,000,000). "Thus was American independence purchased, and it is not clear that it could have been bought in any other way."

N056 [MUST KNOW] Post-Revolution pattern of boom and bust through banks and leverage: First Bank of the United States (1791) created to enforce discipline — required banks to pay notes in specie. Popular in conservative Northeast, hated in South and West. Charter not renewed in 1810. War of 1812 fueled next boom — state banks chartered with abandon: "every location large enough to have a church, a tavern, or a blacksmith shop was deemed a suitable place for setting up a bank."

N057 [SHOULD KNOW] The 1819 collapse: Second Bank chartered 1816 to restore order. But it too "involved itself enthusiastically in real estate loans." Boom collapsed in 1819 — first American panic. The word "panic" entered the financial language. Later, milder terms emerged: "crisis, depression, recession, and now, of course, growth adjustment."

N058 [SHOULD KNOW] Andrew Jackson rallied opinion against the Second Bank (aided by Nicholas Biddle's arrogant enforcement). Charter not renewed. Nearly a century passed before Americans accepted another central bank.

N059 [MUST KNOW] 1837 crash: speculative bubble in real estate, manufacturing, public lands. Financed by proliferating banks and notes, plus British investment in canals and turnpikes. Aftermath introduced debt repudiation — Mississippi, Louisiana, Maryland, Pennsylvania, Indiana, Michigan all repudiated their debts to foreign creditors. "Only the pathological weakness of the financial memory...allows us to believe that the modern experience of Third World debt...is in any way a new phenomenon."

N060 [SHOULD KNOW] Michigan banks' reserve fraud: banks cooperated in shared reserves — gold coins transferred from one institution to the next ahead of the examiner. "The top layer of gold coins in the container was given a more impressive height by a larger layer of ten-penny nails below." A New England bank closed with $500,000 in notes and $86.48 in specie.

N061 [SHOULD KNOW] Post-Civil War boom and 1873 panic: railroads were the object of speculation. Jay Cooke & Company failed in September 1873. NYSE closed for 10 days. Banks suspended payment in coin. Aftermath spawned the Greenback Party and free coinage of silver movement — William Jennings Bryan and "the cross of gold."

N062 [SHOULD KNOW] Joseph Schumpeter normalized boom and bust as the "business cycle" — mania was a detail in a larger process, depression was the benign purge restoring sanity. University courses accepted the alternation as routine.

N063 [SHOULD KNOW] 1907 Wall Street panic: memorable for J.P. Morgan calling on public and private funds to rescue the Trust Company of America and asking New York clergymen to preach sermons of encouragement. "A crash can come to an end without divine intervention."

N064 [SHOULD KNOW] Post-WWI farm land boom: left farmers with debt that in the Great Depression exceeded the aggregate value of all rural property. Born here were the agricultural crisis of the 1930s and the farm programs that still exist.

### CHAPTER 6: 1929

N065 [MUST KNOW] 1929 displayed all the basic features of financial euphoria: presumed financial innovation (leverage rediscovered), parade of publicly celebrated genius, optimism building on optimism, and "the eventual discovery of the severe mental and moral deficiencies of those once thought endowed with genius."

N066 [SHOULD KNOW] Political context: Calvin Coolidge and Treasury Secretary Andrew Mellon created a mood of Republican-era optimism — repeated with Reagan 60 years later. "Most of those who manage investment operations or who have sizable amounts of money to invest are, indeed, Republican in their politics."

N067 [MUST KNOW] Florida real estate boom (1924-1926) was the first manifestation. Lots purchased with 10% cash down payment — leverage. Prices doubled in weeks. Charles Ponzi developed a subdivision "near Jacksonville" — approximately 65 miles away. Choice "beachfront" lots could be 10-15 miles from water. Collapse came in 1926 when new buyers dried up. Blamed on two Caribbean hurricanes, not the speculation. Miami bank clearings: $1,066,528,000 in 1925 → $143,364,000 in 1928.

N068 [MUST KNOW] Stock market boom 1924-1929: prices rising since 1924, took "clear leave of reality in 1928 and particularly in 1929." Leverage was the marvel — stocks purchased on 10% margin, 90% from the broker. Interest rates of 7-15%.

N069 [MUST KNOW] Charles Mitchell vs. Federal Reserve: Spring 1929, Fed announced possible interest rate tightening. Mitchell, head of National City Bank, countered: "We feel that we have an obligation which is paramount to any Federal Reserve warning, or anything else, to avert any dangerous crisis in the money market." His bank would lend to offset any Fed restraint. Market took off again — in three summer months, outpaced the entire previous year's gains.

N070 [MUST KNOW] The leveraged investment trusts — the era's great "innovation": United Founders Corporation: rescued with $500, borrowed and sold securities for eventual total of ~$1 billion. Goldman Sachs Trading Corporation (launched late 1928): first offering $100 million in stock, which then launched Shenandoah Corporation, which launched Blue Ridge Corporation. Leveraged gains flowed upward to the Trading Corporation. Goldman Sachs shares: issued at $104, rose to $222.50, fell to $1.75 by spring 1932.

N071 [MUST KNOW] The celebrated men of 1929: Arthur W. Cutten (Canadian), Bernard E. "Sell 'Em Ben" Smith, M.J. Meehan, Charles Mitchell (National City), Albert H. Wiggin (Chase), Ivar Kreuger (Swedish match king), Richard Whitney (NYSE vice-president, soon president). "Supporting them and sustaining public confidence was a convocation of economics professors."

N072 [MUST KNOW] Irving Fisher's fatal pronouncement: "the most innovative economist of his time," heavily invested in the market himself, declared in autumn 1929: "stock prices have reached what looks like a permanently high plateau." Joseph Stagg Lawrence of Princeton: "The consensus of judgment of the millions whose valuations function on that admirable market, the Stock Exchange, is that stocks are not at present overvalued."

N073 [MUST KNOW] The crash sequence: October 21 opened badly. Thursday (Oct 24) was the first day of disaster — prices dropped seemingly without limit, ticker ran well behind. At noon Thursday, great bankers met at House of Morgan — Thomas Lamont, Mitchell, Wiggin — and resolved to act. Richard Whitney appeared on the Exchange floor making stabilizing purchases. Confidence evaporated over the weekend. Tuesday October 29 was "the most devastating day in the history of the Exchange."

N074 [SHOULD KNOW] Aftermath — scapegoats punished: Mitchell and Wiggin "peremptorily sacked." Mitchell spent a decade in court defending against tax evasion charges (unloaded depreciated stock on his wife for a capital-loss deduction). Wiggin was short his own bank's stock — denied his pension. Cutten suffered "acute amnesia." Meehan "absent-mindedly went abroad." Richard Whitney went to Sing Sing for embezzlement. Ivar Kreuger shot himself in Paris (had counterfeited Italian government bonds). Irving Fisher lost millions, rescued by Yale. Kennedy and Baruch got out early and won lasting esteem.

N075 [MUST KNOW] Post-crash evasion: "The market in October 1929 was said only to be reflecting external influences" — a weakening in industrial production discovered belatedly. Not the speculation but external factors were blamed. Some useful steps taken: SEC created, restraints on holding-company pyramiding, margin requirements. "But, as ever, the attention was on the instruments of speculation. Nothing was said or done or, in fact, could be done about the decisive factor — the tendency to speculation itself."

N076 [SHOULD KNOW] Therapeutic effect of 1929: the crash lingered "somewhat exceptionally" in the financial memory. For the next quarter century, securities markets were "generally orderly and dull." But this was not permanent.

### CHAPTER 7: OCTOBER REDUX (1987)

N077 [MUST KNOW] The 20-year financial memory cycle: "for practical purposes, the financial memory should be assumed to last, at a maximum, no more than 20 years. This is normally the time generally required for a new generation to enter the scene, impressed, as had been its predecessors, with its own innovative genius."

N078 [SHOULD KNOW] 1929's memory lasted longer than 20 years — along with John Law and the South Sea Bubble, it "did remain more vividly in the financial as well as the general public memory." Result: a somewhat longer period of "doubt, caution, and comparative sanity."

N079 [SHOULD KNOW] Mid-1950s: Americans began to regard the stock market without the post-1929 misgiving. Modest booms in 1954-1955. The 1960s: "Go-Go boys" believed by others to have "a new and highly innovative approach to investment opportunities." Mild setbacks in 1962 and 1969.

N080 [MUST KNOW] Investors Overseas Services (IOS) — Bernard Cornfeld and Edward Cowett: a vast pyramid of mutual funds investing in mutual funds, with a six-story-high sales pyramid where salesmen recruited salesmen for commissions. "Only a fraction of the original investment found its way into the securities it was meant to buy. The rest went into all those commissions." IOS was forbidden by the SEC to sell in the US, extruded from Brazil, had problems with the Swiss. Cornfeld's compelling appeal: "Do you sincerely want to be rich?"

N081 [SHOULD KNOW] IOS attracted eminent board members: James Roosevelt (FDR's son), Sir Eric Wyndham White (GATT secretary-general), a former German vice-chancellor. Galbraith was invited to join the board but declined. In 1969, IOS collapsed; Cornfeld yielded remaining assets to Robert Vesco (fugitive financier, eventually in Cuba). Cornfeld detained in Swiss jail but not charged with criminal activity.

N082 [SHOULD KNOW] 1960s-70s REITs and the recycling of OPEC petrodollars: Citibank, Manufacturers Hanover, Continental Illinois sent oil deposits to Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, Poland in the form of loans. "This recycling, as it was called, was considered innovative and imaginative at the time as well as an operation at the highest level of financial respectability." Legacy: default or oppressive debt burden for recipient countries.

N083 [MUST KNOW] 1980s — the Age of Reagan: All speculative elements in place, as the Age of Coolidge had set the stage for 1929. Leverage returned as corporate takeovers and leveraged buyouts. The "new" financial instrument: junk bonds (high-risk bonds carrying high interest rates). "Their novelty, as noted, resided only in their deeply valid name — junk bonds."

N084 [SHOULD KNOW] Young Wall Street men with abbreviated memory. Dennis Levine and Ivan Boesky — the most famed — were on their way to "disgrace and prison" even before the crash. Milken and Drexel Burnham Lambert encountered difficult days after. Drexel went bankrupt. Fortune: "Did Drexel do itself in? Or was it done in? The truth is that this was a case of suicide — and murder."

N085 [MUST KNOW] October 19, 1987 crash: Galbraith had predicted it in the Atlantic in early 1987 and spoke of "a day of reckoning...when the market goes down seemingly without limit." He predicted it would be less devastating than 1929 because of welfare systems, deposit insurance, trade unions, and Keynesian government commitment to sustaining economic activity.

N086 [MUST KNOW] Post-1987 evasion was "especially notable": former Treasury Secretaries, corporate executives sponsored a NYT advertisement attributing the crash to the budget deficit (which had persisted unchanged for six years of Reagan). The Brady Commission report affirmed a bull market existed but emphasized "superbly subordinate factors — program trading, portfolio insurance, largely unspecified specialist misbehavior." The SEC report: devoted entirely to market performance and "strategies" during the crash, "no mention of the circumstances that induced it."

N087 [SHOULD KNOW] Japanese stock crash (March 1990): Tokyo market went down nearly 25%. "A leading investment house concerned with Japan reported that there was talk of changing the accounting rules, so that an institution that loses money in stocks can keep that fact confidential."

N088 [SHOULD KNOW] Robert Campeau: Canadian real estate operator who acquired Bloomingdale's and other great retail houses, loading them with crushing debt. Fortune: "THE BIGGEST LOONIEST DEAL EVER. HOW THE WACKY ROBERT CAMPEAU AND HIS FEE-HUNGRY BANKERS CONCOCTED A HUGE TAKEOVER THAT PROMPTLY WENT BUST." The bankers who financed Campeau and Trump escaped most criticism — "Almost no mention has been made of the deeply defective judgment of the banks that financed these men."

### CHAPTER 8: REPRISE

N089 [MUST KNOW] The unchanging pattern since 1636: (1) Individuals and institutions captured by "the wondrous satisfaction from accruing wealth." (2) The illusion of insight protected by the public impression that intelligence marches with money. (3) Out of that belief comes the bidding up of values. (4) The upward movement confirms personal and group wisdom. (5) Then comes "the moment of mass disillusion and the crash. This last...never comes gently. It is always accompanied by a desperate and largely unsuccessful effort to get out."

N090 [MUST KNOW] Why it is always misunderstood: (1) Those involved never wish to attribute stupidity to themselves. (2) Markets are theologically sacrosanct — "it can be subject to monopoly, imperfect competition, or errors of information, but apart from these, it is intrinsically perfect." (3) Therefore, external causes must be found — budget deficits, market mechanisms, etc. "There is nothing in economic life so willfully misunderstood as the great speculative episode."

N091 [MUST KNOW] Galbraith's pessimism about regulation: "Regulation outlawing financial incredulity or mass euphoria is not a practical possibility." Laws against foolishness would be "impressive, perhaps oppressive, and certainly ineffective." The only remedy is "an enhanced skepticism that would resolutely associate too evident optimism with probable foolishness."

N092 [MUST KNOW] The two rules for individual investors: (1) "There is the possibility, even the likelihood, of self-approving and extravagantly error-prone behavior on the part of those closely associated with money." (2) "When a mood of excitement pervades a market or surrounds an investment prospect, when there is a claim of unique opportunity based on special foresight, all sensible people should circle the wagons; it is the time for caution."

N093 [MUST KNOW] Certainty of recurrence: "There will be another of these episodes and yet more beyond. Fools, as it has long been said, are indeed separated, soon or eventually, from their money. So, alas, are those who, responding to a general mood of optimism, are captured by a sense of their own financial acumen. Thus it has been for centuries; thus in the long future it will also be."
