SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate)

If you've been following commercial real estate news or anything related to floating-rate debt, you've probably heard the term SOFR dropped without much explanation. Let's fix that. SOFR, which stands for the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, is a benchmark interest rate published daily by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. It measures the cost of borrowing cash overnight using U.S. Treasury securities as collateral. Think of it as the pulse of the short-term lending market — a daily snapshot of what it actually costs major financial institutions to fund themselves for one night, backed by the safest collateral on earth. It replaced a prior benchmark called LIBOR, which was phased out after a series of manipulation scandals. SOFR is now the dominant reference rate underpinning trillions of dollars in loans and financial contracts.

SOFR matters because it is the invisible dial that sets the cost of enormous amounts of debt across the U.S. economy. When lenders write floating-rate loans — meaning loans whose interest payments adjust over time rather than staying fixed — they need a reference point for "what does money cost right now?" SOFR is that reference point. A loan might be priced at SOFR plus 2.5%, for example. If SOFR moves up, the borrower's monthly payment moves up automatically. This affects commercial real estate borrowers, corporate debt issuers, student loan products, adjustable-rate mortgages, and derivatives contracts. The reach is enormous. When SOFR rises by even 50 basis points — half a percentage point — it can add hundreds of thousands of dollars annually to debt service on a large commercial property loan, flipping a marginally solvent deal into a cash-flow crisis overnight.

Mechanically, SOFR is calculated from actual transaction data in the Treasury repo market. Here's how that works: every day, large institutions — banks, money market funds, broker-dealers — lend cash to each other overnight, and the borrower pledges U.S. Treasury bonds as collateral. These transactions are called repurchase agreements, or repos. The Fed collects data on hundreds of billions of dollars worth of these trades every single day, then calculates the volume-weighted median interest rate across all of them. That number gets published the following morning. Because it's based on real, executed transactions rather than survey estimates (which is how LIBOR worked), SOFR is considered far more credible and manipulation-resistant. It reflects actual market supply and demand for overnight cash, not what a banker guessed it would be.

The connection to the Federal Reserve's policy rate is tight and direct. The federal funds rate — the rate the Fed sets at its FOMC meetings — is essentially a target range for overnight lending between banks. SOFR trades in the same overnight, collateralized space and almost always moves in lock-step with that target. When the Fed hiked rates aggressively in 2022 and 2023, SOFR climbed from near zero to above 5.3% within roughly 18 months. That transmission was nearly instantaneous. As the video explains:

"By the way, that's called SOFR. All these things are based on SOFR, which is almost directly tied to the Fed funds rate."

This near-perfect correlation is not accidental — it's structural. Both rates live in the same overnight secured lending ecosystem, and Fed policy directly controls the level of reserves in that system, which sets the floor on what overnight money costs.

To see why this matters in the real world, consider the Sunbelt apartment syndication story the video describes. A syndicator buys a 200-unit apartment complex in Phoenix in 2021 using a bridge loan — a short-term floating-rate loan, priced at SOFR plus a spread. At origination, SOFR is near zero, so the all-in rate might be around 3.5%. The deal underwrites to that payment level. The syndicator also buys an interest rate cap — a derivative instrument that limits how high SOFR can go before a counterparty steps in to cover the excess. With rates at zero, that cap is relatively cheap to buy. Fast forward to 2026: SOFR is above 5.3%, the interest rate cap has expired (they typically last 2–3 years), and the borrower is now facing a rate north of 8%. The monthly debt service on a $30 million loan might jump from $87,500 to $200,000. If the rent roll can't cover that, the math breaks — regardless of occupancy.

Traders and investors use SOFR in several practical ways. First, SOFR futures and Overnight Index Swap (OIS) contracts trade actively and are considered the cleanest real-time market signal for where the Fed funds rate is headed. If you want to know what the bond market is pricing for the September Fed meeting, you look at SOFR futures. They'll tell you, with a specific probability, whether traders think rates go up, down, or stay flat. Second, anyone holding or analyzing floating-rate loans — whether in a CMBS portfolio, a direct real estate investment, or a leveraged buyout model — must track SOFR constantly because it directly determines cash flow. Third, fixed income analysts use the SOFR term rates (1-month, 3-month, 6-month forward-looking rates published by CME) to model interest expense for future periods in deal underwriting.

One of the most common misconceptions about SOFR is that it moves slowly or predictably, giving borrowers time to react. That is wrong. SOFR can move the day after a Fed decision — meaning a rate hike on a Wednesday becomes higher debt service on Thursday. There is no lag for unhedged floating-rate borrowers. A second misconception is that SOFR and the federal funds rate are the same thing. They are not identical — SOFR is a market-determined rate that reflects actual repo transactions, while the fed funds rate is a target set by policy — but they are closely tethered. Occasionally they diverge slightly due to quarter-end balance sheet pressures or collateral scarcity, but in normal conditions the spread between them is minimal. A third misconception: people confuse SOFR with the prime rate, which is a different benchmark used for consumer credit products and moves at a slower, more lagged pace.

When the borrowing environment deteriorates sharply, SOFR can exacerbate stress in ways that create feedback loops. If a wave of commercial real estate defaults forces regional banks to recognize losses, those banks may curtail lending and tighten their own balance sheets — reducing repo market participation, which can create temporary upward pressure on SOFR independent of Fed policy. More broadly, rising SOFR doesn't just hurt existing floating-rate borrowers — it also raises the hurdle rate for any new deal. When the risk-free overnight rate is above 5%, a commercial real estate deal has to return significantly more than 5% just to justify the risk premium. This compresses valuations across the entire sector. The video's central thesis — that $1.5 trillion in commercial real estate debt is hitting a wall — is ultimately a SOFR story: debt written assuming cheap overnight money now repricing into an environment where SOFR stays elevated at precisely the moment interest rate caps expire and maturity extensions run out.

SOFR connects directly to several other concepts worth understanding in tandem. Interest rate caps are derivatives priced off SOFR — their cost rises as rate volatility increases, which is why caps bought in 2020 were cheap and caps bought today are expensive. CMBS (Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities) pools frequently contain floating-rate loans indexed to SOFR, making the delinquency rates in those pools a downstream consequence of where SOFR trades. Duration risk in fixed income is in many ways the mirror image of SOFR risk: fixed-rate bondholders worry about rising rates hurting their bond prices, while floating-rate borrowers worry about rising SOFR increasing their cash costs. Understanding SOFR is foundational to understanding why the extend-and-pretend strategy described in the video only works when the central bank is cutting — because every extension buys time, but SOFR determines how expensive that time actually is.
