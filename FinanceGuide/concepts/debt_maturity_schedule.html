Debt Maturity Schedule (Maturity Wall)

A debt maturity schedule is a table that lists when a company's borrowings come due. Every bond, term loan, and note has a maturity date, the day the borrower must repay the principal in full. Add up everything due in each calendar year and you get the schedule: $50 million in 2026, $400 million in 2027, $0 in 2028, $900 million in 2029, and so on. Public companies publish it in the notes to their financial statements in the annual 10-K, usually under a heading like "Debt maturities by year." A maturity wall is what you get when a large share of the debt is bunched into one short window. The schedule is a calendar of the company's obligations to its lenders.

It matters because debt is a promise with a date on it, and the date does not move when conditions get worse. A company can skip no bond payment without defaulting, so how much comes due, and when, determines how much pressure management faces. Cash used to repay or refinance debt cannot go to dividends, buybacks, hiring, or new factories. When a wall approaches, the board has to choose among those uses. That is why the schedule affects workers, shareholders, and lenders alike. It is one of the earliest places to see stress, because the repayment dates are written down years in advance, long before earnings show any damage.

Mechanically, most corporate bonds are bullet maturities: the company pays interest (the coupon) every six months and repays the entire principal on one day. Companies rarely have that much spare cash, so they refinance, meaning they issue a new bond and use the proceeds to retire the old one. The process runs in steps. Treasurers start preparing a year or more ahead, the bank's debt capital markets desk prices the new issue off the current Treasury yield plus a credit spread that reflects the company's riskiness, and investors buy or don't. The old bond's low coupon vanishes and the new one's rate applies. Refinancing therefore works only if lenders are willing to lend, and at what price.

The video's example is the "expired teaser rate." Companies issued 3- and 5-year bonds in 2020 and 2021 at 2% to 2.5% and sometimes 3%. The speaker claims over $1.8 trillion of such paper comes due between late 2026 and the end of 2027, and says refinancing yields now sit around 7.5% to 9%. The arithmetic is simple. A company with $500 million of debt at 2.5% pays $12.5 million a year; at 8.5% it pays $42.5 million, a $30 million increase. That extra interest comes out of operating cash flow. The credit-card analogy fits: the principal is unchanged, but the cost of carrying it triples.

"Instead of rolling over a $100 million note at 2.5%, the refinancing yield today sits between 7.5 and 9%."

Investors use the schedule in a specific way, and the video's playbook step is a good template: open the 10-K for each of your top dividend holdings, find the debt maturities table, and look at the next two or three years. Then compare those amounts with what the company can actually pay from. The key comparisons are maturities against cash on hand, against undrawn credit lines, and against annual free cash flow. A company with $300 million due next year, $1 billion in cash, and strong cash flow is fine. A company with $300 million due, $40 million in cash, and thin cash flow is dependent on capital markets staying open. Also look at interest coverage (operating earnings divided by interest expense), because it shows how much room there is for a higher rate. The video suggests 3-to-1 as a floor.

There are important nuances. First, a big number in the table is not automatically a threat. Large, healthy companies routinely carry a rolling schedule and refinance on time without drama; a $2 billion maturity is trivial for a firm earning $5 billion a year. What matters is the amount relative to resources. Second, the headline "$1.8 trillion" is an aggregate across very different borrowers. Investment-grade giants can refinance easily; the stress concentrates in lower-rated, highly leveraged firms. Third, the effect is gradual, not a single day. Debt rolls a little at a time, so the average interest rate creeps upward as old low-coupon bonds are replaced. Fourth, the table usually shows the face amount only, and it may leave out leases, pensions, or off-balance-sheet items. It also shows only contractual dates, so check footnotes for early repayment triggers.

Treat the specific figures in the video with care. The speaker says Treasury yields are around 5.34% for the 10-year and that refinancing costs 7.5% to 9%; those are claims about the market at the time of recording, not universal facts, and actual rates depend on each company's rating. The $1.8 trillion total and the claim that over 22% of the Russell 2000 are "zombie" companies are also presented without sourcing. The 22% figure is plausible as a rough estimate of unprofitable or low-coverage small caps, but definitions vary. It is wise to verify such numbers against Federal Reserve data, rating-agency research, or S&P and Moody's maturity tallies, and, above all, against the filings of the companies you actually own. The mechanism is real even if the dramatic framing is not.

When a company cannot refinance, there is a sequence of outcomes, ordered roughly from mild to severe. It may first use cash reserves or draw on its revolving credit facility, a bank line it can borrow against. It may sell assets, cut the dividend, freeze hiring, or slash capital spending. It may do an amend-and-extend, persuading lenders to push the maturity date out in return for a higher rate or tighter terms. It may offer a distressed exchange, swapping old bonds for new ones worth less. At the far end is default and Chapter 11 bankruptcy, where creditors are paid ahead of shareholders, and stock is often wiped out. The ranking of claims is why equity holders are exposed: bondholders are paid first, so shareholders absorb the pain before lenders do.

"Bondholders must be paid before shareholders receive a single dime."

The schedule connects to many other concepts. Interest coverage and debt-to-EBITDA measure whether the company can afford the new, higher interest bill. Free cash flow and the payout ratio tell you whether the dividend survives it. Covenants in loan agreements can trigger default earlier than the maturity date if earnings fall below set thresholds. Credit spreads and the Treasury yield curve set the price of refinancing, while refinancing risk is the formal name for the danger that a company cannot roll its debt. Zombie companies, firms whose earnings barely cover interest, are those most dependent on cheap, available credit. Also note that some firms had already termed out debt (extended maturities) when rates were low, which makes them much safer.

To discuss this confidently, remember the core logic: the maturity schedule tells you when a company must find new money, and the credit market tells you what that money will cost. The risk is highest when a large maturity coincides with weak cash flow, high leverage, low cash, and expensive or closed credit markets. The practical routine is short. Read the 10-K table, compare the next two years' maturities with cash, credit lines, and free cash flow, check interest coverage at a higher assumed rate, and see whether management has already refinanced early. Be skeptical of any single scary total, and look at each company's own numbers. A high dividend yield is only reliable if the debt calendar leaves room to pay it.
