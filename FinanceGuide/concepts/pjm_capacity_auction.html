Wholesale Capacity Auctions (PJM)

A wholesale capacity auction is a market where electricity suppliers get paid not for power they actually produce, but for promising to be available when the grid needs them. PJM Interconnection is the nonprofit regional grid operator that runs the largest of these markets. It coordinates the high-voltage grid across 13 states and Washington, D.C., serving more than 65 million people, from Illinois and Ohio through Pennsylvania, Virginia, and New Jersey. PJM holds an auction, called the Reliability Pricing Model (RPM), to buy "capacity" years ahead of time. Capacity means a commitment: a power plant, battery, or demand-response program agrees it will be ready to deliver in a future delivery year. Think of it as paying for a fire department's readiness, not just the fires it fights.

This matters because capacity is a real slice of what you pay for electricity, and it is set in a market, not by a regulator's formula. If you live in PJM territory in a state with retail choice, the price from the auction flows through to your supply charge, sometimes with a lag of a year or more. The auction also steers where billions of dollars of investment go. High prices signal that new plants are needed. Low prices can push old plants into retirement. So the same number affects household budgets, utility earnings, power-plant profits, and whether the lights stay on during a heat wave or a polar vortex.

Here is the mechanism, step by step. First, PJM forecasts its peak demand for a future year, then adds a reserve margin (extra supply beyond the forecast peak) to produce a reliability requirement. Second, it draws a demand curve, a line showing how much it would pay at different quantities of capacity. Third, generators submit offers stating the minimum price at which they will commit each megawatt. Fourth, PJM stacks the offers from cheapest to most expensive until the requirement is met. The last offer needed sets the clearing price, quoted in dollars per megawatt-day, and every cleared resource gets that same price. Finally, PJM charges that cost to load-serving entities, the utilities and retail suppliers who sell power to customers. They then build it into your rates.

The video says capacity prices exploded by about 800%, and the broad outline is real, though the details need care. PJM's auction for the 2024/25 delivery year cleared at about $28.92 per megawatt-day. The 2025/26 auction cleared at about $269.92, nearly a tenfold jump. The 2026/27 auction hit the cap of roughly $329.17. Later auctions also cleared at or near a cap that regulators imposed after consumer complaints. Utility bills in parts of Maryland, Ohio, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania rose noticeably on those dates. The presenter's headline of a 40% bill jump is a hard thing to attribute to capacity alone, though, because delivery charges, fuel costs, and rate cases also moved.

"Regional capacity auctions recently exploded by over, get this, 800%. Why? Because hyperscalers are purchasing upfront forward power commitments at any cost to ensure computing servers never power down."

That quote contains a claim worth slowing down on: that hyperscalers are buying capacity in the auction. Mostly, they are not. Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, and Meta do not bid in PJM's capacity auction. The mechanics run the other way. Their data centers are load, and PJM's demand forecasts rose sharply because of them, especially in northern Virginia, one of the densest data-center clusters on Earth. A higher forecast means a higher reliability requirement, so PJM needs to buy more capacity. Supply was shrinking at the same time as coal and gas plants retired, and new projects waited in long interconnection queues. More demand chasing less supply raised the clearing price. Hyperscalers influence prices through their consumption forecasts, not through bidding.

Traders and investors read these auctions as a scarcity signal. Independent power producers that own plants in PJM, such as Vistra, Constellation, and Talen, see their earnings estimates move when results come out, because a higher capacity price lifts revenue on plants they already own. Utilities that are only wires companies are mostly pass-through, so the effect on them is different. Analysts also watch the reserve margin that PJM reports. A margin falling below target means tighter supply and more upside for prices in future auctions. Retail energy suppliers, who sell fixed-price contracts, watch their margins, since a capacity price they did not hedge can turn a profitable contract into a loss.

There are several gotchas. Capacity is only one component of your bill. A typical household bill splits into supply (energy plus capacity), transmission, and distribution, and capacity is usually a modest share of the supply portion, so a tenfold capacity price rise does not mean a tenfold bill rise. Second, the pass-through depends on where you live. In a state with regulated default service, the price is often averaged over several auctions, so the shock is smoothed. Third, the video blends separate things together. Capacity prices, the rate-base transmission build-out, and delivery riders are three different mechanisms. The video is right that they can all hit one bill, but each has its own cause and its own fix.

Auctions can also fail in ways that matter. Clearing prices depend on rules, and rules change. PJM's market is run under price floors and caps, which regulators negotiated with governors after the spike. A cap protects consumers in the short run, but it can undersupply the signal that tells builders to invest, so reliability risk can come back later. Another risk is that capacity cleared on paper may not show up. In Winter Storm Elliott in December 2022, a large share of PJM's forced outages came from gas and other plants that had been counted on. PJM has since changed how it rates each resource's expected availability, which lowers the credit given to units that may fail in cold snaps.

This concept connects directly to other ideas in the video. Cost-of-service ratemaking covers the regulated side, where utilities earn a return on infrastructure they build. The capacity auction covers the market side, where prices are set by supply and demand. They overlap in PJM because some states have regulated utilities that own their wires but buy power from the market. Both transmit data-center demand growth into household bills, by different routes. If you want to act on it, look at your own statement, find out whether your supplier is on a fixed or variable plan, and check whether your state's default service is cheaper. That is a more reliable defense than any trade.
