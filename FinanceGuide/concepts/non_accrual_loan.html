Non-Accrual Loan

A non-accrual loan is a loan on which the lender has stopped recording interest income because the borrower has stopped making timely interest payments — or the lender believes the borrower is unlikely to continue paying. The word "accrual" comes from standard accounting practice: normally, a lender books interest income as it accumulates day by day, whether or not the cash has actually arrived. When a loan goes non-accrual, that accounting stops. The lender is essentially saying: "We no longer trust that we'll receive this interest, so we won't pretend it's revenue." It's a formal acknowledgment that something has gone wrong with the borrower's ability or willingness to service the debt, even before any official default declaration has occurred.

Non-accrual status matters enormously because it is one of the earliest and most honest stress signals in a loan portfolio. Banks and credit funds report non-accrual rates as a percentage of total loan cost — and when those rates rise, it signals deteriorating credit quality across their borrower base. Rising non-accruals compress a lender's actual income (since they stop booking that interest), can force them to reserve more capital against potential losses, and often precede a wave of formal defaults. For investors in bond funds, business development companies (BDCs), or private credit vehicles, a rising non-accrual rate is a flashing yellow light: the portfolio is under stress even if the headline default numbers still look clean.

Here is how the mechanics work step by step. A borrower takes out a loan and agrees to pay interest monthly. As each day passes, the lender's accounting system credits the interest that has "accrued" — earned but not yet received. When the borrower misses a payment, or when the lender judges that future payments are in serious doubt, the loan is moved to non-accrual status. At that moment, any previously accrued but uncollected interest is reversed from income. Going forward, no new interest is recorded as revenue. If the borrower actually does send a cash payment later, it gets applied first to reduce the outstanding principal balance — not booked as income — because the lender can no longer assume the full debt will be repaid.

The distinction between non-accrual and default is critical and widely misunderstood. A borrower can be on non-accrual without having technically defaulted on their loan agreement. Loan contracts often include grace periods, covenant waivers, and amendment processes that allow a borrower to stay technically current on paper while their economic situation deteriorates badly. This is exactly the dynamic described in the video: the Bank for International Settlements data showed that less than 1% of the software borrowers tracked were actually behind on a contracted payment, yet lenders were aggressively placing loans on non-accrual and marking down values. Non-accrual is the lender's internal judgment call; default is a legal trigger defined in the loan document. The two can diverge significantly, especially in private credit where lenders have wide discretion.

The Reuters audit cited in the video provides a concrete, real-world illustration of why this metric matters at scale. Across 44 American BDCs — the core vehicles behind the $1.4 trillion private credit boom — non-accrual loans jumped from 2.5% of cost to 3.4% in a single reporting period. A parallel audit of 35 rated private credit funds showed median non-accrual rates surging from 1.81% in Q1 to 2.75% by June 30. These numbers don't sound enormous in isolation, but consider the base: against a $95 billion loan book, moving from 2.5% to 3.4% represents roughly $855 million in additional loans where interest income has evaporated. Multiply that across the broader private credit universe and you are talking about billions in lost revenue that funds were previously counting on to pay investor dividends.

"Non-accrual loans — these are where borrowers have flat out stopped making timely interest payments — jumped from 2.5% of cost to 3.4%. Another audit of 35 rated private credit funds revealed that the exact same trend. Median non-accrual rates surged from 1.81% in Q1 to 2.75% by June 30th. Everyone's finding these numbers and they're all agreeing."

Sophisticated investors and institutional risk desks use non-accrual rates as a leading indicator — a signal that arrives before the formal default statistics do. When you see non-accrual rates climbing while official default rates stay low, that gap tells you something important: the stress is real and building, but it hasn't yet tripped the contractual wire. Analysts track the ratio of non-accruals to total portfolio cost over time, watching for acceleration. They also look at which sectors are driving the increase. In the video's context, the concentration of non-accruals in software and technology lending told risk desks that the AI disruption of per-seat subscription pricing was not a theoretical threat — it was already eating into actual cash flows at the borrower level.

One major nuance investors frequently miss is how non-accrual interacts with payment-in-kind (PIK) loans. PIK arrangements let a borrower skip cash interest payments by rolling the unpaid interest into the loan balance — the debt grows instead of being serviced. Crucially, a PIK loan may not appear on non-accrual, because technically the lender is receiving "payment" in the form of added principal. The fund can still book the accrued interest as income on paper. This creates a troubling illusion: a fund's non-accrual rate can look stable while its PIK percentage climbs, masking the fact that an increasing share of borrowers have no real cash to pay interest. The video notes that PIK usage surged from roughly 6% to over 10% of private credit loans between early 2022 and early 2026 — a 66% jump that runs parallel to, and partially obscures, the true stress picture.

When non-accrual rates rise sharply across a fund, the downstream consequences compound quickly. First, the fund's net investment income (NII) — the actual cash available to pay dividends — falls, because interest income that was modeled into the payout schedule simply isn't arriving. Dividend cuts follow. Second, lenders typically must increase loan loss reserves, further depressing reported earnings. Third, if a fund is paying out dividends partly funded by PIK-booked phantom income rather than real cash, investors who don't scrutinize the schedule of investments may not realize their "income" is partly an accounting construction. The video's warning about funds "manufacturing yield by borrowing against the future" is precisely this dynamic: rising non-accruals combined with heavy PIK usage can make a 10% dividend yield look solid on the surface while the underlying cash economics are deteriorating beneath it.

Non-accrual loans connect directly to several other critical fixed-income concepts. They are closely related to mark-to-market valuation: when a loan goes non-accrual, the lender often simultaneously marks down the loan's fair value on the balance sheet, since the present value of expected future cash flows has declined. They also feed into credit loss provisioning and allowance for loan and lease losses (ALLL) on bank balance sheets. For BDC investors specifically, tracking the relationship between non-accrual rates, PIK percentages, and net asset value (NAV) per share over successive quarters gives you a composite picture of portfolio health that no single metric provides alone. The video frames non-accruals and mark-downs as "twin stress indicators" — and that pairing is exactly right. Together they tell you not just that borrowers are struggling today, but that the collateral backstopping those loans is worth less than the original underwriting assumed.
