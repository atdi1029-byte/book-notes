Overnight Reverse Repo Facility (ON RRP)

The Overnight Reverse Repo Facility, or ON RRP, is a Federal Reserve tool that lets money market funds, banks, and other eligible institutions park cash overnight at the Fed in exchange for Treasury securities, with a promise the Fed buys them back the next day plus a small amount of interest. Think of it as a savings account the Fed offers to institutional cash managers who have nowhere better to put their money. It sits on the liability side of the Fed's balance sheet, and the New York Fed's open market desk runs it as a floor for short-term interest rates. When cash has no attractive private-market home, it flows into the RRP instead of sitting idle or chasing yield in riskier corners of the financial system.

This facility matters because it acts as a buffer between the government's borrowing needs and the banking system's reserve levels. When the Treasury General Account (TGA) — the government's checking account at the Fed — needs to be refilled through bond and bill issuance, that new debt has to be purchased by someone. If money market funds buy those bills using cash they would have otherwise stashed in the RRP, then commercial bank reserves are left untouched. But if the RRP is empty, that same purchase has to be funded by pulling cash directly out of the banking system, draining the reserves banks rely on to meet regulatory liquidity requirements and to keep credit flowing to households and businesses.

Mechanically, here is the chain: the Treasury sells a bill, an investor pays for it, and that payment lands in the TGA at the Fed, removing it from private circulation. If a money market fund is the buyer and it was previously holding cash in the RRP, it simply withdraws that cash from the RRP window and hands it to the Treasury — the bank reserves outside the RRP are never touched. The RRP shrinks, the TGA grows, and total bank reserves stay flat. But once the RRP balance hits zero, or close to it, that escape valve is gone. Now, when the same bill is purchased, the cash has to come from somewhere else — and that somewhere else is bank reserve balances sitting at the Fed, which then fall dollar-for-dollar with every dollar added to the TGA.

The video's example lays out the timeline vividly: from 2022 through 2023, the RRP held over $2 trillion in idle money-market cash, acting as what the narrator calls a "shock absorber." During that period, the Treasury issued enormous quantities of short-dated bills to rebuild its cash balance after the debt-ceiling standoffs, and it did so without meaningfully disturbing commercial bank reserves, because money funds simply drained their RRP holdings to buy the new paper. By the week referenced in the video, that same RRP balance had collapsed to roughly $4 billion — a more than 99% decline — while the TGA had climbed past $1 trillion and bank reserves were hovering near the closely watched $3 trillion level.

Traders and desk analysts watch the RRP balance as a leading indicator of how much slack is left in the financial plumbing. A high RRP balance signals there is a cushion of parked cash that can absorb Treasury issuance without stressing bank reserves. A falling RRP balance is a warning sign — it tells you that future TGA buildup will start extracting liquidity directly from the banking system rather than from idle money-fund cash. Alongside the RRP, desks track the weekly H.4.1 release, the daily Treasury statement, and the SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate) for signs of repo market stress. As the video puts it, once the RRP sponge is dry,

every single dollar Bessant pulls into the TGA now extracts liquidity one for one directly out of commercial bank settlement reserves.

A common misconception is that the RRP is simply "money the Fed borrowed" in some abstract sense, or that its balance reflects the health of the broader economy. In reality, it is best understood as an overflow reservoir for cash that has no better short-term use — its size rises and falls based on relative yields, bill supply, and how much collateral is available elsewhere, not based on economic growth or recession risk directly. Another nuance: the RRP declining isn't inherently bad — it can reflect money funds finding better-yielding alternatives, like newly issued bills themselves. The danger only emerges when the RRP is nearly empty at the same time the Treasury is aggressively rebuilding its cash balance, because that combination removes the one mechanism that used to insulate bank reserves from TGA swings.

When the RRP effectively runs dry, as described in the video, the risk is that continued Treasury cash accumulation collides directly with bank reserves, pushing them toward levels regulators and banks consider uncomfortably tight relative to Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) requirements. Banks respond by hoarding what reserves they have — cutting unused credit lines, tightening underwriting, and raising the spread they charge over base rates, because every dollar of contingent credit exposure (like an unused credit card limit) is a liability they might have to fund with reserves they no longer have in abundance. In the most severe historical precedent, September 2019, a similar reserve-scarcity dynamic caused overnight repo rates to spike violently, forcing the Fed to intervene with emergency liquidity operations.

The RRP is best understood as one leg of a three-way relationship with the Treasury General Account and total bank reserves — together these three balances make up the liability side of the Fed's balance sheet alongside currency in circulation, and they must move in ways that sum to a roughly constant total. It also connects directly to quantitative tightening (QT): as the Fed lets its own securities holdings run off, it is separately draining reserves from the system, compounding whatever pressure the TGA and a depleted RRP are already creating. Understanding the RRP means understanding that reserve scarcity isn't caused by one single force — it is the net result of Treasury cash management, Fed balance sheet policy, and the shrinking cushion that once separated the two.
