Long-Term Power Purchase Agreements / Contracted Capacity

A Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) is a long-term contract, often running 10 to 20 years, in which a company agrees to buy a fixed amount of electricity from a power generator at a set price, or agrees to reserve capacity at a data center site that requires that much power to run. Contracted capacity is the total amount of power a company has legally locked in through these deals, whether or not it is actually using all of it yet. For an AI infrastructure company like CoreWeave, this means signing agreements years in advance to guarantee electricity supply for data centers that may not even be built yet, betting that by the time the power comes online, there will be enough AI demand to fill it.

This matters because electricity, unlike almost every other input in the AI buildout, cannot easily be resold, repurposed, or shipped elsewhere. A chip that goes unused can sit in a warehouse or be sold to another buyer. A rack of GPUs can be redeployed to a different customer or a different workload. But a 20-year contract to buy power tied to a specific electrical grid in a specific county is a fixed, location-locked obligation. If the AI demand that was supposed to consume that power never materializes, the company is still on the hook to pay for it, with no natural buyer waiting to take the obligation off its hands. This is why contracted power capacity has quietly become one of the most important hidden risk indicators in the entire AI infrastructure trade.

Mechanically, the process works like this: a company like CoreWeave identifies a site, often in a rural or secondary market where land and power are cheap and available, and signs a long-term agreement with a utility or power producer to reserve a specific amount of megawatts. This contract usually predates the actual data center construction and often predates having a confirmed customer for the compute that facility will eventually provide. The company is essentially pre-ordering electricity on a bet that demand will catch up. Analysts then classify these power markets by tier, roughly reflecting how easy it would be to find an alternative buyer if the original plan falls through, with tier 1 and tier 2 markets being major metro areas with deep, liquid demand, and tier 3 and tier 4 markets being smaller, rural regions where alternative demand is thin to nonexistent.

The real-world number that drives this entire chapter comes from a Bernstein research note referenced in the video: analysts estimated that about 25% of CoreWeave's existing active U.S. power sits in tier 3 and tier 4 markets, but roughly 74% of its contracted (not yet active) power sits in those same smaller, rural markets. That gap between 25% and 74% is the entire story. It means the power CoreWeave is currently using is reasonably diversified into higher-demand areas, but the power it has already promised to pay for in the future is heavily concentrated in places where, if AI demand from that specific site doesn't show up, there is essentially no one else who wants it. As the video put it, this is "electricity that somebody has already contracted for in places that nobody else wants it against demand that has not been sold yet."

Traders and analysts use contracted capacity data as a forward-looking stress test for infrastructure-heavy AI companies. Rather than only looking at reported revenue or chip orders, which get most of the media attention, sophisticated investors dig into power purchase disclosures, site lists, and utility filings to map out where a company's obligations sit geographically and how exposed those locations are to a demand shortfall. A company with contracted capacity concentrated in dense, high-demand corridors like Northern Virginia is in a fundamentally different risk position than one with contracts scattered across rural tier 4 counties, even if the total megawatt numbers look similar on a spreadsheet. This is precisely the kind of detail that doesn't show up in headline earnings numbers but shows up first when a slowdown actually hits.

The biggest misconception here is treating power contracts the same way people treat chip orders, assuming that if demand doesn't materialize, the company can simply cancel, resell, or redeploy the commitment elsewhere with modest losses. That logic works reasonably well for hardware, which is why the market barely blinked when chip-related warnings came out repeatedly over the prior year. It does not work for power. These agreements are typically structured with minimum payment obligations, sometimes called take-or-pay provisions, meaning the buyer must pay for the reserved capacity whether or not it actually draws that electricity. There is no secondary market where another company swoops in to buy a stranded 20-year rural power contract the way there is for a warehouse full of GPUs.

When this kind of arrangement breaks, the failure mode is a slow, structural cash drain rather than a sudden crisis. A company keeps paying for power capacity it isn't using, watching margins erode quarter after quarter while revenue growth that was supposed to justify the buildout fails to arrive on schedule. Because these contracts often sit behind complex financing structures, including debt raised specifically against expected data center revenue, a demand shortfall in a tier 4 market can cascade into credit concerns, refinancing pressure, or asset write-downs long before it shows up as an obvious headline. This is exactly why the video frames it as the "real exposure" in the AI slowdown story: it's the kind of risk that sits quietly on a balance sheet for months before anyone outside a specialist research desk notices it.

This concept connects directly to broader ideas in capital-intensive infrastructure investing, including asset-liability duration mismatches, where the timing of committed costs and the timing of expected revenue drift apart, and stranded asset risk, a concept long familiar from energy and real estate investing. It's also the infrastructure-world cousin of overcapacity risk that shows up in any buildout cycle, from telecom fiber in the late 1990s to shipping capacity before a trade slowdown. The lesson generalizes well beyond CoreWeave: whenever a company locks in a long-duration, illiquid, location-specific commitment ahead of confirmed demand, the quality of the underlying demand forecast, not the technology itself, becomes the single largest determinant of whether the investment pays off or becomes a liability.

In practice: CoreWeave's power contracts show what happens when committed costs and realized revenue drift apart. Bernstein's screen found roughly 25% of CoreWeave's existing active US power sits in tier three and tier four markets, but 74% of its contracted-but-not-yet-active power sits there — rural sites nobody else wants. A 20-year power contract has no resale market the way a chip does; if AI demand paces slower than Anthropic's Dario Amodei suggested in his "We Must Pace the Frontier" essay, that capacity becomes an obligation with no buyer on the other end, well before the underlying technology itself ever becomes obsolete ("THE AI SLOWDOWN SHOCK: Why Tech Giants Just Agreed to Hit the Brakes!").
