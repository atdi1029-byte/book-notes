Months' Supply of Housing Inventory

Months' supply of housing inventory is a simple ratio that tells you how long it would take to sell every home currently listed for sale at the current pace of sales, if not a single new listing came onto the market. You calculate it by dividing the number of unsold homes sitting on the market by the average number of homes sold per month. If there are 96,000 unsold new homes and builders are selling 10,000 a month, that's 9.6 months of supply. It's a clock, not a headcount — it converts a raw inventory number into a measure of time, which is what actually matters to a buyer, seller, or builder trying to gauge how tight or loose a market is.

This number matters because it's the single cleanest gauge of supply-and-demand balance in housing, and housing is one of the largest drivers of household wealth, construction employment, and consumer spending in the entire economy. When months' supply is low, homes move fast, sellers hold pricing power, and bidding wars are common. When it's high, homes sit, sellers start cutting prices, and builders start offering incentives just to keep cash flowing. Economists, the Federal Reserve, and Wall Street analysts all watch this metric because it leads price changes — inventory builds up months before prices actually soften, giving you an early warning system rather than a lagging one.

Mechanically, there are two inputs, and both matter independently. The numerator is the stock of unsold homes on the market at a given moment, which grows when sellers list faster than buyers absorb and shrinks when the reverse happens. The denominator is the trailing sales pace, typically expressed as a seasonally adjusted annual rate that gets converted back to a monthly figure. A rising months'-supply number can come from either side of the equation: inventory piling up because listings outpace buyers, or sales collapsing while inventory stays flat. Both point to the same conclusion — weakening demand relative to what's available — but they tell you different stories about whether the problem is too much building or too little buying.

The video lays out a real split that most people miss: new homes carry 9.6 months of supply while existing homes carry only 4.9 months. That's roughly double. New home sales are down 6.3% year-over-year, while existing home sales are down a much milder 1.2%. This directly contradicts the popular narrative that resale is "frozen" while builders are thriving — the data says the opposite. Builders like Lennar are the ones sitting on the glut, which is exactly why Lennar's gross margin collapsed from 29.5% at the pandemic peak to 15.8% last quarter, and why the company is using rate buy-downs and price cuts to move inventory rather than letting homes sit.

"We are compromising margin in order to maintain volume. Of course, we understand that this is a choice. It is deliberate and it is not something the market is doing to us." — Stuart Miller, Lennar executive chairman

Traders and investors use months' supply as a leading indicator across several angles. Homebuilder stock analysts watch the new-home figure specifically, because it's a proxy for future margin pressure — when supply climbs above roughly 6 months, that's historically the threshold where builders start discounting meaningfully rather than just trimming at the edges. Macro investors watch the existing-home figure as a read on the broader consumer and labor market, since resale supply is driven by ordinary households deciding to list, which reflects job security, mortgage lock-in effects, and life events. Fixed-income and mortgage-REIT investors also track it because rising supply eventually feeds into home-price indices, which affect collateral values across the entire mortgage market.

The biggest misconception is treating "months' supply" as one unified housing market number. It isn't — new and existing homes are functionally different products with different sellers, different financing incentives, and different supply dynamics, and the video's entire point is that conflating them gets the story backwards. Another gotcha: the number is a snapshot ratio, so it can spike or drop for reasons that have nothing to do with underlying health — a single large subdivision completing construction can bump new-home supply for a quarter even if demand hasn't changed. And regionally, the number varies enormously; a national 9.6-months figure can hide a market with 3 months of supply sitting next to one with 15, which is why the video explicitly flags that local conditions can look nothing like the headline.

When months' supply breaks from its normal range, it signals real stress. Extremely low supply (under roughly 3-4 months) historically produces sharp price acceleration and bidding-war conditions, like the 2021 environment. Extremely high supply (above 8-9 months, where new homes sit now) is the range where builders historically start slashing prices outright rather than just offering incentives — which is precisely the dynamic the video documents: Lennar's incentive rate actually fell from 14.3% to 12% year-over-year, but the sticker price dropped enough that the total subsidy per house barely changed, just shifted from the mortgage rate to the price tag. The risk in a high-supply environment is that price cuts, unlike temporary rate buy-downs, are permanent and show up in every neighboring appraisal, which can trigger a self-reinforcing cycle of falling comps and further price cuts.

Months' supply connects directly to a builder's mortgage rate buy-down strategy, which is the other half of this video's story. When supply is high and margins are compressed, as with Lennar's drop to 15.8% gross margin, builders can't afford to permanently buy down a mortgage rate — doing so consumes a disproportionate share of already-thin margin, which is why the American Enterprise Institute estimates a full one-point permanent rate reduction costs a builder roughly 3.2% of the sale price. High months' supply is the pressure that forces builders toward cheaper, shorter-term teaser rates and eventually toward outright price cuts instead — which is exactly the "asterisk" mechanism the video spends its first half decoding through the APR disclosure.
