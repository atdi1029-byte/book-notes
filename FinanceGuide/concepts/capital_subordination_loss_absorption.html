Subordinated Debt and Loss-Absorbing Capital

Every company, bank included, funds itself with a stack of claims, and the order of that stack decides who gets hurt first. Senior debt sits at the top and gets paid first. Subordinated debt ranks below it, so if the borrower fails, senior creditors must be repaid in full before subordinated holders see a cent. Below that sits common equity, the owners, who get whatever is left, which is often nothing. Loss-absorbing capital is the name for the layers built to take losses first so that depositors and senior creditors do not. Because they stand closer to the blast, these layers demand higher yields as compensation.

This matters because banks are different from ordinary companies. They borrow short from depositors and lend long, so a modest loss of confidence can become a run. After 2008, governments had spent enormous sums rescuing banks, and voters and regulators wanted a rule that private investors, not taxpayers, would eat the next round of losses. The result was a thick set of mandated cushions. That affects the real economy directly: when the cushion is cheap to raise, banks lend freely, and when it is expensive or unavailable, they tighten credit. The price of the cushion works like a thermostat for how willing banks are to lend to households and businesses.

Here is the mechanism step by step. A bank has assets, such as loans and bonds, funded by liabilities and equity. When loans go bad, the loss is first subtracted from equity. If equity is wiped out, the next layer is hit, then the next, moving up the stack until the loss is absorbed. Regulators set minimum ratios of capital to risk-weighted assets, and the layers are ranked by how readily they absorb losses: common equity first, then Additional Tier 1 (AT1), then Tier 2 subordinated debt, then senior bonds, and finally deposits. A thicker junior layer means a thinner chance that anyone higher up is touched. The cushion works only if it can absorb losses while the bank is still operating, not merely after it dies.

AT1 bonds, also called contingent convertibles (CoCos), are the most engineered of these layers. They pay a fixed coupon, usually have no real maturity date, and the issuer can choose to redeem them on a set "call" date. Their special feature is the trigger: if the bank's capital ratio falls below a stated threshold, or a regulator declares the bank non-viable, the bonds are either converted into shares or written down, sometimes to zero. The issuer can also skip coupons without defaulting. So the investor owns something that looks like a bond in good times and behaves like equity, or worse, in bad times. The video called these the "airbag" of the banking system, sitting between senior bondholders and common equity.

The video gave a live reading of how the market prices that airbag. Global spreads on AT1 bank capital bonds surged to 253 basis points over benchmarks, after collapsing below 200 basis points earlier in the summer. Bank of New York Mellon had to pay 32.4 basis points more to sell preferred shares than it did three months earlier. Orders for Banco BPM's 400 million euro capital deal were cut in half during marketing, forcing the bank to accept concessions. Belfius Bank's brand new hybrid broke to 99 cents on the euro on day one. Each is a small signal, but all point the same way: buyers wanted hazard pay.

"When the airbag in the car gets expensive, my friends, the engine does not run as fast."

Investors and traders watch these markets as a stress gauge. AT1 spreads and new-issue behavior tell you how much risk the market wants to be paid to stand at the front of the line. Signals include the spread level versus its recent range, the "new issue concession" (the extra yield a bank must offer to sell new paper), whether order books are covered several times or shrinking, and whether a deal trades above or below face value on day one. Fund managers quoted in the video said they were refusing to buy junior bank debt at tight spreads because of extension and reset risk. Equity investors use the same data: higher capital costs squeeze net interest margins and can limit buybacks and dividend increases.

Two terms in that quote deserve explanation. Extension risk is the chance that the bank does not call the bond on the expected date, leaving you holding a perpetual-style security for years, often at a coupon below what new issues pay. Reset risk refers to the coupon being reset after the call date to a new rate based on prevailing benchmarks plus a fixed spread, which may be better or worse than you expected. Investors long assumed banks would always call, because failing to call looked like a sign of weakness. That assumption is a convention, not a contract. When market spreads widen, calling and refinancing at a much higher cost may no longer make sense for the bank, and the convention gets tested.

There are common misconceptions. The first is that AT1s are "just high-yield bonds." They are not, because the loss can be imposed without a bankruptcy: the bond can be written down while the bank continues operating. The second is that the capital ranking always applies strictly, with equity wiped before AT1. In Credit Suisse in March 2023, Swiss authorities wrote down roughly 16 billion Swiss francs of AT1 to zero while shareholders still received about 3 billion francs in the UBS takeover. Many investors had assumed the hierarchy would be respected, and they learned the "airbag gets sacrificed" lesson. Contract terms and national law decide, not textbook diagrams. A third misconception is that a high yield means a good deal; for AT1, it is the price of tail risk.

When the system fails, it fails in layers and by contagion. A shock to one bank's AT1, like Credit Suisse, makes buyers doubt the whole asset class, and issuance windows slam shut. Banks that cannot refinance their capital must hoard it, shrink lending, or pay more, which feeds back into the economy. Sovereign stress adds a second channel: banks hold large amounts of their own government's bonds, so a French spread blowout of 32 basis points in a week weakens bank balance sheets, which then widens bank spreads, a "doom loop." Investors respond by selling junior European paper and moving into Treasuries, which is how the video explained the 10-year US yield dropping to 4.96%. A falling yield was a safety bid, not a vote of confidence.

To connect this to other ideas: capital subordination is the same logic as the credit spread, the extra yield over a safe benchmark for taking credit risk. It is linked to the flight to quality that pushes money into US Treasuries, to sovereign spreads like France over Germany, and to bank net interest margins and shareholder payouts. The practical takeaway for conversations about markets is simple: ask where a security sits in the capital stack, what triggers a loss, and who decides. Then watch the price of the cushion. When the cost of the bank's airbag rises, it tells you something about how the market views the car, the road, and the driver.
