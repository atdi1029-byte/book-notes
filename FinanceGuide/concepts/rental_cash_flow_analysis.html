Rental Cash Flow Analysis (Rent vs. Debt Service)

Rental cash flow analysis is the process of figuring out how much money a rental property actually puts in your pocket, or takes out of it, each month. The idea starts simple: compare the rent a tenant pays with the cost of owning the place. But the phrase "rent versus debt service" hides a trap. Debt service is just the mortgage payment, meaning the principal and interest you owe the lender every month. It is only one of several costs. A real analysis subtracts everything it takes to keep the property rented and in good condition, and what remains is net cash flow. If that number is positive, the house pays for itself and then some. If it is negative, you are paying to own it.

This matters most right now because of the situation the video describes: roughly 85 million American homeowners hold a mortgage below 5%, and nearly 48 million hold one below 3.5%. Many of them need or want to move for a job, for family, or for space. Selling means giving up a cheap loan and taking on a 6.85% one. Renting the old house out is the alternative. It lets you keep the cheap debt while you live somewhere else, which turns a "mortgage prison" into a possible strategy. Whether that strategy works comes down to the arithmetic of cash flow, not to how good the idea sounds.

Here is the mechanism, step by step. Start with gross rent, the full amount the lease says the tenant owes each month. Subtract a vacancy reserve, money you set aside because the unit will sit empty between tenants. Subtract escrow items, which means property taxes and homeowners insurance, often collected inside your mortgage payment. Subtract maintenance and repair costs. Subtract management fees if you hire someone to run the property. Then subtract the principal and interest payment itself. What is left is net cash flow. The video's first step is only the top of this ladder: gross rent against the mortgage. That comparison tells you whether the idea is worth examining, not whether it is profitable.

"Can your existing $1,300 debt service convert into a $2,200 gross rental cash flow? ... But factor in vacancy reserves, reinsured property escrows, and uninsured tenant repair conduits."

Now apply this to the video's numbers. Rent of $2,200 against a $1,300 payment looks like $900 a month of profit, or $10,800 a year. Suppose the house sits empty one month in twelve, a cautious 8% vacancy allowance. That costs about $176 a month on average. Property taxes and insurance, if not already in the $1,300, might run $550 combined. Repairs and capital reserves, a common rule of thumb being 1% of the home's value per year, on a $400,000 house would be about $333 a month. Adding those up: $2,200 minus $176, $550, $333, and $1,300 leaves roughly $-159. The "$900 profit" can vanish entirely. Note that the video's $1,300 mortgage appears to be principal and interest only, so check whether escrow is included in your own figure.

Investors use this analysis as a screening tool before they commit, and they usually reduce it to a few ratios. The 1% rule says monthly rent should be at least 1% of the purchase price; $2,200 on a $400,000 home is 0.55%, which is weak by that standard, though the rule matters less when your loan is unusually cheap. Net operating income is rent minus operating costs, before any mortgage. Cash-on-cash return divides yearly net cash flow by the cash actually invested. An owner with a 3% loan is judged against a different baseline than a buyer at 6.85%. The question changes from "is this a good property?" to "is keeping this loan worth more than the cash I'd get by selling?"

The strongest argument for renting out the low-rate house is not the monthly check. It is the opportunity cost of selling. The video shows that replacing a 3% loan on $320,000 with a 6.85% loan raises principal and interest from about $1,349 to about $2,097, a penalty of roughly $748 a month for 30 years. Keeping the old loan avoids that penalty. If you sell, you also pay agent commissions and closing costs, and you may owe tax on gains. A rental with slightly negative cash flow can still be rational if the loan stays cheap, the mortgage balance falls each month, and the house appreciates over time.

Several nuances catch people out. First, principal repayment is not a cost in the economic sense. Part of every payment builds your equity, so a property can show negative cash flow while your net worth still climbs. That is not a license to ignore the cash shortfall, because your bank account must cover it. Second, lenders typically require owner-occupancy for the best rates, so check your loan terms before converting a primary residence; most lenders tolerate a genuine move, but read the rules. Third, rental income is taxable, though depreciation and expenses reduce the bill. Fourth, you become a landlord, with local tenant laws, eviction timelines, and time costs that a spreadsheet does not show.

Cash flow analysis breaks in a few predictable ways. One expensive event, such as a $12,000 roof or a $9,000 HVAC replacement, can wipe out years of thin margins, which is why experienced owners hold a cash reserve of several months of expenses. A bad tenant who stops paying can cost months of lost rent plus legal fees plus damage, and it often takes longer to remove them than you expect. Rising insurance premiums, which the video notes have seen double-digit increases, and property tax reassessments squeeze margins from the cost side. If you also pay rent or a mortgage in your destination city, two housing costs at once can strain your finances. Always test the plan with pessimistic numbers, not average ones.

This concept connects directly to the rest of the video's story. The rate lock-in effect is what makes keeping the old house attractive: the bigger the gap between your current rate and today's, the more valuable the old loan is to hold. It also ties to opportunity cost, because home equity is not liquid cash, and renting converts trapped equity into an income stream without selling. It links to the video's point about low-coupon mortgage-backed securities, where lock-in slows prepayments; every owner who rents instead of selling keeps their loan outstanding. And it echoes a broader lesson: treat a house as an investment and demand that it earn its keep. The final test is simple. After every realistic cost, does the house make you money, break even, or cost you? If you can answer that with numbers, you can decide wisely.
