Commercial Real Estate Property Classification (Class A / B / C)

When real estate professionals, lenders, and investors talk about commercial property, they don't treat every building as interchangeable. The industry uses a tiered grading system — Class A, Class B, and Class C — to describe a property's overall quality, location, age, amenities, and the creditworthiness of its typical tenant base. Class A is the top tier: think gleaming glass towers in prime urban cores, recently built or fully renovated, with institutional-grade tenants paying premium rents and long-term leases. Class B sits in the middle — functional, reasonably maintained, but older or in secondary locations, attracting solid but less prestigious tenants. Class C represents the bottom tier: aging buildings, deferred maintenance, fringe locations, and tenants who rent there primarily because rents are low. This isn't just academic terminology. It determines who owns the property, who financed it, and who absorbs the loss when things go wrong.

The classification matters enormously because it determines which part of the financial system carries the risk. Class A trophy assets — the skyscrapers that appear on magazine covers — are typically owned by large institutional investors, financed by money-center banks or packaged into commercial mortgage-backed securities (CMBS) and distributed across global capital markets. When a Class A building in Manhattan stumbles, the pain is spread widely and absorbed by institutions with deep balance sheets. Class B and C properties operate in an entirely different ecosystem. Suburban office parks, strip malls on secondary roads, aging industrial flex space — these are financed overwhelmingly by regional and community banks, held on balance sheet rather than securitized, and concentrated in institutions whose entire lending book may be tied to a single metro area's economic health. The same dollar of stress hits those lenders far harder.

Understanding the mechanical difference between these classes requires thinking about both the asset and the liability side simultaneously. A Class A building carries high rents, creditworthy tenants with multi-year leases, and typically a conservative loan-to-value ratio set by a sophisticated institutional lender. Its debt is often fixed-rate, long-duration, and widely distributed. A Class B or C property, by contrast, was frequently financed during low-rate environments on shorter-term, floating-rate debt — meaning the interest payment adjusts as benchmark rates like SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate) move. When rates were near zero, this was a manageable or even attractive structure. When the Federal Reserve tightened aggressively, those same loans repriced upward month by month, compressing or eliminating the cash flow that makes a property viable. The class of the building directly predicts which debt structure was used, and therefore which borrowers are now under water.

While the financial press ran headlines about Manhattan trophy towers signing tenants at premium rates — technically true — the real stress was accumulating invisibly in suburban Class B office parks and secondary strip retail centers. These properties don't photograph well. They don't generate the kind of news that drives a YouTube thumbnail. But they generate regulatory filings, and those filings told a different story. As of the most recent data cited, 54.8% of regional banks exceeded the regulatory threshold of commercial real estate loans equal to 300% or more of total risk-based capital — the level at which regulators impose heightened supervision. The median regional bank sat at a 312% concentration ratio. That concentration is almost entirely driven by Class B and C assets on their books, not the trophy assets getting the press coverage.

For investors and traders, the class framework is a signal-sorting tool. When you see a headline about commercial real estate recovering, the first question to ask is: recovering for whom? If the data comes from large CMBS servicers or money-center bank earnings calls, it primarily reflects Class A performance — which was never really the problem. The actionable signal is in the monthly delinquency and special servicing reports published by firms like TREPP and Morningstar DBRS. These track loans that are past due or transferred to a workout specialist, broken down by property type and geography. When CMBS multifamily delinquency rates triple — as they did from under 2% in 2023 to over 6% more recently — that's not a Class A story. That's Sunbelt apartment complexes, built by syndicators on short-term floating-rate bridge loans during the 2020–2022 cheap-money era, now failing to cover debt service. The class framework tells you where to look.

The most common misconception about this classification system is treating it as a fixed, objective label rather than a dynamic, relative one. A building's class can drift over time. A Class B office park that was functional and 85% occupied in 2019 may have slipped toward Class C status by 2025 if anchor tenants downsized post-pandemic, deferred maintenance piled up, and management couldn't fund capital improvements. This class migration is insidious because the loan on that property was underwritten against its original Class B characteristics — its rent rolls, its occupancy, its appraised value — not its deteriorated present state. The lender's collateral quietly eroded while the loan balance stayed fixed. By the time a maturity date forced a reassessment, the gap between the outstanding debt and the property's current value had grown far beyond what a normal refinancing market could bridge.

The system breaks down most visibly when a refinancing crisis and a rate shock arrive simultaneously — precisely the scenario the video describes. The mechanism is called a cash-in refinance requirement: when a property's value falls far enough that the outstanding loan exceeds the lender's maximum permitted loan-to-value (LTV) ratio, the borrower must write a check to reduce the principal balance before a new lender will issue fresh debt. This is not optional and not theoretical — it's the mathematical consequence of standard underwriting. For a leveraged owner of a Class B office park whose equity has already been wiped out by falling rents and rising vacancies, that check may be larger than their available capital. The rational response is to stop making payments and hand the deed back to the bank — a process known as deed-in-lieu of foreclosure. At that moment, the regional bank that made the original loan transforms from a lender into an unwilling property owner, with a non-earning asset sitting on its books consuming capital that could otherwise support new lending.

This connects directly to broader concepts of credit concentration risk, extend-and-pretend, and bank contagion. The extend-and-pretend strategy — where lenders grant 12-to-24-month maturity extensions rather than forcing painful refinancings — was a bet that falling rates would eventually rescue Class B and C borrowers. That bet required the Federal Reserve to cooperate. When the Fed instead signaled a potential rate hike at its September 2026 meeting, with the 10-year Treasury near 4.78% and a blowout jobs report behind it, the escape hatch closed. Interest rate caps — derivative contracts borrowers had purchased to artificially limit their floating-rate exposure near 3.5–4% — were simultaneously expiring, leaving them naked against a benchmark pinned above 5.3%. The intersection of these forces affects the entire regional banking sector at once, not just isolated institutions. With 54% of regional banks over the regulatory concentration threshold, a visible wave of write-downs on Class B and C assets doesn't stay contained. As the video notes, investors don't wait to identify which specific banks are fine — they sell the entire regional banking group first and ask questions later. The class of the building, it turns out, determines the class of the crisis.

"The trophy towers and the money center banks are not where this problem lives. They never were, my friends. The real exposure sits in suburban class B and class C office parks, secondary strip retail, and Sunbelt apartment deals built by syndicators on short-term floating rate debt. And most of that is sitting on the books of small and regional banks, not the institutions getting the headlines."

The practical takeaway is that the Class A / B / C framework is not just a real estate term — it's a map of risk distribution across the financial system. When you hear that commercial real estate is stabilizing, locate the claim on that map before accepting it. Is the speaker describing vacancy rates in trophy towers financed by JPMorgan? Or delinquency rates on 2021-vintage floating-rate loans backing suburban garden apartments in Phoenix? Those are different assets, financed by different institutions, carrying different debt structures, and heading toward different outcomes. Any serious analysis of commercial real estate stress — and by extension, regional bank health, local credit availability, and broader financial stability — has to start by asking which class of property is actually under discussion. The headline and the real story are rarely in the same building.
