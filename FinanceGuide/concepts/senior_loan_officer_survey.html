Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey (SLOOS)

The Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey, usually called the SLOOS (pronounced "sloze"), is a quarterly questionnaire the Federal Reserve sends to senior loan officers at a panel of about 80 large domestic banks and U.S. branches of foreign banks. The questions are simple. Over the past three months, did you make it harder or easier for customers to get a loan? Did customers want more or less borrowing? The survey covers mortgages, credit cards, auto loans, commercial and industrial loans, commercial real estate, and in recent years loans to non-bank financial firms. Nothing in it is a hard financial measurement. It is a poll of the people who say yes or no to credit applications, and it tells you which way the faucet of credit is turning.

It matters because credit is the fuel of the real economy. Companies borrow to build inventory, hire workers, and expand. Households borrow to buy homes and cars. When banks quietly raise their standards, asking for more collateral, higher spreads, bigger down payments, or better credit scores, fewer projects get funded and spending slows. When they loosen, activity accelerates. Because bank lending decisions come before the spending and the defaults they influence, the SLOOS has a good record as a leading indicator. Tightening across many categories has shown up before most recessions, often months before unemployment or earnings data look bad. Economists and Fed officials read it for the direction of credit, not just its level.

Mechanically, the survey works through diffusion-style tallies. The Fed asks each bank whether standards were "tightened considerably," "tightened somewhat," "remained basically unchanged," "eased somewhat," or "eased considerably." It then publishes the net percentage tightening: the share of banks that tightened minus the share that eased. Demand questions work the same way, as net share reporting stronger versus weaker demand. Banks are also asked why, with answers like a less favorable economic outlook, worsening collateral values, reduced risk tolerance, or tougher competition. The Fed releases results about two to four weeks after the quarter's survey window, in February, May, August, and November. Reading the three pieces together, standards, demand, and reasons, tells a richer story than any single number.

The video gives a clean recent example. The Fed's November survey asked about loans to non-bank financial firms, the category that includes private credit funds, business development companies, and securitization vehicles. Of roughly 50 responding banks, 12 tightened standards and only one eased. The banks cited a shakier economic outlook and weaker financial strength at the borrowers themselves. Yet in the same survey, banks reported that demand for these loans rose, with greater need for liquidity among the reasons given. That combination is notable. Borrowers want more money precisely when lenders are getting choosier, which is the kind of mismatch that can turn a quiet pressure into a real squeeze.

"12 out of the 50 banks tightening is a yellow light. If that number climbs, the bridge is actually narrowing."

Why does this particular category matter so much? The video points to Boston Fed research showing that loans to non-bank financial firms grew from about 2% of big-bank loan books in 2010 to roughly 21% now, and to the Fed's report that bank credit commitments to other financial institutions passed $2.8 trillion. Private credit funds do not take deposits, so they lean on bank credit lines when they need cash for redemptions or new loans. If banks tighten on those lines, the funds have less room to maneuver just as investors are queuing at the gate and fundraising has slowed. The SLOOS is therefore one of the few public windows into whether the "shadow bridge" between banks and shadow banks is getting narrower.

Investors and analysts use the SLOOS in a few practical ways. First, as a trend signal: a rising net-tightening reading across commercial and industrial loans has historically lined up with wider corporate credit spreads, rising defaults, and weaker earnings a few quarters later. Second, as a cross-check against market prices. If high-yield spreads look calm while loan officers are tightening, the market may be complacent. Third, as a category screen, since a jump in one segment such as commercial real estate or non-bank lending can point to where stress is building. Many traders watch the direction of change from quarter to quarter and compare it with the long-run average, treating a reading above zero as tightening and a spike toward 30 or 40 as a genuine credit squeeze.

There are important nuances. A reading of 12 of 50 banks is about 24%, which is meaningful but far from the 50% or higher tightening seen in 2008 or early 2020. Nor does "tightened" mean "stopped lending": it can mean slightly wider spreads or a few extra covenants. The survey is also subjective and backward-looking by one quarter, and a single bank's answer carries equal weight whether it is giant or small unless the Fed uses its weighted figures. Demand rising while standards tighten can reflect borrowers scrambling for liquidity, not healthy expansion. And a SLOOS reading is context, not a trigger. Used alone it can give false alarms, since standards sometimes tighten and the economy then rides through.

What happens when the signal fails or misleads? Surveys can lag fast-moving shocks. In March 2020 or in the weeks around the March 2023 regional bank failures, conditions changed faster than a quarterly poll could capture, and the survey caught up afterward. Banks can also keep headline standards steady while quietly adjusting terms that the questionnaire does not measure, such as the advance rate on a credit line. The video's example is a bank that lends 70 cents against each dollar of loans in a fund's pool and trims that to 60 cents. No dramatic "tightening" headline appears, yet the fund must find 10 cents per dollar elsewhere at the worst possible time. Credit can therefore tighten in ways the SLOOS only partly shows.

The SLOOS connects to several other ideas in the video. It is the bank-side counterpart to the Fed's Financial Stability Report, which flagged the $2.8 trillion in commitments and changed its private credit default language from "relatively low" to "the upper end of its short historical distribution." It pairs with Fitch's 6.3% private credit default rate, with the gates on non-traded BDC redemptions, and with the broader theme that risk did not leave the banking system but moved "one floor up." Read together, defaults at record highs, redemption queues, slowing fundraising, and banks tightening are a squeeze from both sides. The next SLOOS, due early next year, is the checkpoint: if more than 12 banks tighten, the bridge is narrowing, and Main Street borrowers feel it first.
