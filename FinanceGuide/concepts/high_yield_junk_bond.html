High-Yield (Junk) Bond

A high-yield bond — universally nicknamed a junk bond — is corporate debt rated below investment grade by the major agencies: below BBB- at S&P and Fitch, or below Baa3 at Moody's. Anything from BB+ on down falls into this bucket. The label sounds pejorative, but it's a precise engineering term describing default risk. An investment-grade issuer like Apple or Microsoft borrows against decades of stable cash flow and gets to price debt at a tight spread over Treasuries — often just 50 to 70 basis points — because lenders have almost no doubt they'll be repaid. A junk issuer is asking lenders to accept meaningfully higher odds of missed payments or restructuring, and in exchange must pay a much fatter coupon. SoftBank's recent $11.15 billion offering, rated BB+ — one single notch below investment grade — is a textbook example sitting right at the top edge of the junk universe.

Why this matters extends far past the company issuing the bond. High-yield debt is the pricing mechanism that sets the marginal cost of risky capital across the entire economy. When junk yields climb, every company that depends on debt markets to fund growth — not just other junk-rated borrowers, but the growth-stock ecosystem broadly — inherits a higher hurdle rate. This is the linkage the video hammers on: an AI infrastructure boom that everyone assumed was funded by mega-cap operating cash flow is, in reality, increasingly financed by exactly this kind of speculative-grade paper. When the printed yield here jumps, it's a signal read by every credit desk and every equity analyst simultaneously, because it reflects a repricing of risk appetite that doesn't stay contained to one balance sheet.

Mechanically, a junk bond deal works like any bond syndication, but with a harder sales job. Underwriters — in SoftBank's case City, Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, and Morgan Stanley — build an order book by canvassing institutional buyers: pension funds, insurance companies, high-yield mutual funds, and credit hedge funds. Because the credit is below investment grade, passive index buyers largely stay away, so the underwriters must offer a concession — a yield sweetener — large enough to pull in real demand. SoftBank split its raise into tranches by maturity (3.5-year, 5.5-year, 7.5-year dollar tranches, plus a euro tranche), letting different buyer bases pick the maturity that fits their mandate. The longest tranche tested in the high-9%-to-10% range, a level that had to be set high enough to actually clear the deal, not just to look generous.

The real-world numbers here tell the story cleanly. SoftBank's existing 2031 bonds were trading at an 8.2% yield just before this deal, up sharply from 6.7% back in January — evidence that the market had already begun repricing SoftBank's credit lower before this new supply even hit. Then $11.15 billion of fresh paper — the largest non-financial corporate bond deal in Asian history — landed on top of that, testing near 10% on the long end. For context, that's roughly double what an investment-grade issuer pays and edges toward the yield level normally associated with distressed or deeply cyclical credits, not a company backing one of the largest AI capital commitments in the world.

Traders and portfolio managers watch junk-bond issuance and spreads as a real-time risk sentiment gauge, similar to how equity traders watch the VIX. The key metric isn't the yield in isolation — it's the spread, the extra yield over a comparable Treasury. When spreads widen, it means the market is charging more for the same default risk, which is a tightening of financial conditions even if the Fed hasn't moved rates at all. A single outsized deal like SoftBank's can move benchmark high-yield indexes because of what the video calls the crowding-out effect: if a credit manager can get 9-10% buying blue-chip-adjacent tech-conglomerate paper, they won't accept 6.5% on a random triple-B industrial bond — so they demand more yield there too, and spreads widen across the whole high-yield and crossover complex, not just on the new issue.

The biggest misconception retail investors carry is that junk-bond exposure only matters if you personally hold individual junk bonds. In reality, most people carry it passively through multi-sector bond funds, "core-plus" fixed income sleeves inside target-date and 401(k) funds, and benchmark-tracking ETFs like HYG and JNK. Those funds are mandated to track credit indexes, so when a deal this size prints, index weights shift and the funds are mechanically forced to absorb a proportional slice of it — whether or not the fund manager independently likes the credit. As the video puts it:

"Your retirement savings are underwriting literally underwriting venture capital level risk, collecting a fixed coupon while carrying the balance sheet exposure of a cash burning unlisted startup."

When junk yields break higher in a disorderly way, the failure mode isn't limited to bond markets — it transmits directly into equity valuations through the discount rate used in every DCF model (see the related concept of Multiple Compression / WACC). A rising cost of capital for risky borrowers pulls up the discount rate applied to future earnings across growth and tech stocks, shrinking the present value of those earnings even if nothing about the business itself has changed. In SoftBank's specific case, the failure mode is sharper still: it pledged 769 million shares of ARM Holdings — 72% of the company — as collateral on a separate $25 billion margin facility. If rising junk yields compress multiples on chip stocks, ARM's share price falls, margin covenants tighten, and SoftBank faces a collateral squeeze at the exact moment it owes fixed coupons on freshly issued 10% debt — a direct chain from one bond deal's pricing to a margin call on unrelated equity collateral.
