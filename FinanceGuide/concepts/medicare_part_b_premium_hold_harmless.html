Medicare Part B Premium and Hold Harmless Rule

Medicare Part B is the part of Medicare that pays for outpatient care: doctor visits, lab work, preventive screenings, durable medical equipment, and many outpatient procedures. Unlike Part A, which most people get without paying a monthly premium, Part B costs money every month. That charge is the Part B premium. The video puts the standard premium at about $22.90 a month for the year it covers, and the article's central point is that the premium is usually deducted straight from your Social Security check before the money reaches your bank account. The hold harmless rule is a protection layered on top. It says that for people whose premium is taken from Social Security, the Part B increase cannot be larger than the dollar amount of their cost-of-living adjustment, so their net check cannot shrink.

This matters because retirement income planning usually starts with a headline number, and the headline number is not the number you spend. Seventy-one and a half million Americans receive Social Security, and for most of them the monthly benefit is the foundation of their income. When the Social Security Administration announces a cost-of-living adjustment (COLA), the figure that gets reported is the gross increase. Medicare premiums are one of the biggest things that quietly reduce it. For a household living on a fixed budget, a premium that rises faster than its benefit means real spending power falls even in a year the government calls a raise. Health costs also tend to rise faster than general prices, so this pressure builds year after year. Understanding the premium and its one shield is part of understanding what a retirement paycheck is actually worth.

Here is the mechanics, step by step. First, each fall, Medicare's trustees and then the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) set the standard Part B premium and the annual deductible, the amount you pay out of pocket for covered services before Medicare begins sharing costs. The official numbers usually arrive around November. Second, if you collect Social Security, Medicare subtracts the premium from your benefit before it is deposited. Third, the SSA calculates your new benefit by applying the COLA, which takes effect in January. Fourth, the hold harmless test compares two dollar figures: how much your premium increased and how much your benefit increased. If the premium rise would exceed the COLA dollars, your premium is reduced so that your net deposit stays the same. The comparison is in dollars, not percentages, which is why the size of the COLA matters so much.

Now apply the video's numbers. The standard premium is about $22.90 a month this year, after a jump of $17.90 from the prior year. The Medicare trustees project it will rise to about $29.50 next year. That projection is not final, but if it holds, the increase is $6.60 a month, or roughly $79 over twelve months. The Part B deductible is projected to rise from $283 to $292. Meanwhile, the video says a typical 3.5% COLA on an average retired-worker benefit of roughly $2,0-something a month adds about $73 a month. Compare $73 of new benefit to $6.60 of new premium: the premium increase is far smaller than the raise, so the hold harmless rule never activates. The retiree's $73 becomes roughly $66 after Medicare, which is still a real gain but smaller than the headline.

"It only kicks in when the COLA is tiny. With a $73 raise and a $6.60 premium increase, it doesn't touch most people."

That quote captures the most common misunderstanding. People hear "your check can never shrink" and assume they are broadly protected from Medicare cost increases. In fact the guarantee is narrow. It is a floor on your net check from one year to the next, not a cap on what you pay for Medicare. It only binds when the premium increase would be larger than the COLA increase in dollars, which happens in years when the COLA is very small or zero, or when the premium jumps sharply. In a year with a $73 raise, the rule sits idle and you absorb the whole $6.60 increase. The rule also does nothing about the deductible rising, about Part D drug plan premiums, or about out-of-pocket costs for care. It protects one number, your net Social Security deposit, in one specific kind of year.

There are also whole groups the rule does not cover. The video says about 70% of enrollees are eligible, which means roughly three in ten are not. Two exclusions deserve attention. First, high earners who pay the income-related monthly adjustment amount (IRMAA), a surcharge on Part B and Part D premiums, are not protected. The video says IRMAA begins above about $109,000 of income for a single person and $218,000 for a couple, and that crossing it can lift the monthly Part B premium from $22.90 to roughly $284.10 plus a drug-plan surcharge. Second, new enrollees are excluded, because they have no prior-year benefit and premium to compare against. People who pay their premium directly rather than through Social Security, for example those who have not yet started benefits, also fall outside the protection.

For investors and retirees, the practical use of this concept is planning, because the signals are mostly about timing. IRMAA is based on your income from two years earlier, which the video calls the two-year trap. A large IRA withdrawal, a Roth conversion, or the sale of a house in one year can raise your Medicare bill two years later, so these moves are best planned with that calendar in view, sometimes by spreading them across years to stay under a threshold. When the new benefit notice arrives, do your own net math: ignore the gross raise and look at the deposit after Medicare. Then check your provisional income against the tax thresholds, because a bigger benefit can make more of it taxable. Open enrollment, which runs October 15 through December 7, is the yearly chance to compare plans.

Several nuances trip people up. The first is that the $29.50 figure is a projection, not an announcement; the official premium usually lands in November, and projections can move. The second is the difference between percentage and dollars: a premium that rises 29% sounds alarming, but the rule compares dollar amounts, so what matters is whether $6.60 exceeds your raise. The third is that the standard premium is the baseline. Your actual premium might be higher through IRMAA or, under older enrollment situations, different. The fourth is that Medicare Advantage plans, which the video says average a projected $12 a month next year down from $14.37, are an addition to Part B, not a replacement: you generally still pay the Part B premium. The average will not be your plan, so compare your actual doctors, drugs and costs.

What happens when the protection fails or does not apply? In a year of high inflation and a big COLA, the rule is irrelevant, and the Part B bite is simply deducted. In a year with a small or zero COLA, the rule can bind, but then a quirk appears: because those held-harmless people pay less than the full premium, the cost they avoid is spread across everyone else, which is one reason premiums for non-protected enrollees can rise more sharply. That helps explain the large $17.90 jump the video mentions. For people not covered, such as high earners and new enrollees, there is no floor at all, and their net check can fall. The larger risk is the one the video stresses: building a plan that assumes the COLA keeps you whole, when premiums, taxes and a measuring basket that does not match your spending all take their share first.

This concept connects to several others worth keeping in your head together. The COLA itself is set from CPI-W inflation data, so the size of your raise and the likelihood of hold harmless protection both trace back to the same inflation report. The tax angle comes next: provisional income thresholds set in 1983 and 1993 and never indexed mean each COLA can push more of your benefit into taxable territory, a second hand reaching into the same raise. IRMAA links Medicare costs to your tax return from two years earlier, so withdrawal sequencing matters. And the broader theme is that the trust funds' projected shortfall, with the combined funds running dry around 2034, adds pressure to the whole system. Together these explain why retirees should plan around net income, not the headline increase.
