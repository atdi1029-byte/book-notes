Taxation of Social Security Benefits (Provisional Income)

Most people assume Social Security is tax-free money, a benefit you paid for all your working life and now simply collect. For many retirees that assumption is wrong. The taxation of Social Security benefits is a set of federal income tax rules under which part of your benefit is counted as taxable income. The rules rest on a measure called provisional income, which is half of your Social Security benefit plus all your other income: pensions, IRA withdrawals, wages, interest, dividends, and even tax-exempt municipal bond interest. Compare that number to fixed dollar thresholds. The higher it is, the larger the share of your benefit, up to a cap of 85%, that gets added to your taxable income.

Why does this matter? Because it quietly changes what your retirement income is worth. Two retirees can each receive the same $2,880 monthly benefit and end up with very different spendable money, depending on what else they draw from savings. It also changes how you should think about every dollar you withdraw. For someone inside the affected range, an extra dollar from an IRA does not just add one dollar of taxable income. It can also pull more Social Security into the tax base, so the effective rate on that withdrawal runs well above the stated bracket. The video calls this the "shadow data" of the tax code: a cost most people never see on a statement until the tax return arrives.

Here is the mechanism, step by step. First, take your Social Security benefit and cut it in half. Second, add every other source of income, including tax-exempt interest, which is counted here even though it is not taxed elsewhere. The total is your provisional income. Third, compare it to the thresholds. For a single filer, below $25,000 nothing is taxed. Between $25,000 and $34,000, up to 50% of benefits can be taxable. Above $34,000, up to 85% can be. For married couples filing jointly, the lines are $32,000 and $44,000. Fourth, the taxable portion is added to your other income and taxed at your ordinary bracket rates, not at a special rate.

Notice the phrase "up to." Nobody is ever taxed on more than 85% of their benefit, and 85% does not mean an 85% tax rate. It means 85% of the benefit is included in taxable income, and that included amount is then taxed at your normal rate, perhaps 12% or 22%. The phase-in is also gradual. Within each band, each extra dollar of provisional income pulls in 50 cents or 85 cents of benefit, depending on the band. That is why the effective marginal rate in these zones can reach about 1.85 times your bracket rate. A retiree in the 12% bracket can effectively face roughly 22% on the next dollar of IRA income.

A worked example makes this concrete, using the video's numbers. The average retired worker collects about $2,880 a month, roughly $34,560 a year. Suppose a single retiree also takes $20,000 from an IRA. Provisional income is $17,280 (half the benefit) plus $20,000, which is $37,280. That is above $34,000, so up to 85% of benefits can be taxable. Now add the video's expected 3.5% COLA, about $73 more a month, or roughly $876 a year. Half of that raise, $438, lifts provisional income again. The raise is partly taxed, and for this retiree, who sits above the top threshold, the taxable share is already near the 85% ceiling.

"Those first thresholds were set in 1983. The second one came in 1993. They've never been adjusted for inflation. Not even once."

That brings us to the core problem, a form of bracket creep. Income tax brackets and the standard deduction are indexed to inflation every year, so they grow with prices. The Social Security thresholds of $25,000/$32,000 and $34,000/$44,000 are not. Meanwhile, the benefits themselves rise every year through the cost-of-living adjustment (COLA). Every COLA pushes provisional income up, while the lines stay frozen where they were set in the Reagan and Clinton years. Retirees whose real, inflation-adjusted income has not changed at all keep drifting across the lines. The video cites a 2015 Social Security analyst projection that about 57% of beneficiary families would owe tax on benefits by 2025. Nobody voted for that. Inflation did it automatically.

How do investors and retirees use this? The practical lever is withdrawal sequencing, the order and size of what you pull from different account types. Money from a traditional IRA or 401(k) counts fully toward provisional income. A Roth IRA withdrawal does not count at all. Neither does selling a home with excluded gains or, in many cases, borrowing against assets. Retirees often fill the lower bands with a mix of sources, use Roth conversions in low-income years before benefits start, and avoid lumpy withdrawals that spike one year. The video's advice is blunt: talk to a tax professional about the order in which you take money out, because the sequence can matter as much as the amount.

There are several gotchas. First, the thresholds apply to filing status, and the married-filing-separately case is brutal: if you lived with your spouse, your threshold is essentially zero. Second, tax-exempt municipal bond interest counts in provisional income, so a "tax-free" holding can still push benefits into taxation. Third, a tax deduction is not a change in the formula. The 2025 law's extra deduction for people 65 and older, up to $6,000 per person for tax years 2025 through 2028, lowers your taxable income, but it does not alter how much of your benefit is taxable. And it is temporary. Fourth, some states also tax benefits, though most do not, so check yours separately.

What happens when the planning fails? The first failure is the cliff-like feel of a year with unusual income. A large IRA distribution, a Roth conversion, or a sale of investments can push provisional income far above the top threshold in one year, making 85% of benefits taxable that year. The second risk is the two-year ripple into Medicare. The IRMAA surcharge, a higher Part B and Part D premium for higher earners, is based on your income from two years earlier. The video notes it starts above $109,000 for a single filer and $218,000 for a couple, and that the standard Part B premium of $22.90 a month can jump to $284.10 above the line. One big year can cost you twice, once in the tax on benefits and again in Medicare premiums two years later.

Finally, connect this to the bigger picture. The COLA is calculated from CPI-W and arrives with a lag, and the video calls it "a receipt," not a raise. Medicare Part B premiums come out first, which shrinks the check. Then the tax on benefits reaches in, using thresholds that never moved. That is the video's "three hands" argument, and the tax hand is the least visible. The lesson is not to fear the rule but to plan around it: calculate your provisional income every year, watch the gap between it and the thresholds, and do not assume a number labeled "cost of living" fully covers yours. Trust fund shortfalls projected for 2034 add one more reason to build savings that grow faster than prices.
