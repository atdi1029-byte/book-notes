PITI (Principal, Interest, Taxes, Insurance)

PITI is the acronym for the four components that make up a typical monthly mortgage payment: Principal (the portion that pays down the actual loan balance you borrowed), Interest (the cost of borrowing that money, calculated against the outstanding balance), Taxes (your share of local property taxes, collected monthly instead of in one annual bill), and Insurance (your homeowner's policy premium, plus mortgage insurance if you put down less than 20%). Lenders bundle all four into a single monthly draft so the loan stays current and the collateral stays protected. Most people think of their "mortgage payment" as one number, but PITI is really four separate financial obligations stitched together, each behaving very differently over time.

PITI matters because it explains why a fixed-rate mortgage is not actually fixed. The video's transcript makes this distinction explicitly: your interest rate is locked, but "the cost of carrying the physical collateral, that is not." A 39% share of homeowners surveyed still believed their payment could never change — a dangerous misconception, because taxes and insurance are the two components with no ceiling. Understanding PITI is the difference between budgeting for a stable housing cost and being blindsided by an escrow shortage. As the transcript notes, roughly 65% of American homeowners with an escrow account are projected to hit a shortage in a given year, forcing an average jump of about $175 more per month — money that has nothing to do with the loan itself.

Mechanically, PITI works through an account called escrow (sometimes called an impound account). Instead of you paying property taxes and insurance directly in large lump sums once or twice a year, the servicer collects one-twelfth of the estimated annual bill each month, folds it into your mortgage payment, and holds it in a separate escrow account. When the tax bill or insurance premium comes due, the servicer pays it out of that account on your behalf. Once a year, the servicer runs an escrow analysis: it compares what it actually collected against what it actually paid out. If taxes or insurance rose faster than projected, there's a gap — and that gap gets passed back to you, either as a lump-sum demand or spread across future payments, depending on its size.

The real-world numbers from the video illustrate how lopsided PITI has become. Property insurance now averages $29 a month on a standard loan — an all-time high — and represents 9.6% of the entire monthly payment, having surged nearly 80% since early 2020. Combine taxes and insurance together, and they now eat about 21% of the total monthly payment across the 450 largest U.S. metro markets. In nine states that figure exceeds 40%, and in Pensacola, Florida, it sits around 43%. Meanwhile the average escrow shortage nationally runs about $21,100 per borrower, forcing that roughly $175 monthly out-of-pocket jump — even though the loan's principal and interest schedule hasn't changed at all.

Traders and investors watch the T and I components of PITI as a leading signal for regional housing stress and insurance-sector profitability. When insurance costs rise faster than the underlying catastrophe risk they're supposed to price, as the video argues is happening now, that's a margin signal, not a risk signal. The video points out that the global reinsurance benchmark — essentially the wholesale price insurers pay to insure themselves — has fallen 23% from its post-pandemic peak, back to 2021 levels, even as retail insurance premiums hit record highs. That spread between falling wholesale risk and rising retail premium is exactly the kind of divergence an analyst would flag as unsustainable margin expansion rather than a genuine cost pass-through, and it tends to correct once state regulators intervene, as Florida and Louisiana have already done with rate cuts.

A common misconception is that a "shortage" and a "deficiency" mean the same thing on an escrow statement — they don't, and the difference has real legal teeth. Under Regulation X (12 CFR §1024.17), a shortage means your escrow balance fell below the required target but never went negative; in that case, if the gap equals at least one month's escrow payment, your servicer must offer you the option to spread the deficit over 12 or more equal monthly installments — they cannot legally demand a lump-sum wire in 30 days. A deficiency means the account actually went negative, meaning the servicer had to front real cash; in that case, the mandatory 12-month spread protection disappears, and the servicer only has to offer two equal payments. Reading which word appears on your statement is the single most consequential piece of financial literacy buried in fine print most people never read.

"Your interest rate, well, that's locked. The cost of carrying the physical collateral, that is not."

PITI breaks down — or rather, exposes its cracks — when local conditions diverge sharply from national averages. The video is careful to separate national noise from real, isolated pain: nationally, the average property tax bill rose only about 3% (roughly $127 a year), and popular claims of $1,200–$2,500 annual tax spikes are, per the transcript, "off by a factor of 10 to 20." But brutal local exceptions exist — Memphis saw an assessment jump around 34%, Baltimore around 27% — and those borrowers face a genuinely different reality than the national average suggests. Similarly, insurance markets diverge by state: Louisiana and Florida saw insurers cut rates as reinsurance costs fell, while California's FAIR Plan (the state's insurer-of-last-resort) grew 157% in four years with a 29% rate hike, because the driver there is regulatory and wildfire risk policy, not the national catastrophe-cost trend.

"That is not a natural disaster. That is pure corporate margin expansion."

PITI connects directly to debt-to-income (DTI) ratio calculations, since lenders qualify borrowers using the full PITI payment, not just principal and interest — meaning a spike in the T or I component can push an existing homeowner's effective debt burden higher without them ever missing a payment or refinancing. It also connects to the broader reinsurance cycle, since retail insurance premium trends lag wholesale catastrophe-bond and reinsurance pricing by roughly six months (the market resets every January and July). For anyone assessing regional housing affordability or an insurer's stock, watching PITI's tax-and-insurance slice — not just headline mortgage rates — reveals stress or margin opportunities that the "locked rate" narrative completely hides.
