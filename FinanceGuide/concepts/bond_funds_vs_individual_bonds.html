Bond Funds vs. Individual Bonds

An individual bond is a loan you make to a government or company. It has a fixed coupon, a fixed maturity date, and a promise to return your principal (the par value) on that date. A bond fund pools money from thousands of investors and buys hundreds of bonds. You own shares of the pool, not the bonds themselves. The difference that matters most is that a bond ends and a fund does not. The fund's manager keeps selling bonds as they age and buying new ones, so the fund never reaches a maturity date. Its share price, the net asset value (NAV), moves every day and never has to return to any particular number.

This matters because millions of retirees treat the two as the same thing, and they are not. Retirement portfolios lean heavily on bond funds, which are cheap to buy, need no minimum, and are easy to hold in a 401(k) or IRA. The common belief is that bonds are the safe part of the portfolio. That belief is roughly true for a bond held to maturity and can be badly false for a fund. When yields jump, a retiree who needs to sell shares for living expenses can realize real losses. The video's warning targets this gap between what people think they own and what they actually own.

The mechanism starts with the seesaw between yields and prices. Say you buy a new 10-year Treasury paying a 4% coupon. If market yields later rise to 5%, nobody will pay full price for your 4% bond when new ones pay 5%, so its price falls until its effective yield matches the market. How far it falls depends on duration, a measure of price sensitivity to yields. A rough rule is that each 1 percentage point rise in yields cuts the price by about one percent per year of duration. A 6-year duration fund loses about 6%. A fund with a 16-year duration loses about 16%. Longer bonds hurt far more.

Here is the key difference. The individual bond's price drop is a mark-to-market loss that fades with time. As the bond ages, its price is pulled back toward par, which traders call pull to par. If you hold it to the maturity date, you collect every coupon and get your full principal back, and the paper loss disappears. The fund has no such anchor. Its manager is always replacing aging bonds with new ones of similar length, so the fund holds a roughly constant duration forever. The loss does not vanish on a schedule. It only gets repaired as the fund slowly reinvests at the new, higher yields, and that takes years. The loss is already in the NAV, and the only way to avoid it is to wait.

The video puts numbers on the moment. The 30-year Treasury touched 5.59%, the 10-year sat at 5.26%, and the MOVE index, which measures bond market volatility, was above 100 after a roughly 35% jump in a single month. Meanwhile the stock market's VIX was about 16. The host said that intermediate and long-term bond funds "carry substantial embedded capital losses" in this environment. As a rough illustration, a long fund with a duration around 15 would lose about 15% of its value for every point yields rise. A long bond bought years earlier at a 3% or 4% coupon is deeply underwater today, and the fund holding thousands of such bonds shows it.

"It's not the same thing as owning a bond and holding it to maturity. Commingled funds, they carry substantial embedded capital losses."

Investors use this distinction in three practical ways. First, they match the tool to the time horizon. If you need a specific sum on a specific date, such as a tuition bill or a mortgage payoff, an individual bond or bond ladder (bonds maturing in staggered years) gives you a known outcome. Second, they use funds when they want diversification, convenience, or automatic reinvestment, and accept that the price will float. Third, they use the video's step three, buying Treasury bills directly. Bills mature in one to six months, so there is almost no duration risk, and the video cites yields above 3.8% with no counterparty risk when you hold the bills yourself. Whichever you pick, check the fund's stated duration before you buy it.

The "hold to maturity" argument has real limits, and a careful investor should know them. First, the loss is not imaginary just because it is unrealized. If you hold a 4% bond while new bonds pay 5.5%, you are earning less than the market offers, and that opportunity cost is real. Second, "safe if held to maturity" only helps if you can in fact hold, which means you must not need the money early. Third, individual corporate bonds carry default risk and are costly to trade, since small buyers often pay wide spreads. Funds, by contrast, spread out defaults. Also note that a fund is not secretly hiding its losses: the NAV is public, so the damage is visible to anyone who looks.

The failure case for a fund is a forced-selling spiral. When yields spike, NAVs fall, and nervous holders sell. Redemptions force the manager to sell bonds into a falling market, often the more liquid ones first, which pushes prices down further and makes the next wave of selling more likely. Retirees withdrawing monthly make the problem worse because they lock in losses at the bottom. In 2022 many "safe" aggregate bond funds lost 13% or more in a year, the worst in decades. Individual bonds can fail in their own way: a corporate issuer can default, or an investor can panic and sell early. But a Treasury held to maturity cannot lose principal, and that is the video's point.

This concept connects directly to the rest of the video's story. The jump in the MOVE index is exactly the volatility that damages long-duration funds. The basis trade and swap-spread discussion explains how Treasury yields can be pushed around by leveraged players, and those yield moves are what reprice every bond fund overnight. Higher yields also raise discount rates, which the host links to stock valuations, since "multiple contraction is an unforgiving hammer." The advice to favor short-dated bills over the "duration belly" is the same idea in portfolio form: reduce interest-rate sensitivity, hold cash-like instruments directly, and keep dry powder. Understanding duration is the key that ties bond funds, individual bonds, and the video's three-point playbook together.

To summarize the whole idea for a conversation: a bond fund and a bond can own the same underlying debt but give you very different experiences. The bond has a finish line, so a price decline is temporary if you can wait. The fund has no finish line, so its decline is permanent until it is repaired by reinvestment at higher yields, a process that takes roughly as many years as the fund's duration. Funds win on diversification and convenience, and individual bonds win on certainty. The risk to watch is the length of the bonds, not the label "bond." In a market where the 30-year touches 5.59%, ask how long your fund's bonds run, and whether you could actually hold them as long as it takes.
