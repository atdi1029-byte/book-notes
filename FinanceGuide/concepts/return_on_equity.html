Return on Equity (ROE)

Return on equity, usually shortened to ROE, measures how much profit a business or a trade produces for every dollar of the owner's own money invested in it. The formula is simple: net profit divided by equity, where equity is what is left after you subtract debts from assets. If you put $100 of your own money into a venture and it earns $10 in a year, your ROE is 10%. The figure ignores how large the whole operation is and asks one question: what did my capital earn? Companies report it, analysts compare it across firms, and traders use the same logic on individual positions. It is one of the most quoted numbers in finance, and one of the easiest to inflate.

ROE matters because it separates the size of a profit from the quality of the use of capital. A hedge fund that makes $50 million sounds impressive until you learn it needed $10 billion of investor money to do it. Another that makes $5 million on $20 million of equity has done something far more efficient. Capital is scarce, so it flows toward high ROE and away from low ROE. When a strategy's ROE collapses, funds quietly withdraw, balance sheets shrink, and the market function that strategy provided can weaken. ROE is therefore an early signal of where leverage is heading, which is why it sits underneath the video's argument about the Treasury basis trade.

Mechanically, ROE has two moving parts: the return on the total assets you control and the amount of borrowed money you use to control them. If you buy $100 of assets with $10 of your own money and $90 of borrowed money, then a 1% gain on the assets is $1. That $1 is a 10% return on your $10 of equity. Subtract interest costs first, because the borrowed money is not free. The relationship is roughly ROE = asset return × leverage, after financing costs. Leverage is the multiplier. Ten times leverage turns a 1% asset return into 10% on equity. Fifty times leverage turns a 0.05% return into 2.5%. The same multiplier also applies to losses, which is the part that matters most when something goes wrong.

The video gives a clean worked example. A claim circulating in financial media said the Treasury basis trade earned a 15–20% return on equity. The host rejected it with arithmetic. The profit in a basis trade is a tiny spread, the gap between the price of a Treasury bond and the futures contract tied to it. He used 5 basis points, which is 0.05%. At 50 times leverage, 0.05% × 50 = 2.5%. To reach 15%, you would need a spread roughly six times wider, around 30 basis points, which does not trade in reality. As he put it:

"Five basis points times 50 leverage is 2.5%. It's math. To get to 15% you would need a spread six times wider than anything trading in reality."

He added that the IMF found basis returns rarely reach even 2%. That is a useful cross-check, because it comes from an outside researcher looking at actual data, not from the host's own estimate. Notice what the host is doing here as a practical skill: he is not looking up a database. He is doing back-of-the-envelope ROE in his head from two inputs, spread and leverage, and testing whether a headline survives it. Any claimed return can be sanity-checked this way. Ask what the underlying margin is, how much leverage is being assumed, and whether multiplying them gives the number being advertised. If the answer is off by a factor of six, something in the claim is wrong, fabricated, or cherry-picked.

Traders and investors use ROE in several practical ways. When comparing companies, a persistently high ROE (say, 15% or more for years) often marks a business with a durable advantage, though you must check whether debt is doing the work. When evaluating a leveraged trade, ROE is the number that tells you whether the effort and risk are worth it relative to alternatives such as simply holding Treasury bills. The video's Bank of America quote captures this: funds were leaving the basis trade voluntarily because it "stopped paying." When ROE falls toward what safe assets yield, nobody needs to force the exit. The trade becomes not worth the risk and balance sheet space. Falling ROE therefore predicts de-leveraging better than regulators' warnings do.

The gotchas are significant. First, ROE can be raised without any improvement in the business, just by adding debt. A company that buys back shares with borrowed money shrinks equity and lifts ROE while becoming riskier. Second, very small or negative equity produces absurd results: a firm with $1 of equity and $1 of profit has a 100% ROE, which says nothing useful. Third, a quoted return is only meaningful with its time period and its cost basis. A 2.5% return on equity over a year is very different from 2.5% on a single trade held for a week. Fourth, headline ROE numbers often leave out financing costs, haircuts (the cash a lender requires you to put up), and margin requirements, all of which reduce what a leveraged trader truly earns.

When ROE-driven thinking breaks, the failure usually comes from the leverage multiplier working in reverse. A trade earning 2.5% on equity at 50 times leverage can lose that entire year of profit if the spread moves just 5 basis points the wrong way, and it can wipe out all of its equity if the move reaches 2%. Lenders respond to rising volatility by raising haircuts, which forces the trader to put up more equity or sell, which pushes prices further against the position. Thin ROE therefore leaves almost no cushion. This is why the video treats the wide claims of 15–20% with suspicion: a strategy that really earned that would attract enormous capital, and a strategy that earns 2% on equity with 50 times leverage is fragile rather than lucrative.

ROE connects to several other ideas in the video. It is tied directly to leverage and to the basis trade, since the leverage ratio is the multiplier. It links to swap spread trades, where the host says leverage migrated after the basis trade's returns dried up: capital chases the highest ROE available, and when one trade's spread tightens, funds rotate into another, so leverage "never disappears, it only mutates." It also relates to dealer balance sheet rules like the supplementary leverage ratio, which determine how cheaply a trader can finance positions and therefore how large ROE can be. Reading any leveraged strategy through ROE gives you a quick, honest test of whether the story being told about it adds up.
