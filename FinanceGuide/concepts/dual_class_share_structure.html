Dual-Class Share Structure (Non-Voting Shares)

A dual-class share structure is a way of organizing a company's stock so that it has more than one type of common share, and the types carry different rights. The most important right that can differ is the vote. Ordinary shares usually give you one vote per share on things like electing the board, approving mergers, and changing the company's charter. In a dual-class company, one class (often called Class A or Class B) might carry ten votes per share, while another class carries one vote, or none at all. Non-voting shares are the extreme version: they own a slice of the company's profits, assets, and dividends, but they have no say in how the company is run. Everything else about them, including the ticker-tape price, can look just like ordinary stock.

This matters because ownership and control are two different things, and dual-class stock pulls them apart. In a normal company, if you own 20% of the economic value, you control about 20% of the votes. In a dual-class company, a founder can own 15% of the equity but control 60% of the votes, which means outside shareholders can fund the business, bear most of the risk, and still be unable to replace management, block an acquisition, or veto a risky strategy. Founders like this because it lets them think long-term without activist pressure. Critics dislike it because it removes the main accountability mechanism public shareholders have. It affects who gets to be a "real" owner and how much a vote is worth in the market.

Mechanically, the structure is written into the company's charter (its certificate of incorporation) before or at the time of going public, or amended later with a shareholder vote. The charter defines each class: how many shares are authorized, how many votes each carries, and whether they get the same dividends and the same payout if the company is sold. Typically the high-vote shares are held by founders and insiders and are not sold to the public, while the lower-vote or no-vote shares trade on the exchange. To create a new class mid-life, the company usually needs approval from the existing voting holders, which is easy if insiders already control the majority. Often a stock split or stock dividend is the delivery vehicle, handing existing holders the new class as a "free" distribution.

That is exactly what the video describes at Meta. The company reported third-quarter 2026 revenue of $64.6 billion, up 26%, and announced a two-for-one stock split, but with a twist: the split is paid in a new class of non-voting shares, not more of the existing stock. Picture a holder of 100 shares. After the split they would still have their 100 original voting shares and would receive 100 new non-voting shares, so 200 shares total, each worth roughly half as much per share. Their slice of the economics is unchanged on day one. But the company now has a second currency, a class of stock that can be issued to investors without handing over any votes, and Meta said it can use that class to raise capital.

Why would that be useful? Imagine a founder-controlled company that wants to sell $20 billion of new stock. If it sold ordinary voting shares, the founder's percentage of the vote would shrink, possibly toward the point where control is at risk. Selling non-voting shares raises the same cash with zero dilution of control. The buyers get a claim on earnings but no governance power. In the video, the presenter linked this to the quarter's numbers: Meta spent $34.91 billion on capital expenditure, far more than its operations produced, leaving free cash flow of negative $2.12 billion, and it raised its 2026 capex range to $135 to $145 billion. A company burning that much cash has a reason to want every funding tool available.

"When a company that brings in 64 billion a quarter still burns cash and creates a new share class with no vote to help pay the bills, the AI arms race has officially moved from the income statement to the shareholders' wallet."

Investors use this information in several ways. First, the creation of a non-voting class is a signal about management's funding intentions. Authorizing a security is not the same as selling it, so the presenter's read, that the build-out is eating cash and shareholders may be asked to pay, is an inference, not a confirmed fact. Still, companies rarely build a financing tool they never plan to use. Second, investors watch for a later announcement of an actual issuance, because new shares dilute existing holders' per-share earnings. Third, they compare the prices of voting and non-voting classes. Where both trade, the non-voting shares often sell at a small discount, and that gap is the market's price tag on a vote. Fourth, they check whether index providers and funds allow the new class, since that decides how much natural demand exists.

There are several gotchas. The first misconception is that a stock split by itself changes anything. A plain split just cuts the share price and multiplies the share count, leaving each holder's total value unchanged; the substance here is the new class, not the split. The second is that non-voting means worthless. These shares still have full economic claims, and many investors happily own them for the cash flows. The third is that existing holders are being "charged" for the split. They are not, since the split is a distribution, and nothing is taken from their accounts. The real cost, if any, comes later through dilution if new shares are sold, and through a permanent loss of influence if control is already concentrated in a few hands.

The structure can fail or cause damage in a few ways. Entrenched control means that if management makes poor decisions, such as overspending on projects that never pay back, outside holders have little power to stop it, and the only remedy is selling the stock. Non-voting shares can also be hit harder in a crisis, since they sit at the back of the line for governance and sometimes carry weaker protections in a takeover unless the charter guarantees equal treatment. Index providers have at times excluded or limited companies with unequal voting rights, which can reduce demand. And if the company issues many new shares at a depressed price, existing holders are diluted when the stock is already down. In the video, Meta fell 6.98% Thursday and 0.96% Friday to $642.62, down 9.4% for the week, so a fundraising at those levels would be expensive for owners.

This concept connects to several others in the video. It ties directly to free cash flow, because the whole reason a company reaches for new capital is when operating cash flow, minus capital spending, turns negative, as it did for Meta at negative $2.12 billion. It links to capex and the debate over whether AI spending will pay off, the central question the presenter raised for Microsoft, Alphabet, and Amazon the following week. It also connects to dilution and to the other ways of funding spending, such as debt: the video noted that Core Weave fell more than 10% on debt worries, showing that investors are already nervous about how this build-out is financed. Dual-class stock is one more answer to the same question: who pays for the AI arms race, and who gets a say in how the money is spent?
