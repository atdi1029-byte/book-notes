Housing Affordability Ratio (Payment-to-Income)

The housing affordability ratio, often called the payment-to-income ratio, measures what share of a household's gross monthly income goes toward housing costs — principally the mortgage principal, interest, taxes, and insurance. The most commonly cited benchmark is the 28% rule: conventional lending wisdom holds that a household should spend no more than 28% of gross income on housing. Extend that to all debt obligations — car loans, student debt, credit cards — and the ceiling rises to 36%, forming the classic 28/36 rule that most mortgage underwriters have used for decades. These thresholds aren't arbitrary; they emerged from decades of default data showing that borrowers above those levels fail at meaningfully higher rates during economic stress.

Why does this ratio matter beyond the individual borrower? Because housing is the single largest asset on most household balance sheets, and mortgage payments are the most inflexible item in most household budgets. When a large share of buyers are stretched past 30% of income, the entire economy becomes fragile in a specific way: any income disruption — a job loss, a medical bill, a divorce — tips households into default or forces consumption cuts elsewhere. Consumer spending, which drives roughly 70% of U.S. GDP, contracts. Retailers, restaurants, and discretionary businesses feel it before the official data does. The affordability ratio is therefore not just a personal finance metric — it's a leading indicator of credit stress, consumer spending capacity, and ultimately, the health of the broader economy.

The mechanical construction of the ratio is straightforward but has several moving parts. Take the median household income — as of recent years, roughly $80,000 annually, or about $6,700 per month gross. Then compute the total monthly housing payment on the median-priced home. At a 7% 30-year fixed mortgage rate on a $372,000 home with a standard 20% down payment, the principal and interest alone run approximately $1,990 per month. Add property taxes (typically 1–1.5% of value annually) and insurance, and the total payment approaches $2,400–$2,500 per month. Divide that by $6,700 gross income and you're at 36–37% — already above the traditional danger threshold before the buyer has a car payment or a dollar of student debt.

The real-world example that crystallized this for markets came directly from a builder's earnings call. In September 2026, Lennar — one of the two largest homebuilders in the United States — reported quarterly results on the same day the Federal Reserve raised rates. Lennar's chairman didn't bury the affordability problem in footnotes. He stated it plainly:

"Today, at approximately 7% with the 10-year Treasury hovering right around 5%, the buyer at the median family income is stretching well past 30% of gross income to carry a home."

That admission landed in a financial press focused entirely on Fed dot plots and rate projections — and most of it went unnoticed. But the builder's own financials told the same story in numbers. Lennar's gross margin had compressed from roughly 17.5% the prior year to 15.8% — a nearly two-point deterioration in a single year. The company was offering an average of 12% of the home price back in mortgage rate buydowns and other incentives on a typical $372,000 home. New orders had fallen 9% year-over-year. Each of those metrics is a direct consequence of buyers hitting the affordability wall. When the monthly payment exceeds what buyers can comfortably carry, the builder has two options: cut the price or buy down the rate. Lennar was doing both, and it was showing up as margin compression.

Traders and investors use affordability ratios as a leading signal for several related trades. The most direct: when affordability deteriorates sharply, homebuilder stocks tend to compress on both volume (fewer closings) and margin (more incentives needed to close). That Lennar quarter illustrated the pattern — weaker orders, margin erosion, incentive escalation. More broadly, high payment-to-income ratios suppress existing-home sales because potential sellers are often locked into 3% mortgages from 2020–2021 and won't voluntarily trade into a 7% payment — the so-called rate lock-in effect. This suppresses inventory, which perversely keeps prices elevated, which keeps the affordability ratio high even as rates eventually fall. Investors in mortgage REITs, building materials companies, and consumer discretionary names all have exposure to this dynamic.

The most common misconception about housing affordability is treating it as a static snapshot. The ratio is the product of three variables — home prices, mortgage rates, and incomes — and all three move, just at different speeds. Prices are sticky downward: sellers resist cutting, and distressed supply takes months to hit the market. Incomes adjust slowly, typically lagging inflation by a year or more. Mortgage rates, by contrast, can move 50–100 basis points in a single month. This asymmetry means affordability can deteriorate very quickly (rates spike, prices hold) and recover very slowly (prices eventually fall, rates stabilize, incomes catch up). Analysts who model housing as purely rate-sensitive miss the price and income legs of the stool entirely. You need all three moving in the right direction simultaneously before affordability genuinely heals.

The ratio also breaks in ways that don't show up in aggregate data. Geographic bifurcation is the clearest failure mode: a national median income and a national median home price produce a national ratio that masks the fact that San Francisco and rural Alabama have almost nothing in common in affordability terms. Similarly, the ratio uses gross income, not take-home pay — but mortgage payments come from take-home pay. A buyer in a high-tax state earning $100,000 gross might net $68,000, meaning a 28% gross ratio is actually closer to 41% of spendable income. The ratio also ignores HOA fees, maintenance, and the insurance premium spikes that have hit coastal and disaster-prone markets since 2022. In some Florida ZIP codes, insurance alone adds 3–5 percentage points to the effective payment-to-income ratio.

The affordability ratio connects directly to several other important financial concepts. The most immediate is the mortgage spread — the gap between the 30-year fixed mortgage rate and the 10-year Treasury yield. That spread normally runs 170–200 basis points, but it widened significantly in 2023–2026 as mortgage-backed securities investors demanded higher compensation for prepayment uncertainty. A normalized spread alone could reduce mortgage rates by 50–75 basis points without any Fed action, improving affordability meaningfully. The ratio also links to rental yield dynamics: when owning is prohibitively expensive, rental demand rises, pushing rents up, which changes the rent-vs.-buy calculus and further inflates the asset class that was already unaffordable. Finally, builder margins function as a real-time affordability barometer — when Lennar starts giving back 12% of the purchase price in incentives, the market is telling you that the natural clearing price is 12% below the listed price, and that affordability at the listed price simply doesn't exist for most buyers.
