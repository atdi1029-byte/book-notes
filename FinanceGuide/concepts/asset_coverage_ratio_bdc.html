Asset Coverage Ratio (BDC 150% Requirement)

The asset coverage ratio measures how many dollars of assets a fund holds for each dollar of debt it owes. A business development company (BDC) is a closed-end investment company, created by Congress in 1980, that lends to or invests in mid-sized private businesses. Because a BDC can borrow, federal law caps how much it can borrow. Under the Investment Company Act, as amended in 2018, a BDC must keep total assets (less non-debt liabilities) of at least 150% of its senior debt. In plain terms, the fund can't be financed by lenders more than two-to-one against its shareholders' equity. It's a statutory leash on leverage.

It matters because BDCs are the retail face of private credit, and retail money is the kind that panics. A BDC sells shares to ordinary investors and 401(k) rollovers. It also uses bank credit lines and bond issues to buy more loans than its equity alone could. Leverage raises yield in good times and magnifies losses in bad ones. The coverage test limits that magnification, which protects lenders and shareholders alike. It also decides whether a fund can borrow when it most needs cash. In a redemption wave, the fund's flexibility is determined by this one ratio, so the number reaches well beyond accounting. It governs whether investors get their money out.

The mechanics are simple arithmetic. Take total assets, subtract liabilities that aren't borrowed money, and divide by senior securities representing debt, meaning credit lines, notes, and preferred stock. If the result is 150% or higher, the fund may borrow more or make distributions. The key identity is that assets equal equity plus debt. Set the ratio at 1.5 and debt can be at most twice equity: a 2-to-1 debt-to-equity limit. The video's phrasing, "$1.50 of net assets for every dollar of debt," is loose. Strictly, the test counts total assets, so $1.50 of assets per $1 of debt means only 50 cents of equity cushion. That distinction changes how fast trouble arrives.

Here is a worked example. A BDC has $100 million of shareholder equity and borrows $200 million, giving $300 million of assets. Coverage is $300 / $200 = 150%, exactly at the limit. Now the borrowers' loans are marked down 10%, to $270 million. Debt is still $200 million, so coverage drops to 135%, a breach. Equity fell from $100 million to $70 million, a 30% loss from a 10% markdown. That is leverage working in reverse. Most non-traded BDCs run well below the maximum, often near 1-to-1 debt to equity (about 200% coverage), because they know this cliff exists. A fund at 200% coverage absorbs a roughly 25% asset decline before the same breach.

"When loan valuations contract, guess what? That margin shrinks rapidly, limiting their ability to borrow emergency short-term cash."

This connects directly to the video's central story. Investors in large non-traded BDCs asked to withdraw about $7.1 billion in the first half of the year, and funds cap quarterly repurchases at 5% of net asset value. A manager short on cash has three sources: sell loans at a discount, prorate redemptions, or borrow against the credit line. Borrowing is the cheapest way to avoid a fire sale, and the video notes Fitch-rated vehicles issued over $25 billion of fresh debt for liquidity. But every dollar borrowed to pay a redeemer raises debt and shrinks equity at once. The asset coverage ratio is the gauge that says how many more such dollars the fund is allowed to borrow.

For an investor, the ratio is a due-diligence line item, which is why it's item three on the video's audit checklist. You find it in the 10-Q and 10-K, usually in the financial highlights or the senior securities table, where the fund states its asset coverage per unit of debt. Look at three things: the level (170% is far safer than 155%), the trend over several quarters, and the distance to 150% expressed as a markdown the portfolio could absorb. Then cross-check it with the other two checklist items. Rising PIK income, prorated tender offers, and thinning coverage together tell you the fund's liquidity is being squeezed from several sides at once.

There are nuances that trip people up. First, the 150% test isn't a speed limit that triggers a forced liquidation the moment it's crossed. Second, the numerator uses the fund's own fair-value marks on mostly Level 3 assets, which are illiquid loans valued by the manager and board using models. Mark them generously and coverage looks healthy; mark them honestly and it may not. Third, PIK interest increases the reported asset balance without any cash arriving, so it can prop up the ratio. Finally, the 150% is a legal maximum, not a safe level. Lenders add their own covenants, often tighter than the statute, and those can bite first.

When coverage falls under 150%, the consequences are concrete. The fund generally can't take on new senior debt, and it can't declare dividends or buy back shares unless coverage is restored after the payment (Section 18 of the 1940 Act). Repurchases are exactly the redemptions investors are demanding, so a breach can freeze the exit door that the 5% cap already narrows. The manager's cures are all painful: sell loans, often at discounts, to repay debt; suspend the tender offer; cut the dividend; or raise new equity at a weak price. Forced sales then push valuations lower for the remaining holdings, which can shrink coverage further. That is the feedback loop that turns a markdown into a run.

The ratio ties together nearly every theme in the video. The maturity mismatch, short-term redemption promises backed by three-to-four-year loans, is what makes borrowing necessary. Floating-rate loans priced at SOFR plus about 650 basis points, with SOFR near 3.88%, stress borrowers, and weaker borrowers mean lower valuations, which cut the numerator. PIK income hides delinquency and so flatters the ratio until reality arrives. Tender prorating and gate suspensions are the downstream effects when coverage runs out of room. Think of the asset coverage ratio as the pressure gauge on the whole system: it shows how much slack remains before a fund moves from managing withdrawals to being forced to suspend them.
