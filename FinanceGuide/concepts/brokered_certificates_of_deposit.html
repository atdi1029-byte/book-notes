Brokered Certificates of Deposit

A brokered certificate of deposit (brokered CD) is an ordinary bank CD that reaches the customer through a middleman. A regular CD is simple: you walk into a bank, or open an account online, and agree to lock up money for a set term, say 12 months, at a fixed rate. In return the bank pays interest and returns your principal at maturity. A brokered CD is the same product, issued by the same kind of bank and usually insured the same way. The difference is the sales channel. A brokerage firm or a specialized deposit broker buys or distributes the CD, and the investor holds it inside a brokerage account instead of opening a relationship with the bank itself.

The thing to understand is why banks issue them. A bank is not a place that stores your money. As the video puts it, a bank is a leverage spread machine: it collects funding, lends it out at higher rates, and pockets the difference. Funding is the raw material, and brokered CDs are one way to buy more of it. The bank doesn't need you as a customer. It needs your cash for a year or two. A brokerage can gather it from thousands of investors across the country within hours, and the bank never has to build branches, run ad campaigns, or win anyone's checking account.

Why does this matter beyond one product? Because a bank's profit depends on the gap between what it pays for funds and what it earns on loans, called net interest margin. Cheap, sticky funding is the most valuable thing a bank owns. When it has to replace that funding with something pricier, its margin shrinks. That shows up in earnings, in dividends, in how willing the bank is to lend, and in its stock price. Brokered CDs are an important clue. When a bank leans on them heavily, it usually means customers aren't bringing in enough deposits on their own. The CD itself is boring. What it reveals about the bank's funding is not.

Here is the mechanism, step by step. First, a bank decides it needs funding, perhaps because deposits are leaving or loan demand is growing. Second, it sets a rate and a term and offers the CD through a broker or a deposit-listing service. Third, the brokerage lists the CDs on its platform, often hundreds of them from banks nationwide, and investors buy in chunks as small as $1,000. Fourth, the investor's money flows to the issuing bank, which records the CD as a deposit and a liability. Fifth, the bank pays interest, often semiannually or at maturity, and returns principal on the due date. The broker earns a fee, typically paid by the bank, so the investor often sees no explicit commission.

The key contrast is with core deposits. Core deposits are checking, savings, and payroll balances that customers keep out of habit, convenience, or inertia. They tend to be cheap and sticky. Brokered deposits are the opposite: rate-sensitive, transactional, and held by people with no loyalty to the bank. A brokered CD investor bought it because the rate was the best available and will happily move next year if another bank pays 10 basis points more. That is why regulators treat a heavy brokered CD reliance as a sign of a more fragile funding base. Each CD is a fixed-term contract, but the bank must continually win the money back through higher rates at every maturity.

Now the video's example. The host describes Bank of America's third-quarter results: $6.9 billion in net income, down 12% from a year earlier, including a $1.2 billion regulatory and compliance charge, with the stock falling nearly 5% in one session. The deeper point he makes is about deposits. With SOFR near 3.87% and Treasury bills paying over 4%, corporate treasurers sweep idle cash out of zero-interest checking accounts into government money market funds and repo. When a bank loses, say, $50 million in cheap core deposits, it can't call in 30-year mortgages or 10-year commercial real estate loans. So it goes shopping for replacement funding.

"The bank is forced to replace those lost deposits by borrowing expensive wholesale money in the open market or issuing brokered certificates of deposit at, guess what, premium rates."

The arithmetic shows why this hurts. Suppose a bank held $50 million in operating deposits costing nothing, funding loans that yield around 4%. That is $2 million a year of pure spread. If those deposits leave and the bank replaces them with brokered CDs at 4.5%, it now pays about $2.25 million a year for the same funding. The $2 million of spread turns into roughly negative $250,000 on that slice of the balance sheet. The video describes this as a 200-basis-point step up in funding costs set against loan books locked in at three or four percent. Multiply that by billions of dollars and you can see the margin compression he describes.

How do investors and traders use this? There are two sides. As a saver, brokered CDs are a way to capture high fixed rates, often above what the local bank offers, and to build a ladder of maturities: some CDs maturing in 6, 12, 18, and 24 months. Because they trade in a secondary market, you can sell before maturity, though not at par. As a bank analyst or stock investor, you read brokered deposits as a signal. Check the bank's quarterly filings and call reports for brokered deposits as a share of total deposits, and compare the trend over several quarters. A rising share, together with a rising cost of deposits and a shrinking net interest margin, suggests pressure. The video's advice is to check "deposit stability ratios" before relying on a regional bank's dividend.

There are several gotchas. First, brokered does not mean unsafe. Most brokered CDs are FDIC insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, and the insurance is the same as for a branch CD. But that limit applies per bank, so if you hold several CDs from the same issuer through different brokers, they are added together. Second, selling early has no guaranteed price. If rates have risen since you bought, your CD is worth less on the secondary market, and thin trading can widen the discount. Third, many brokered CDs are callable, meaning the bank can redeem them early if rates fall, which leaves you reinvesting at lower yields. Fourth, a high headline rate is not always a sign of weakness, because even healthy banks use brokered CDs for ordinary balance sheet management.

When does this break? Consider the bank's side first. Brokered funding can be pulled away or priced out fast. Under federal rules, banks that are not "well capitalized" face restrictions on accepting brokered deposits and on the interest rates they can pay, and a bank that falls below that standard can lose access to this funding source just when it needs it most. That creates a vicious cycle: losses cause capital to fall, capital falls restrict funding, and restricted funding deepens losses. A bank that financed long-term loans with short-term brokered CDs also faces a rollover problem when those CDs mature in a stressed market and investors demand more. The 2023 regional bank failures showed how quickly confidence in funding can vanish. Depositors with uninsured balances left first, and replacing them cost far more.

For an investor, the risks are quieter but real. If an issuing bank fails, the FDIC steps in and covers insured amounts, normally paying out within days. But amounts above $250,000 are exposed, and you can lose interest accrued but unpaid or face delays in access. Premium purchases are a further trap: if you pay above par on the secondary market, the FDIC covers principal and accrued interest but may not cover the premium you paid. And the interest is taxed as ordinary income each year, even if the CD pays at maturity, which can leave you owing tax on money you haven't received.

Finally, connect this to the bigger picture of the video. Brokered CDs sit in a family of what the host calls wholesale funding, along with Federal Home Loan Bank advances and repo borrowing. All of them cost more than core deposits and respond directly to market rates. When money market funds and Treasury bills pay 4% or more, they compete directly with bank deposits, and the bank has to meet that price somewhere. The loop runs like this: deposit outflows lead to wholesale borrowing, which raises funding costs, which compresses net interest margin, which tightens lending standards, which squeezes small businesses and consumers. The video's takeaway fits in one line: headline profit is a glamour metric, but deposit plumbing is the real engine. For your own money, the lesson is the other half of that coin. If banks are paying 4.5% to rent your cash through a broker, you shouldn't leave it earning almost nothing in checking.
