Mark-to-Market Accounting

Mark-to-market accounting (often called fair value accounting) is the practice of valuing an asset on your balance sheet at what it could be sold for today, not at what you paid for it. The word "mark" means to record a value, and "market" means the current price other buyers would pay. If you bought a bond for $100 and the market will now pay only $70, a mark-to-market balance sheet shows $70. If you bought a stock at $50 and it trades at $80, it shows $80. The idea is simple: your books should reflect reality as it stands today, not the history of your purchase price. Its opposite is historical cost accounting, where an asset stays at its original price until you sell it or formally write it down.

The video builds a picture around a restaurateur who bought $100,000 of Wagyu beef at peak prices and stored it in a walk-in freezer. Under mark-to-market, his balance sheet would say the meat is worth what a buyer would pay on the sidewalk today, perhaps $20,000. He is out $80,000 and everyone can see it. The video calls this the "free market discipline," and it is the baseline the rest of the argument depends on. As the presenter puts it, "Under normal free market capitalism, assets are carried under a discipline called mark-to-market. If an asset loses value, you mark it down. You deduct it from your equity and let the market see your true net worth."

Why does this matter to the real economy? A balance sheet is a claim about solvency. Equity, also called net worth or capital, is assets minus liabilities. A bank's liabilities are mostly your deposits, and those are fixed in dollar terms: a $10,000 deposit is owed as $10,000 no matter what happens to bond prices. So if the assets shrink while the liabilities stay put, equity is what absorbs the loss. Marking assets to market therefore shows depositors, regulators, lenders and investors how thick the cushion really is. When that cushion is overstated, people make decisions on false information: they deposit, lend, invest and buy bank stock believing the institution is safer than it is. Accurate marks are how a market prices risk properly.

Mechanically, mark-to-market works like this. At each reporting date, the institution finds a current price for each asset. For actively traded items such as Treasuries or stocks, that is a quoted market price. For thinly traded items, accountants use models or comparable trades, often called mark-to-model. The change in value since the last date is a gain or a loss. Where the loss goes depends on the accounting bucket. In trading accounts it flows through the income statement and reduces profit right away. In available-for-sale (AFS) accounts, the loss bypasses earnings and lands in accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI), a line inside equity. The video notes that AFS declines "visibly reduce tangible common equity." Either way, the loss is recognized in reported equity.

Now the bucket that escapes it. Under FASB accounting standard ASC 320, a bank can classify a security as held-to-maturity (HTM) if management attests it has both the intent and the ability to hold it until it matures. HTM securities are carried at amortized cost, essentially the purchase price, and price declines are not recorded on the balance sheet. The video describes it this way: "a magical statutory veil drops over the asset. Even if that 30-year Treasury is trading at 60 cents on the dollar in the secondary market, the bank carries it on its official regulatory balance sheet as 100 cents on the dollar." The loss still exists. It just is not in the equity number. It appears only in footnotes as an "unrealized loss," which is why the FDIC's quarterly banking profile can report it separately.

The numbers in the video show the scale. Banks loaded up in 2020 and 2021 on 10-year and 30-year Treasuries and mortgage bonds yielding roughly 1.5% to 2.2%. The video then says the 10-year yield has climbed to about 5.27% with the Fed funds target near 4%. Bond math is unforgiving here: a long bond with a 2% coupon, when the market demands more than 5%, loses roughly 30 to 40% of its value. The FDIC reported total unrealized losses on bank securities rising $19 billion in a single quarter to $325.1 billion. Some of that sits in AFS, where it is already deducted from equity, and a large share sits in HTM, where it is invisible on the balance sheet. The freezer door is padlocked, and the label says "held to maturity."

How do investors and traders use this? They don't take reported book value at face value. Analysts read the securities footnote in a bank's filings, or Schedule RC-B of the call reports mentioned in the video, and subtract HTM unrealized losses from reported equity to compute a "true" or market-adjusted tangible equity. They compare that adjusted figure to uninsured deposits. A bank whose adjusted equity is near zero or negative, with lots of uninsured depositors who can leave with a click, is fragile. Traders watch the gap between HTM book value and market value, the deposit mix, and deposit outflows. Short sellers and cautious depositors use the same math. This was the core of the analysis in the 2023 bank failures, when Silicon Valley Bank's HTM losses exceeded its equity.

There are gotchas. First, an unrealized loss is not automatically a real loss. If a Treasury is held to maturity and the bank is paid par, the loss reverses over time as the bond "pulls to par." The HTM argument is not fraudulent; for a bank with stable funding, it is a reasonable view. Second, the danger is not the accounting itself but the mismatch: the bank only escapes the loss if it never has to sell. Third, mark-to-market is not perfectly accurate either. Prices in stressed markets can overshoot, and critics argue that forcing marks in a panic can create a downward spiral, as happened in 2008. Fair-value rules have real tradeoffs, and both sides of that debate have a point. Finally, "unrealized" does not mean "harmless." It means the loss is waiting for a trigger.

That trigger is the video's central warning: selling any HTM bond can force the rest of the portfolio to be marked to true market value. Under the accounting rules, selling or transferring more than an insignificant amount of HTM securities calls into question the bank's stated intent to hold to maturity. Auditors can then "taint" the whole HTM portfolio and require it to be reclassified to AFS, at which point every hidden loss hits AOCI and equity all at once. So a bank that needs cash cannot quietly sell a few bonds. Selling a little means recognizing everything. This is the padlock: opening the door for one shelf of meat means the auditor inspects the entire freezer. That is why the video says such banks are "trapped." It also explains why a bank facing deposit flight, the video's "deposit rate spread" catalyst, may prefer to borrow, pay up for funding, or restrict lending than to sell bonds.

"If he had to open that door today and sell that meat on the sidewalk to pay his electric bill, well, nobody would give him more than $20,000."

This connects to several other concepts. Duration measures how sensitive a bond's price is to rate changes, and it determines how big the mark-to-market hole is. Liquidity risk and deposit flight are what force a sale. Net interest margin suffers because low-yield bonds earn 2% while funding costs rise. The uninsured deposit share decides how quickly the trigger can be pulled. One caution about the video: it frames this as regulators "hiding" insolvency, but HTM losses are publicly disclosed in filings, and whether a given bank is truly insolvent depends on its own funding and mix, not the industry total. Use the $325 billion as a signal to investigate individual banks, not as proof that every one is broke. The practical habit is to always ask of any balance sheet: at what price could this really be sold, and does the owner ever have to sell?
