Regulated Utility Stocks

A regulated utility is a company that delivers an essential service, usually electricity, natural gas, or water, as a legally protected local monopoly. Nobody wants two sets of power lines on one street, so the state hands one company the exclusive right to serve a territory. In return, the state controls what that company can charge. Duke Energy, NextEra Energy, Southern Company and Dominion Energy are the large publicly traded examples named in the video. A regulated utility stock is a share in one of these companies. You are buying a slice of a business with no real competitors, steady demand, and prices set by a government body rather than by the market. That mix makes utilities one of the most predictable corners of the stock market, and it also makes them one of the most politically exposed.

Utilities matter because everything else depends on them. Homes, factories, hospitals and data centers all need power, and demand barely drops in a recession. That reliability is why investors treat utilities as a defensive sector, a place to hide when growth stocks wobble. They also matter because of scale. Building and maintaining the grid takes hundreds of billions of dollars, and the bonds and stock sold to pay for it make utilities some of the largest borrowers in the economy. The video's central claim is that a new demand source, AI data centers, is pushing that borrowing sharply higher, and the bills ultimately land on households. Whether or not you accept the video's framing, the mechanism it describes is real, and it explains both utility profits and your own electric bill.

The mechanism is called cost-of-service ratemaking, and it works differently from every other industry. The video puts it plainly: a utility is not supposed to profit on the electricity or fuel itself, which is passed through at cost. Profit comes from building things. Each power line, substation, transmission tower and turbine the utility installs is added to its rate base, the total value of the physical assets that regulators allow it to earn a return on. The state utility commission, the PUC, then sets an allowed return on equity (ROE), typically in the 9% to 10.5% range the video cites. Rates are set so the utility collects its operating costs plus that return on the rate base. Bigger rate base, bigger allowed profit.

"The more money they borrow and spend on mega scale infrastructure projects, the larger their rate base becomes and the larger their absolute profit check at the end of the year."

Here is the cycle step by step. First, the utility identifies a need, such as new substations for a data center cluster. Second, it borrows money, usually by issuing bonds at 6% or 7%, and sells new shares to cover the equity portion. Third, it builds the project and adds the cost to its rate base. Fourth, it files a rate case with the PUC asking to raise customer rates enough to cover the new costs plus the allowed return. Fifth, the commission holds hearings and approves some or all of the request. The utility then earns roughly 10% a year on the equity portion of the new assets, for decades, while paying 6% or 7% on the debt portion. Most of the utility's profit growth comes from this loop, not from selling more kilowatt-hours or cutting costs.

Now the real-world example. The video points out that US electricity demand was nearly flat from 2010 to 2020, so utilities had little reason to expand. Then hyperscalers such as Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta and Amazon began building huge AI data centers. A single gigawatt-scale facility can use more power than hundreds of thousands of homes, and the video says regional data center consumption could rise 300% over five years in places like northern Virginia, Texas, Ohio and Georgia. Utilities responded with hundreds of billions of dollars of planned substations, high-voltage lines and gas turbines. The video says utilities filed nearly $10 billion in new rate increase requests in the first quarter alone, affecting more than 81 million people. For shareholders of Duke, NextEra, Southern and Dominion, that spending growth translates directly into a growing rate base and rising earnings.

Investors use utilities in a few practical ways. The first is income: because earnings are predictable, utilities pay generous dividends, often well above the market average, and they tend to raise them slowly and steadily. The second is defense: utility stocks usually fall less than the broad market in downturns. The third is rate base growth, the figure most analysts focus on. If a utility says it will grow its rate base 8% a year, and the allowed ROE holds, earnings per share should grow at a similar pace. Investors watch capital spending plans, the outcomes of rate cases, and the allowed ROE each commission grants. A utility in a state with a friendly regulator, constructive rate case outcomes and fast-growing demand tends to earn a higher valuation than one in a hostile jurisdiction.

Utilities are also very sensitive to interest rates, which is a nuance beginners often miss. Because they carry heavy debt and are bought for their dividends, they behave like bond substitutes. When Treasury yields rise, a utility's 3% to 4% dividend looks less attractive, and the stock often falls even if the business is fine. The cost of borrowing also rises, which eats into the gap between what the utility pays on its debt and what it is allowed to earn. A second misconception is that the "guaranteed" ROE is a guaranteed profit. It is only an allowed return. The utility must still win approval for the spending, build efficiently, and avoid cost overruns, and regulators can disallow costs they judge to be imprudent.

The risks come from the same place as the profits: politics. The video's argument is that the system pushes costs onto captive residential customers, and that sets up a feedback loop. When bills jump 40%, as the video's opening describes, voters get angry, and commissions respond. They can lower the allowed ROE, deny or trim rate increases, force the utility to separate data center costs from residential rates, or require large customers to sign long-term contracts and pay for their own connections. The video notes that organized consumer groups have blocked or cut proposed increases in several jurisdictions. Other failure modes are real too: wildfire liability has bankrupted utilities such as PG&E, nuclear and power plant projects have run billions over budget, and if the data center boom slows, the new infrastructure could end up underused. A rate base built for demand that never arrives is a problem for someone, either shareholders or ratepayers.

This concept connects to several others. It ties to capital expenditure and leverage, since the model rewards heavy borrowing and spending. It links to interest rate sensitivity and the bond market, because utilities are among the biggest corporate issuers and their stocks trade like bond proxies. It connects to the defensive versus cyclical sectors idea, and to dividend investing. It also runs through the video's wholesale-market point about PJM capacity auctions, which show how deregulated markets can pass price spikes to bills in a different way. The key takeaway for a conversation about markets: a regulated utility's profit is set by a negotiation between the company, the regulator and the public. Shareholders do well when that negotiation allows steady growth, and customers push back when the bill starts to feel like someone else's investment.
