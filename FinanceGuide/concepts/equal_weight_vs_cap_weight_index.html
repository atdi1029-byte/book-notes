Equal-Weight vs. Cap-Weighted S&P 500

The S&P 500 is a list of roughly 500 large U.S. companies, but a list alone doesn't tell you how much each company counts. That is the job of the weighting method. In the version most people mean when they say "the S&P," the cap-weighted index, each company counts in proportion to its market capitalization: share price times shares outstanding. A $4 trillion company counts about forty times as much as a $100 billion one. The equal-weight S&P 500 ignores size and gives every company the same slice, about 0.2% each. Same 500 stocks, two very different scoreboards, and they can tell opposite stories on the same day.

This matters because "the market" is not one thing. When a headline says the S&P rose, it is describing mostly what a handful of giants did. Index funds, retirement accounts, and pension portfolios track the cap-weighted version, so trillions of dollars rise and fall with it. Meanwhile the equal-weight version is a better picture of the typical stock. If you want to know whether the average company, the average sector, or the average investor's holdings are doing well, you have to compare the two. The gap between them is one of the cleanest measures of how concentrated a market rally really is, and concentration is a risk in its own right.

Mechanically, the cap-weighted index works like this. Take each company's market cap, add them all up, and divide each company's cap by the total to get its weight. If the total is $60 trillion and one company is worth $6 trillion, it is 10% of the index. When its price rises 5%, the index moves about 0.5% from that stock alone. Weights drift automatically as prices move, so winners grow more important without anyone trading. The equal-weight index instead resets every holding to the same weight on a schedule, typically quarterly. Between resets, winners drift above 0.2% and losers below. At each reset, it sells some of what rose and buys some of what fell.

The video gives a clean example. On Wednesday, November 11, 2026, a beat-and-raise from CoreWeave pushed the AI trade back to life. The cap-weighted S&P 500 gained 1.31% to 7619 and the Nasdaq rose 1.6%. Yet the presenter noted the equal-weight S&P lagged with a gain of roughly eight-tenths of a percent, and said that "makes sense given who drove the trade yesterday." Think through why. AI-linked mega-caps hold enormous weights in the cap-weighted index, so a strong day for them lifts it heavily. In the equal-weight index, each of those names is about 0.2%, so their gains barely register and the other 450-plus stocks dominate.

"The equal weight S&P lagged with a .8% gain. That makes sense given who drove the trade yesterday."

Traders use the comparison as a diagnostic. When the cap-weighted index beats equal-weight by a wide margin, the rally is narrow: a few leaders are carrying the tape. When equal-weight keeps pace or wins, participation is broad, which is usually healthier. The presenter turned this into a live test for the next morning, with Nvidia down about 7% pre-market on reports its board was weighing a change at the top and Nasdaq 100 futures off about 0.7%. His checkpoint was:

"If the equal weight index holds green while the NASDAQ bleeds, this again is a rotation, not a liquidation."

The logic is worth spelling out. A rotation means money leaves one group of stocks and goes into others, so the overall market can hold up even while leaders fall. A liquidation means investors are selling everything, often because of leverage or fear, so nearly all stocks fall together. If Nvidia and the Nasdaq drop but the equal-weight index stays green, then capital is moving into the other 490 or so names. If equal-weight drops along with the Nasdaq, selling is broad, and that is a much more worrying signal. The presenter also pointed to breadth: advancers beat decliners 1.48 to 1 on the NYSE, positive but "not super powerful."

There are gotchas. First, equal-weight is not "the real market" and cap-weighted is not "the fake one." Each answers a different question. Cap-weighted measures what the average dollar invested earned, which is what most investors literally experienced. Equal-weight measures what the average stock did. Second, equal-weight carries its own tilts: it holds far more smaller companies, so it behaves partly like a mid-cap index, and it overweights sectors with many names, such as industrials and financials, while underweighting technology. Third, quarterly rebalancing creates turnover and costs, and the funds that track it usually charge higher fees. Fourth, a single day tells you little. Equal-weight lagging by half a point on one session is noise; a gap that persists for months is a trend.

Things break in a few ways. In a year driven by mega-cap leadership, equal-weight can trail for long stretches, and investors who switched to it for "diversification" can underperform for years. The reverse also happens: when leaders crack, the cap-weighted index falls harder than equal-weight, because concentration cuts both ways. And the rotation-versus-liquidation test can fail. A rotation can turn into a liquidation within days, and the video hints at why: hedge funds are highly leveraged and "twitchy" near year-end, so forced selling can hit everything at once. The 10-year yield sitting near 5% and a 30-year auction at 1:00 are exactly the kind of events that can flip a calm rotation into broad selling. Treat the signal as a clue, not proof.

This concept connects to several others. Market breadth, such as advancers versus decliners, is the cousin of the equal-weight comparison, and the two often confirm each other. Concentration risk is the underlying issue: when a few names such as Nvidia carry the index, one headline, like the governance reports in the video, can move the whole market, which the presenter captured with "when one company carries the theme, one headline reprices the theme." Rebalancing explains why equal-weight systematically sells winners. And interest rates tie in because AI-heavy mega-caps depend on borrowed money for data center buildout, so higher yields hit the cap-weighted leaders hardest. If you remember one thing, make it this: always ask who is driving the move, and compare the two versions of the index to find out.
