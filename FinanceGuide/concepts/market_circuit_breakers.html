Market Circuit Breakers

A circuit breaker is a rule that automatically pauses trading on an exchange when prices fall too fast. It works like the breaker panel in your house. When too much current runs through a wire, the breaker trips so the wiring doesn't catch fire. In markets, the "current" is the speed of a price decline, and the "fire" is a panic in which people sell because everyone else is selling. In the US, the rules halt trading in the entire stock market after a drop of a set size from the prior day's close. Separate rules pause individual stocks that move too far, too quickly. These pauses are short and mechanical, and no human has to decide to trigger them.

Circuit breakers matter because markets can fail in ways that have nothing to do with a company's value. When prices fall fast enough, three things happen together. Buyers step back because they can't tell what is going on. Sellers rush to get out before prices fall further. Leveraged investors get margin calls that force them to sell. Prices then overshoot what any sensible estimate of value would justify, and real people pay for it. Retirement accounts drop, companies can't raise money, and some investors sell near the bottom. A circuit breaker doesn't stop prices from falling. Its job is to stop a disorderly, panicked fall from feeding on itself, by giving everyone time to catch up.

Mechanically, the US market-wide system uses the S&P 500 as its yardstick and has three levels. A decline of 7% from the previous close triggers Level 1, and a decline of 13% triggers Level 2. Each of those halts trading for 15 minutes. A decline of 20% triggers Level 3, which closes trading for the rest of the day. Levels 1 and 2 only apply between 9:30 a.m. and 3:25 p.m. Eastern. A halt in the last half hour would do more harm than good, so a drop in that window doesn't trigger one. Each level can only trigger once per day. Below the market-wide rules sit the single-stock "limit up-limit down" bands, which pause any one stock that moves outside a narrow price range.

The video points to the history behind these rules. On October 19, 1987, the S&P 500 fell about 20% in a single day. The presidential task force known as the Brady Commission blamed part of the damage on mechanical selling by funds using a strategy called portfolio insurance. SEC staff later estimated that at least $55 billion of stock was covered by that strategy on that Monday, while three insurers alone sold just under $2 billion of stock plus futures. The rules were written in advance, and they all fired at once. The speaker says that circuit breakers exist now "mainly because of that." The 1987 crash was the template for what regulators wanted to prevent.

"The rules were all written in advance and they just all fired at once."

The 1987 story shows what circuit breakers are for. In a crash, selling comes from forced sellers as well as from people who changed their minds. A margin call, a stop-loss order and a portfolio-insurance rule are all instructions set before the fall started, and they execute whatever the news is. A halt breaks that chain for a few minutes. Margin desks can contact clients, and buyers can reprice their bids. Sellers who were about to hit "sell" can ask whether they really want to. The pause can't change the underlying news, but it can separate a panic from a considered decision.

The video's main subject, the "ghost seller" of trend-following funds, shows why this connects to the present. Goldman Sachs estimated that CTAs, the computer-driven commodity trading advisors, would sell about $31 billion of S&P 500 futures in a flat week. Their volatility rules say to hold less stock when the ride gets bumpier, so a 4.4% one-day drop to 7,353 made them shrink their positions for weeks afterward. That is mechanical selling of the kind that broke markets in 1987, and it is why the speaker draws the comparison. But he also draws the distinction: today's trend followers aren't 1987 portfolio insurers, the market is far larger, and breakers exist. The $31 billion is about 1.2% of a normal week's E-mini volume of roughly $2.5 trillion, so it is a headwind rather than a tidal wave.

For investors, the practical use of circuit breakers is a mental one more than a trading one. Know the thresholds. A 7% single-day drop is rare enough that a halt tells you the market is under extreme stress, and you should expect volatility, wide spreads and fast reversals to follow. Don't try to trade into a halt, because orders can't execute while trading is paused, and when it reopens, prices often jump. Long-term investors should do the opposite of what the machines do: avoid reacting to the move itself. A halt is the market telling you the move is mechanical, and the speaker's advice applies here too: understand why a move is happening before you act on it.

There are real gotchas. First, a circuit breaker doesn't set a floor, and it doesn't guarantee prices will recover. In 1987 the market kept falling the next day in many places. Second, some people argue that halts cause a "magnet effect," meaning that as prices approach the trigger level, traders rush to sell before the halt traps them. That would speed up the very decline the rule was meant to slow. Third, the breaker only covers the stock market and doesn't stop trading in other markets that are still open. Fourth, 4.4% is not a circuit-breaker day: the November 15 drop in the video was well short of the 7% Level 1 trigger, and the market kept trading normally.

When breakers fail, or seem to, the evidence is mixed. On March 9, 2020, the S&P 500 hit Level 1 within minutes of the open, and it did so again on March 12, 16 and 18, four times in ten days. Trading resumed each time without chaos, which many people count as a success. But prices kept falling through each halt, and the market lost roughly a third of its value by late March. Single-stock limits have also misfired. On August 24, 2015, hundreds of ETFs and stocks were halted repeatedly in the opening minutes and some traded at absurd prices. The lesson is that a pause buys time but doesn't create buyers.

Circuit breakers connect to several other ideas in the video. They answer program trading and rule-based selling, which is why the speaker mentions them next to portfolio insurance. They are linked to volatility and the VIX, since the CTA funds in the video size their positions off volatility, and a rise in volatility is what triggers both their selling and, in extreme cases, a halt. They also relate to negative gamma, where options dealers' hedging amplifies moves in either direction. Together these are the plumbing of modern markets: rule-based flows can lean the same way at once, and circuit breakers are the last safeguard when they do. That is one reason the market is more resilient to a 1987-style repeat, though not immune to a scare.
