Reaching for Yield

Reaching for yield is what happens when investors, unsatisfied with the income they can earn on safe assets, move into riskier ones to get a higher payout. The behavior has a simple engine: people and institutions have return targets. A pension fund may have promised 7% a year. A retiree may need 5% to pay the bills. A money manager may be judged against a benchmark that yields more than Treasuries. When safe bonds pay less than the target, the gap has to be filled somehow, and the easiest way is to accept more credit risk, less liquidity, longer maturities, or more complexity. The key word is "reaching." The investor isn't hunting for a better deal. They are stretching for a number, and the risk they take on often gets underpriced along the way.

It matters because it moves enormous sums of money and, in doing so, changes what risk costs for everyone. When a large crowd chases income into the same corner of the market, prices there rise and yields fall. Borrowers in that corner, whether junk-rated companies, emerging-market governments, or banks selling capital notes, can then raise money cheaply. That looks like healthy markets, but the cheapness is not a sign that the risk has shrunk. It is a sign that buyers are being paid less to carry it. Reaching for yield therefore builds fragility in the financial system quietly, over years, and the bill arrives later, usually all at once and usually when conditions turn.

The mechanism runs in steps. First, safe yields fall or stay below what investors need. Second, a few managers move into a higher-yielding category and report better income and returns. Third, that performance attracts inflows and imitators, because nobody wants to lag peers who are visibly earning more. Fourth, the new money pushes up prices and compresses the credit spread, the extra yield a risky bond pays over a safe benchmark such as a government bond. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent, so a spread of 200 basis points means 2 extra percentage points of yield. Fifth, as spreads shrink, the pay per unit of risk drops, but the trade keeps going because past gains feel like proof the risk is small.

The video gives a clean example in the market for bank capital. After 2008, regulators required banks to hold loss-absorbing debt that protects depositors and senior bondholders. In Europe this became AT1 bonds, also called CoCos (contingent convertibles), which usually yield 7% to 9%. The speaker compares them to airbags: they sit between senior bondholders and common equity, and they take the first loss if the bank is in trouble. That higher yield exists precisely because the holder agrees to be sacrificed first. The speaker reminds viewers that in March 2023 Swiss regulators wiped out about $17 billion of Credit Suisse AT1s to zero overnight, a vivid reminder of what the extra yield was compensating for.

"Money managers were hunting for yield. They chased bank capital higher and higher."

The chase did what the mechanism predicts. Over roughly two years, investors who had pushed the Credit Suisse wipeout to the back of their minds bought bank capital aggressively, and the spread on Bloomberg's global CoCo Tier 1 index fell below 200 basis points for the first time in history. Wall Street called it "ridiculously tight." Put plainly, investors were accepting less than 2 percentage points over safe benchmarks for an instrument designed to be wiped out in a crisis. Banks loved it, because they were raising regulatory capital at the cheapest relative cost in a generation. The price of that cheap funding was paid by the buyers, who held risk that was no longer being priced like risk.

Then the repricing came. In the week covered by the video, the average spread on the Bloomberg multicurrency global CoCo index jumped from below 200 to 253 basis points, and year-to-date returns on the sector fell from 3.6% in August to 1.6%. New deals showed the damage. Bank of New York Mellon had to pay 32.4 basis points more than the post-crisis low it had secured over the summer to sell dollar preferred shares. Italy's Banco BPM saw an order book of €1.5 billion for a €400 million AT1 deal shrink by about half as buyers balked, forcing aggressive concessions. A fresh Belfius AT1 slipped to 99 cents on the euro almost right after pricing. Fund managers said extension risk and reset risk were "biting," and they wanted hazard pay.

For investors, the practical use of this idea is as a warning light and a discipline check. Tight spreads relative to history are a signal that compensation for risk is thin, which doesn't predict timing but does lower your margin for error. Traders watch new-issue concessions, order-book sizes, and how quickly fresh bonds trade below their issue price, because these show whether buyers still have appetite. For your own portfolio, the habit is to ask a plain question whenever a yield looks attractive: what is the extra payment for, and what is the worst case? Compare the spread to its own history, check where the instrument sits in the capital structure, and size the position so a wipeout would hurt but not wreck you. The video's conclusion was to avoid reaching into distressed yield now.

There are several common misunderstandings. One is that a high yield is a reward for skill. Usually it is a price paid for taking risk, and you only collect it if the bad outcome doesn't happen. Another is that tight spreads mean the market has judged the risk to be low. In reality, tight spreads often reflect crowded demand, not improved fundamentals, and they can tighten for years before reversing. A third is confusing income with return: a 7% coupon is meaningless if the price falls 10%. And "reaching" isn't only about credit. Investors also reach by lengthening maturity, accepting illiquidity, or buying structures they don't fully understand, such as notes with call dates, resets, and extension features that can shift the cash flows against them.

When the trade breaks, it breaks through liquidity and herd behavior. Everyone who reached for yield holds the same positions and wants out at once, but buyers have vanished, so prices gap lower and spreads widen quickly, as the 53-basis-point move in a single week showed. Funds facing redemptions must sell what they can, which often means selling good assets alongside the risky ones. Banks, now paying more for capital, tighten lending, which spreads the stress from markets into the real economy. The tail risk is the Credit Suisse outcome, a complete write-off. And because the damage is concentrated in the crowded corner, investors who thought they were earning a "safe" 7% discover they were underwriting the bank's survival.

Reaching for yield connects to several other ideas in the video. It is the flip side of the flight to quality: once the risky trade cracks, the same institutions rush into short-duration US Treasuries, which is why the 10-year yield dropped from above 5.3% to 4.96%, a safety bid rather than a vote of confidence in the economy. It ties to the point that why yields fall matters more than how far they fall. It also shows up in bank economics: wider hybrid spreads raise a bank's cost of capital, squeeze net interest margins, and can limit buybacks and dividend increases. Underneath it all is a behavioral pattern, recency bias plus herd behavior, where two quiet years make investors forget the Credit Suisse lesson.
