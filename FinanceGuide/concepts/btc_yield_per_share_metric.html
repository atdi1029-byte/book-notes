BTC Yield (Bitcoin Per Share)

BTC Yield is a measure invented by Strategy (the company formerly called MicroStrategy) to answer one question: how much Bitcoin does each share of our stock represent, and is that number growing or shrinking? Think of it like a pizza cut into slices. The pizza is the company's Bitcoin pile, and each slice is a share. If the pizza grows but the company also cuts it into many more slices, each slice might actually get smaller. BTC Yield tracks the size of your slice over time, expressed as a percentage change. A positive number means each share is backed by more Bitcoin than before. A negative number means each share is backed by less.

It matters because owning a Bitcoin treasury company's stock is only a good deal if the stock gives you something that simply buying Bitcoin yourself doesn't. If you can buy Bitcoin directly in an account in two minutes, the only reason to pay for a company wrapped around it is that the company grows your Bitcoin exposure faster than you could on your own. BTC Yield is the scorecard for that promise. It cuts through headline numbers like total holdings or quarterly profit, which can look spectacular while your own slice quietly shrinks. It affects how the stock is valued, how investors judge management, and whether issuing more shares is helping you or hurting you.

Mechanically, the calculation is simple. Take the company's Bitcoin holdings and divide by the number of shares outstanding. Strategy uses a fully diluted share count, meaning it also counts shares that could be created from convertible bonds and similar instruments. That gives Bitcoin per share. Compare that figure at two dates, and the percentage change is the BTC Yield. Two things move it. The numerator, Bitcoin held, rises when the company buys coins and falls when it sells them. The denominator, share count, rises whenever new stock is issued. So the metric boils down to a race: is Bitcoin growing faster than the share count, or slower?

Here is the real example from the video. For the year through October 25, Strategy's BTC Yield was -3.7%, even though the company reported a third-quarter net income of about $19.5 billion. How can both be true? The profit was a mark-to-market gain, an accounting entry created because Bitcoin's price rose during the quarter. No coin was sold and no cash arrived. It was a bigger valuation of the same pile. BTC Yield ignores price entirely and counts coins per share. Strategy held 849,327 Bitcoin, far more than at the start of the year, yet share issuance and coin sales meant each share represented less Bitcoin than on January 1.

"A $19.5 billion profit and less Bitcoin per share. Those two facts shouldn't live in the same press release."

The reason the number turned negative is the heart of the lesson. Strategy owes roughly $1.48 billion a year in interest and dividends, mostly on its preferred stock, including the 12% STRC. Those bills must be paid in dollars, and the company has three sources: sell new common stock, sell new preferred stock, or sell Bitcoin. Selling common stock at a big premium to the Bitcoin behind it adds coins per share. But the premium, measured by mNAV, had shrunk to about 1.1. At that level, new shares bring in barely more value than the Bitcoin they dilute, so issuing them adds almost nothing. Issuing them to pay dividends, rather than to buy coins, makes things worse.

The sales side shows the other lever. Strategy sold 1,395 Bitcoin in the second quarter and 5,552 in the third, nearly 7,000 in total, to help fund dividends. Selling a coin lowers the numerator directly. In the third quarter those sales covered $239.9 million of the $419.6 million in preferred dividends, more than half. The average sale prices, about $59,663 and $62,780, were well below the company's $75,451 average cost. And there is a twist in the arithmetic: the same $19.5 billion paper profit could not pay anyone. Cash dividends need real cash, which is why BTC Yield can fall in the same quarter profit soars.

Investors use BTC Yield as a quality check on every capital raise. A rising BTC Yield means management is issuing stock at premiums and converting that premium into extra coins for existing holders, which is the engine that justified owning the stock over the coin. A falling or negative yield says the machine is running in reverse. Practical signals: watch the weekly Monday filings for coins bought and sold, watch the share count, and watch mNAV. If mNAV drops below 1, every new share sold costs existing holders Bitcoin per share. Compare BTC Yield against a simple benchmark too: if it is negative for long stretches, you'd have done better holding Bitcoin directly or a spot fund.

There are several gotchas. First, BTC Yield is a company-defined metric, not an accounting standard like earnings per share. Strategy chooses the formula, the share count, and the start date, so treat it as a useful lens, not an audited fact. Second, it measures coins, not dollars. A positive BTC Yield can coexist with a crashing share price, and a negative one with a rising stock if Bitcoin soars. Third, it does not capture the claims ahead of you. Preferred holders and bondholders get paid first, so your slice of Bitcoin per share is not the same as your slice of net value. Finally, a negative reading is not automatically a disaster. Management can accept a temporary dip to build a cash reserve or fund obligations.

When it breaks, the failure is a slow treadmill, not a sudden collapse. The video is explicit that the $75,451 average cost is not a margin-call line. It is a purchase price, no assets are pledged to secure the preferred, and the main debt is convertible bonds. The real pressure is the dividend bill. It works out to roughly 2.3% of the Bitcoin pile's value each year. Bitcoin must appreciate about that much annually just so the pile keeps pace with the cash flowing out. Below that, someone pays: common shareholders through dilution, the cash reserve through drawdown, or the Bitcoin pile through sales. If mNAV falls under 1 and the preferred trades below par, as STRC did near $89, all three doors jam together.

BTC Yield connects to several other ideas worth knowing. It is the flip side of mNAV, since the premium decides whether issuing stock helps or hurts it. It links to dilution, because every new share shrinks each existing share's claim unless the proceeds are worth more than the claim given up. It ties to preferred stock and fixed-dividend obligations, which are the fuel for the treadmill, and to mark-to-market accounting, which explains why profit and BTC Yield can disagree. Strategy also keeps a cash reserve of $4.79 billion, over three years of obligations, which buys time. Understand those four links, and you can talk about any Bitcoin treasury company, not just this one, with real confidence.
