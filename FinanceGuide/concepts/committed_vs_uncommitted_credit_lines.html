Committed vs. Uncommitted Credit Lines

A credit line is a pre-approved borrowing limit. You can draw on it when you need cash, repay it, and draw again, and you pay interest only on what you have borrowed. The critical question is whether the lender has promised to lend. With a committed credit line, the bank has signed a contract to provide the money up to the limit for a set term, usually one to five years. In exchange you pay a commitment fee on the unused portion, often 0.25% to 0.50% a year. With an uncommitted credit line, the bank has only said it is willing to lend. It can decline any request, reduce the limit, or cancel the line, often with little notice and no explanation. Committed means a promise. Uncommitted means a courtesy.

The distinction matters because a credit line is only useful in a crisis if it works in a crisis. Companies and households do not draw credit because they enjoy paying interest. They draw it to cover payroll when a customer pays late, to buy inventory before a busy season, or to survive a bad quarter. Those are exactly the moments when a bank is worried about its own balance sheet. An uncommitted line is a fair-weather friend: it is easy to get, cheap or free, and most available when you don't need it. A committed line costs more because the bank is selling you insurance. It has to hold capital and funding against a promise it can't walk away from. The real economy runs on this arrangement, which is why credit-line behavior shows up in hiring, investment, and layoffs.

Mechanically, a committed facility is a legal agreement with a fixed maximum, a maturity date, a pricing formula (typically a benchmark rate plus a spread), and a list of covenants. Covenants are conditions such as keeping a minimum debt-service coverage ratio or a maximum leverage ratio. If you stay inside them, the bank must fund every draw. If you breach one, that is an event of default, and the bank can refuse further draws, demand repayment, or force a renegotiation. An uncommitted line has none of this structure. The bank reviews it at its own discretion, often annually, and can cut it "at any time." Consumer lines such as credit cards and home equity lines sit in between. They are governed by consumer-protection rules that limit when a lender may freeze or reduce them, but the lender still holds considerable discretion.

Its argument is that banks carrying an estimated $325 billion of unrealized bond losses, with 10-year yields near 5.27%, must protect their capital ratios by shrinking their loan books. The presenter says this shows up as HELOC denials, mid-tier banks "lowering unused credit limits" and "demanding 40% cash to collateral" on small-business facilities, and credit card issuers trimming unused limits on prime borrowers. The logic is that an unused line still consumes bank resources, so it is the easiest thing to shrink. Treat the specific figures as the presenter's claims. The mechanism of a capital-constrained bank pulling back available credit is real, but the numbers were not independently checked here.

"Once a bank commences statutory balance sheet contraction, uncommitted and revolving lines are the first assets that unilaterally freeze or terminate with 30 days notice."

That quote is the basis for the video's step three: draw your lines now, park the cash in short-term Treasuries, and keep control of the money. The strategy reasons that a bank can't take back money you have already borrowed, only the availability of money you haven't. Once you have drawn, the balance becomes a term obligation, and the bank generally can't demand it back early unless you default or the agreement allows a call. Companies have done this at scale. In March 2020, many large corporations drew down billions from their revolving credit facilities in days. They wanted cash in hand before markets seized up, and they could do it because those facilities were committed. Precautionary borrowing is a real treasury practice, not a fringe idea.

Investors and traders watch credit-line behavior as a signal. Rising revolver draws across many companies suggest firms distrust future access to funding. Bank earnings calls that mention "reducing unfunded commitments" or "tightening exposure" point to a lender conserving capital. The Federal Reserve's Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey shows whether banks are tightening standards and what share of firms report harder access. Equity analysts read the ratio of unused commitments to loans, and credit analysts read the covenant language in filings. A company that depends on an uncommitted line, or on a committed line expiring within 12 months, carries refinancing risk that a balance sheet with plenty of cash and long-dated debt doesn't. That is what the video means by looking for firms that self-fund their working capital.

The gotchas are large, and the video glosses over several. First, its wording blurs the categories. Committed revolving lines are specifically the ones that can't be cut on 30 days notice while you are in compliance. The lines that can vanish are the uncommitted ones, and a bank's power over a committed line comes through covenant breaches and its renewal date. Second, drawing is not free. If you borrow on a HELOC at 8% and earn 4% in T-bills, you lose about 4% a year on the balance for the privilege of holding cash. Third, drawing has behavioral and credit costs: it raises your utilization and debt load, and spending "just a little" of a buffer is easy. Fourth, consumer rules matter. A HELOC lender generally can't freeze or cut a line at will. Under federal rules it typically needs a significant drop in home value, a material change in your finances, or a default, and the contract may spell out more.

Consider a practical example. A small manufacturer has a $2 million facility. In one version it is committed, three years, with covenants it meets comfortably. In the other it is uncommitted and reviewed annually. A bank that is short on capital can't legally refuse to fund the first company's draw, but it can decline to renew in three years and can tighten terms on any breach. It can freeze the second company's line at the next review or sooner. If the owner expects a squeeze, the rational moves are to negotiate the uncommitted line into a committed one, pay the commitment fee, and possibly draw part of it. Drawing everything makes sense only if the firm truly expects to need the cash, because it will pay interest on money that sits idle.

When it breaks, it breaks in a few recognizable ways. A borrower who breaches a covenant can lose a committed line just when they need it, so covenant headroom matters as much as the size of the limit. A bank in real distress can fail outright, and while regulators usually arrange a sale to another bank, the borrower's line may be frozen or replaced with new terms in the process. During the 2008 crisis, many businesses that assumed their lines were reliable found them reduced or canceled. Drawing on a line when the market senses distress can also send a signal to lenders and rating agencies that you are worried. Finally, cash you draw and park at another institution has its own risks: hold it in Treasuries, not in the same bank you owe.

Committed versus uncommitted lines connect to several other ideas from the video. They tie into counterparty risk, which is step one's warning about keeping more than the $250,000 FDIC limit at one regional bank. They tie into capital ratios and risk-weighted assets, because unused commitments count toward a bank's capital calculations, which explains why a stressed bank cuts them first. They tie into liquidity vs. solvency, since having cash on hand is a different problem from having a strong balance sheet. And they tie into negative carry, the cost of borrowing at a high rate to hold assets earning less. A useful rule is this: know exactly which of your credit lines are promises and which are courtesies, read the terms, and plan as if the courtesies may not be there when you need them.
