MOVE Index

The MOVE Index (Merrill Lynch Option Volatility Estimate, now published by ICE BofA) is often called "the VIX for bonds." It measures how much traders expect U.S. Treasury yields to swing over the next month. It is built from prices of options on Treasury securities of several maturities, weighted toward the 2-, 5-, 10- and 30-year points. The number is quoted in basis points of annualized yield volatility. A MOVE of 100 means the options market is pricing yields to move about one percentage point per year, one standard deviation. Higher readings mean traders are paying more for protection against big rate moves. Lower readings mean they expect calm.

It matters because Treasury yields are the base price of money for the whole economy. Mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs, bank lending standards and the discount rate used to value stocks all key off them. When yield volatility rises, the people who make markets in bonds, mainly primary dealers, face a harder time pricing risk. They widen their bid-ask spreads, hold less inventory and charge more to take the other side of trades. Those costs flow downstream to borrowers. A high MOVE therefore signals more than nervous traders. It can mean the pipes that carry credit through the system are becoming more expensive and less reliable.

Mechanically, the MOVE Index starts with implied volatility. An option's price depends partly on how far the underlying asset is expected to move. If you know the option's price, the strike, the time left and the current yield, you can solve backward for the volatility the market is assuming. The MOVE takes the one-month implied volatilities from Treasury options across the maturity curve and combines them with fixed weights into one number. Because it is a yield volatility, it is expressed in basis points, not percent of price. A reading of 100 divides into roughly 29 basis points of one-standard-deviation movement per month (100 divided by the square root of 12) and about 6 basis points per day.

The video gives a concrete case. The MOVE rose from 75.3 to 101.8 in a month, a gain of about 35%, while the 10-year yield sat near 5.26% and the 30-year near 5.59%. The speaker said a one-month jump that large into triple digits had happened only eight times since 2008. Meanwhile the VIX sat near 16, which implies stock moves of about 1% a day. So bond traders were paying up for protection against sharp yield swings while stock traders were paying little for protection against sharp equity swings. The speaker framed the gap as a divergence that had to close.

"One of those two markets is wrong, my friends. And as I said before, the bond market is virtually never wrong on cost of capital."

Traders use the MOVE in several ways. Rates traders treat it as the price of options, so a high reading means hedging is expensive and selling volatility pays more. Portfolio managers use it as a stress gauge. A sustained climb above 100 often coincides with funding strain, forced selling, or worries about the Federal Reserve or Treasury supply. Cross-asset investors compare it to the VIX, because equity valuations depend on discount rates. If rates are expected to swing wildly, the present value of future earnings is harder to pin down. Some also watch the ratio of MOVE to VIX. A high ratio says bonds are more anxious than stocks, and it is a prompt to ask why.

The nuances matter. First, the MOVE measures uncertainty about yield changes, not direction. It can spike when yields surge or when they collapse. Second, it is a coincident gauge as much as a forecast. It often spikes during a shock instead of ahead of one. Third, the claim that stocks always catch up is a tendency, not a law. The video's "eight times" count is a small sample, and the speaker admitted "not always, but usually." Fourth, the level should be read against its own history. Readings near 50 to 60 in 2021 were extremely calm. About 100 is elevated but far below the 2008 peak of roughly 260, or the 2020 and 2023 stress readings in the 160 to 200 range.

The video also draws a straight line from the MOVE to mortgage rates, and that link needs care. Higher bond volatility does widen the spread between mortgage rates and Treasury yields, because mortgage-backed securities contain a prepayment option that becomes costlier to hedge when rates are jumpy. That relationship is real. But the numbers quoted do not fit the claim neatly. A 7.405% mortgage rate over a 5.26% 10-year yield is a spread of about 215 basis points, not "above 260." Inflation, Fed policy, Treasury supply and investor demand for mortgage bonds also drive mortgage rates. Volatility is one input among several, not the single explanation.

When the MOVE signals trouble, the failure modes are about liquidity and forced selling. In March 2020, Treasuries, normally the safest and most liquid asset, briefly became hard to trade as investors sold everything for cash. Dealers could not absorb the flow, spreads blew out, and the Fed stepped in to buy. In March 2023, after Silicon Valley Bank failed, the MOVE reached roughly 198 as the market repriced Fed expectations almost overnight. Leveraged investors are the most exposed. Funds that borrow to hold Treasuries face margin calls when volatility rises, because lenders demand more collateral. That can force selling that deepens the very volatility that caused it.

The MOVE connects to several other ideas in this guide. It is tied to the basis trade and swap spreads the video discusses, since hedge funds running leveraged Treasury positions are sensitive to volatility and margin requirements. It links to duration risk: bond funds with long average maturities lose the most when yields jump, which is why the speaker warns about long-term bond funds. It also connects to the VIX, the equity counterpart, and to the term premium, the extra yield investors demand for holding long bonds. Reading the MOVE alongside these gives a more complete view of whether stress is real, spreading, or already priced in.

To discuss the MOVE confidently, hold these points. It is the bond market's implied volatility gauge, in basis points of yield. Roughly 100 is elevated, below 80 is calm, and above 150 signals real stress. It says how uncertain traders are, not which way yields will go. A gap between a high MOVE and a low VIX is a reason to look harder at risk in stocks, but history shows only a tendency for equities to follow, not a guarantee. The video's three-step playbook (audit stock holdings for interest-rate sensitivity, be cautious with long-duration bond funds, hold short Treasury bills) is one reasonable response to that tendency. It is a judgment call, not the only conclusion the data supports.
