Time-of-Use Pricing

Time-of-use pricing (usually shortened to TOU) is an electricity rate design where the price per kilowatt-hour changes depending on when you use the power, not just how much you use. Under a traditional flat-rate tariff, a kilowatt-hour at 3 a.m. costs the same as one at 6 p.m. Under TOU, the day is cut into windows, typically off-peak, mid-peak, and peak, and each window has its own price. A tariff is simply the official rate schedule a utility files with its state regulator. The idea is that electricity is not one product. Power at a moment of scarcity is a different thing from power when the grid is idle.

It matters because your electric bill is one of the few large household expenses that you can change by changing behavior rather than by earning more or buying less. The video's central claim is that utility bills are rising even as usage falls, and TOU is one mechanism by which that can happen. If your habits are fixed by work, school, and kids, a rate plan that charges the most during your dinner hour can raise your bill without a single extra appliance. In the wider economy, TOU is how utilities try to flatten demand, which reduces how much expensive generation and wires they must build, and that decision flows into the rate base.

Mechanically, it starts with the smart meter, a two-way digital meter that records usage in short intervals, usually every 15 minutes to an hour, instead of one monthly total. An old analog meter could not support TOU because it only knew the cumulative number. With interval data, the utility sorts each hour of your usage into a pricing window, multiplies each bucket by its rate, and adds them up. Many plans also add critical peak pricing, where on a handful of extreme days the utility announces an even higher rate for a few hours. Your bill is the sum of every bucket, so one habit, like running the dryer at 6 p.m., can matter a lot.

The video gives concrete numbers. It says utilities are installing smart meters and enrolling customers by default into time-based plans, with power used between roughly 4:00 p.m. and 9:00 p.m. charged at triple or quadruple the baseline rate. Take an illustrative baseline of 12 cents per kilowatt-hour. A 4x peak would be 48 cents. A family using 10 kWh during those five hours on a typical weekday pays $4.80 at peak, versus $1.20 on a flat rate for that same slice. Over about 22 weekdays that is roughly $79 versus $26 for that slice of usage alone. These figures are an illustration of the arithmetic, not a quote from any particular utility.

"If your family is unavoidably home during evening hours, cooking dinner and living a normal life, demand a traditional flat rate residential tariff schedule."

That advice is the practical core. The question to ask is how much of your usage lands in the peak window. A household where everyone is away until 6 p.m. and which can run laundry and the dishwasher overnight may come out ahead on TOU, because the off-peak rate is usually lower than the flat rate. A household that is home all afternoon and evening has the opposite profile. Investors and analysts read the same signal at the system level. When a utility pushes TOU, critical peak pricing, or demand charges, it is telling you that peak capacity is tight, and that is the same pressure behind the video's story about data centers and the grid.

There are several gotchas. First, TOU is not automatically a trap. It is not a hidden fee, and for some households it saves real money. The video's framing, that the plans exist to charge you "peak commercial rates," is a strong claim. The more neutral view is that peak prices are meant to reflect real scarcity, and that the plans are designed so that shifting use pays off. Second, "default enrollment" differs by state and utility. Some make TOU the default for new customers, some require you to opt in, and some give a trial period or a bill-protection guarantee. Check your own utility's rules rather than assuming.

Third, the peak window is not the same everywhere. The video uses 4 p.m. to 9 p.m. as an example, but real windows vary by region and season, and many utilities have moved their peak later in the day, to 5-8 p.m. or later, as solar power has changed when the grid is stressed. Fourth, TOU only changes the energy portion of the bill in many cases. Fixed customer charges, delivery fees, and riders, the line items the video tells you to read on page two, may stay the same. So even a perfect shift of usage does not erase a bill that is rising for other reasons. And TOU does not fix the rate-base problem the video describes; it only changes how you are billed inside it.

When TOU goes wrong, it is usually because the household does not match the design. Families with young children, people who work from home, elderly residents who need cooling or heating during the day, and people who rely on medical equipment cannot easily shift. For them, the plan acts as a penalty rather than an incentive, and a hot summer evening with air conditioning at full blast can produce a shock bill. Critical peak events make this worse, since the notice may be short. The risk is higher if you never look at your interval data. The fix is simple: use your utility's online portal to compare a month of actual usage under both rate plans, and switch if flat wins. Many utilities allow a switch, though some limit how often.

TOU connects to several other ideas in this guide. It sits inside the rate base and cost-of-service framework the video describes: regulators approve a tariff designed to recover the utility's costs plus an allowed return, and TOU is one way of dividing that recovery among customers by when they use power. It relates to the capacity auctions in wholesale markets like PJM, since peak pricing at the retail level is a downstream echo of scarce capacity upstream. And it connects to cost causation, the principle that whoever drives the cost should pay it. The practical takeaway is to read your rate schedule, check your usage by hour, and choose the plan that fits how your household actually lives.
