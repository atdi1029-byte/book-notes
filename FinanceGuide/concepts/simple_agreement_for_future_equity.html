SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity)

A SAFE, short for Simple Agreement for Future Equity, is a contract in which an investor hands a company cash today in exchange for a promise of shares later. It is not a loan, so there is no interest rate and no maturity date. It is not stock either, so the investor has no vote, no dividend, and no seat at the table yet. It sits in between. The investor holds a right to receive equity when a defined trigger happens, usually a priced funding round, an acquisition, or an IPO. Y Combinator created the standard form in 2013 so that early startups could raise money quickly without weeks of legal negotiation over the company's valuation.

SAFEs matter because they are how a huge share of the modern startup economy gets funded before anyone can agree on what the company is worth. A two-person team with a prototype cannot credibly say whether it is worth $4 million or $14 million, and arguing about it burns time and legal fees. A SAFE postpones the argument. The founder gets cash now, the investor gets a claim on future ownership, and the pricing question is answered later by a larger investor with better information. That is also why SAFEs show up in the Anthropic filing: a company that raised money early and then became enormously valuable owes those early backers a very large amount of stock.

Mechanically, a SAFE works in a few steps. First, the investor wires, say, $1 million and signs the agreement. Second, the contract sets the terms that decide how many shares that money will eventually buy: usually a valuation cap (the maximum company valuation at which the SAFE converts), a discount (often 20%) off the next round's share price, or both. Third, nothing happens until a trigger event. Fourth, when a priced round or IPO occurs, the SAFE converts, and the investor receives whichever deal is better: the capped price or the discounted price. Early believers are rewarded for taking risk when the company was unproven, and the reward is calculated automatically from the contract.

Here is a worked example. An investor puts $1 million into a SAFE with a $10 million valuation cap. Two years later the company raises a priced round at a $50 million valuation. Because of the cap, the SAFE converts as though the company were worth only $10 million, so the investor's $1 million buys roughly 10% of the pre-round company, not the 2% it would buy at $50 million. The investor now holds stock worth about five times what was paid. The company did not spend a dollar of cash to deliver that gain; it paid in ownership. This is the same logic as the restaurant story in the video, where the brother-in-law's slice becomes far more valuable than the cash he put in.

Now connect this to the Anthropic loss. The video points out that the $72.4 billion charge came from the change in fair value of convertible notes, derivative liabilities, and SAFEs. Under US accounting rules, many SAFEs are recorded as liabilities, not equity, because the company may have to hand over a variable number of shares. Each reporting period the company must re-measure that liability at fair value. When the company's value rises, the stock owed to SAFE holders is worth more, so the liability grows, and the increase flows through the income statement as a loss. No cash left the building. The video's summary is blunt:

"The better Anthropic did, the bigger the loss it had to report."

Investors and analysts use SAFEs in two ways. As a founder or early backer, you compare caps and discounts to understand who is getting the better deal and how much dilution you are accepting. As a public-market investor reading a prospectus, you learn to separate noise from signal. The video's lesson is to look at the operating loss, which was $693 million, rather than the $80.7 billion net loss. You also read the share count section: when SAFEs and notes convert at the IPO, the fair-value charge stops recurring, and the real cost shows up as new shares. Watch the pro forma share count and the percentage of the company already claimed.

There are several gotchas. The biggest misconception is that a SAFE is debt. It has no repayment schedule and no interest, so the company cannot default on it in the usual sense. A second is that a large fair-value loss means the business is failing; as the video shows, it usually means the opposite. A third is that SAFEs are harmless to founders. Stack several uncapped or low-cap SAFEs and the founders can discover at the priced round that they have given away far more ownership than they expected, because every SAFE converts at once. Different versions also exist (pre-money and post-money), and they calculate dilution differently, so the exact wording matters.

SAFEs can also go wrong. If the company never raises a priced round and never gets acquired or listed, the SAFE may sit indefinitely, leaving the investor with an unresolved claim and no liquidity. If the company is sold for less than the money invested, SAFE holders are generally paid ahead of common stockholders but behind actual creditors, and they may recover only part of their cash. If the company fails, the claim is usually worth nothing. For the company, a surge in value can create large non-cash losses, as seen here, which can confuse headline readers and distort per-share figures such as the $39.88 loss per share the video cites.

SAFEs connect to several other ideas. Convertible notes are the closest relative: they also convert into equity, but they are real debt with interest and a maturity date. Derivative liabilities are the accounting category that captures instruments whose value depends on something else, here the company's own share price. Fair-value accounting is what turns a rising valuation into a reported loss. Dilution and the cap table show who ends up owning what, and dual-class share structures explain why the converted shares may carry no vote. Together they explain the video's final point: the loss is a receipt for value already promised to early money before public buyers arrive.
