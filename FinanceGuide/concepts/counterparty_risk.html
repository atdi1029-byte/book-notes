Counterparty Risk

Counterparty risk is the chance that the other side of a financial arrangement fails to do what it promised. Every time you hand money to someone in exchange for a promise, you take on this risk. When you deposit cash at a bank, the bank promises to give it back on demand. When you buy a bond, the issuer promises interest and principal. When you trade options, the exchange or broker promises to honor the contract. In each case your outcome depends not only on the asset itself but on whether the party holding the promise stays solvent and willing to pay. It is the risk that lives in the relationship, not in the market price.

It matters because most people never see it. A bank account feels like a vault, with a balance on a screen and a familiar logo on the branch. But legally, an uninsured deposit is a loan from you to the bank. You become an unsecured creditor, standing in line behind others if the bank fails. Counterparty risk decides whether your payroll clears, whether your emergency fund is reachable, and whether your brokerage cash is truly yours. It also drives system-wide events. When many people doubt the same counterparty at once, they all try to leave together, and doubt becomes the thing that causes the failure.

Mechanically, a bank funds itself with deposits and lends or invests them in longer-term assets. Depositors can leave in a day, while the assets take years to mature. That mismatch works only while confidence holds. If depositors withdraw, the bank must raise cash by selling assets, borrowing, or both. If its assets are worth less than their stated value, selling them crystallizes losses that wipe out its equity cushion. Once equity is gone, the remaining depositors are funded by nothing but hope. The first people out are paid in full, and the last may take a haircut. That order of exit is why speed matters in a panic.

The video builds its case on a specific example. It cites FDIC data showing unrealized losses on bank securities rose $19 billion in one quarter to $325.1 billion, because yields rose and bond prices fell. The presenter's "frozen ice box" analogy makes it concrete. A restaurateur buys $100,000 of Wagyu beef, and it could now fetch only $20,000 on the sidewalk. He padlocks the freezer, labels it "held to maturity," and reports the meat at full value as long as the door stays shut. Banks do the same with low-coupon Treasuries and mortgage-backed securities, carrying them at cost rather than market value.

"If you have money deposited in an institution that can't liquidate its own assets without going insolvent, you don't have a liquid account. You have an unsecured loan to a trapped balance sheet."

The video then lays out a three-stage sequence for how the trap could spring. First, banks paying 0.2% to 0.4% on checking while T-bills yield over 4% see sophisticated depositors and corporate treasurers sweep cash out. Second, to replace those deposits the bank cannot easily sell its held-to-maturity bonds, because selling even a slice can force the whole portfolio to be marked to market and could erase regulatory capital. So it borrows from the Federal Home Loan Banks or the Fed funds market at 4% or more. Third, that costly funding squeezes earnings, tightens lending, and strains the balance sheet further. Note that this is the presenter's thesis, not a certainty.

Investors manage counterparty risk by asking three questions. Who exactly owes me this money? What do they own that backs the promise? What happens if they fail? The video's practical answer has layers. Keep balances under the $250,000 FDIC limit per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. For larger sums, use deposit placement networks that split cash into sub-limit slices across many banks. Better still, hold Treasury bills directly through TreasuryDirect or a reputable custodian. A T-bill is a claim on the US government itself, so there is no bank between you and the borrower. The video notes you earn over 4% while removing bank counterparty risk.

"When you hold a one-month or a three-month US Treasury bill directly, you have zero bank counterparty risk."

There are gotchas. First, "zero" means zero bank counterparty risk, not zero risk. You still face the US government as counterparty, plus interest rate risk if you sell a longer bill early, and inflation risk on the real value of the money. Second, custody matters. A T-bill fund or a bill held at a brokerage adds that firm as a link in the chain, though properly segregated securities are generally protected from the firm's creditors. Third, FDIC insurance is real and powerful, so insured balances are not at risk in the way the video's dramatic framing can imply. Fourth, unrealized losses are not realized losses. Many banks can hold to maturity and never take them, so $325 billion is a measure of vulnerability, not proof of insolvency.

When counterparty risk breaks, it breaks fast and unevenly. The video points to the 2023 bank failures as the template, where uninsured depositors rushed out because deposits far above the insurance cap made them the most alert and most exposed. Insured depositors are typically made whole, though access can be delayed. Uninsured depositors may be rescued by policy decisions, but you cannot plan on that, since a rescue is a choice and not a contract. Businesses face the sharpest danger: cash sitting in one bank on payroll day can freeze operations. The lesson is to treat the safety of a deposit as something you verify and structure, not something you assume.

Counterparty risk connects to several other ideas. Duration risk is the price sensitivity of bonds to rising yields, and it is the source of the banks' unrealized losses. Liquidity risk is the inability to turn assets into cash without a loss, which is what the padlocked freezer illustrates. Maturity transformation and fractional reserve banking explain why a bank is fragile by design. It also relates to position sizing, since the right response is rarely all-or-nothing. Spread cash across insured limits, hold bills for the excess, and size any exposure to a single institution so its failure would be an inconvenience rather than a catastrophe. The video also urges checking equity holdings for hidden regional-bank exposure, which is counterparty risk arriving through your portfolio.
