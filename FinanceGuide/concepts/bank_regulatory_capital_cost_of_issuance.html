Bank Hybrid Capital Issuance and Order Book Dynamics

A bank's regulatory capital is the cushion that absorbs losses before depositors or senior creditors are hurt. The thickest and most expensive layer is common equity. Just beneath it sits Additional Tier 1 (AT1) debt, often called CoCo bonds (contingent convertibles). An AT1 is a bond that behaves like a preferred share. It pays a coupon, has no real maturity date, and the bank can call it after roughly five years. The bank can also skip coupons. If its capital falls below a trigger, or regulators judge it non-viable, the bond can be converted into shares or written down to zero. The video's image is an airbag: it sits between senior bondholders and common equity, and it is built to be sacrificed.

This matters because AT1 is how banks raise loss-absorbing capital without issuing new common shares and diluting existing owners. A bank that must hold a certain percentage of its assets as capital can only lend as much as that capital supports. So the price of AT1 feeds directly into what a loan costs and whether the loan gets made. When AT1 is cheap, banks can grow their balance sheets and still pay dividends and buy back stock. When it gets expensive, management has to pick between lending less, retaining more earnings, or issuing equity. That is why a bond market most people never look at can change credit conditions for ordinary borrowers, and why the video treats it as a stress gauge.

Here is the mechanism. A bank decides it needs capital and hires underwriting banks, called bookrunners. They sound out big institutional investors and announce initial price thoughts, meaning a coupon or a spread over a benchmark rate, such as "mid-seven percent area." Investors then submit orders in the order book. As demand shows up, bookrunners tighten the price, a process called guidance. A deal with many times more orders than bonds can be priced lower in yield. The book closes, the bonds are allocated and the deal prices. The bonds then trade in the secondary market, where the price relative to the 100-cent issue price tells everyone, immediately, whether the deal was priced too generously or too stingily.

Banco BPM, the Italian lender, is the video's first case. It marketed a €400 million AT1 and early on had about €1.5 billion of orders, nearly four times the amount it wanted. That looks like a strong deal. But as the marketing continued, investors saw the price tightening and stopped bidding. The video says roughly half the book vanished. If it was exactly half, about €750 million of orders remained, still about 1.9 times the deal size. That would normally be healthy. The point is the direction. A book that shrinks while the price is being pushed tighter means demand was conditional on a price investors would no longer accept. The bank then had to make concessions, meaning a higher coupon, to hold the remaining buyers.

"Buyers balked, and when they balk, they walk."

The second case is Belfius, which issued a fresh AT1 that quickly dropped to about 99 cents on the euro. Be careful with this one. A one-point drop on a new issue is mild. Plenty of new bonds trade slightly under par for a while because the bookrunners stabilize the price and investors sell to take profits. On its own it proves little. The video reads it as a sign of buyers refusing tight spreads, and that fits the other evidence, but the stronger signals were the Bank of New York Mellon concession of about 32 basis points over its summer low and the Bloomberg index widening from below 200 to 253 basis points. The Belfius price move is a supporting detail, not proof.

Investors read these signals in a fairly practical way. Four things are worth watching. First, how big the book is compared with the deal size, called coverage. Second, how far the price moved from initial thoughts to final pricing. A wide move signals reluctance. Third, how the bond trades in the first days. Fourth, the spread of the broad AT1 index, such as the Bloomberg index in the video, against its own history. Fund managers also look at the new issue concession, the extra yield a new bond must offer over a similar bond already trading. When concessions grow across several issuers at the same time, it usually means the whole market has repriced risk, not that one bank has a problem.

Several nuances matter. First, a tight spread is not wrong by definition. The video notes that spreads under 200 basis points were a record low, but that only tells you the price was unusual, not what comes next. Second, AT1 investors are not only afraid of losses. They also worry about extension risk and reset risk. A bank may choose not to call the bond on its first call date, leaving the investor holding a perpetual security. The coupon then resets, often to a level that may not match the market, and the investor must wait for the next call date. Third, "the order book halved" does not mean the deal failed. And a falling 10-year Treasury yield is not automatically good news. The cause of the move is what counts.

What if it breaks? The worst case is the one the video describes from March 2023. When Swiss authorities forced Credit Suisse into a takeover by UBS, about $17 billion of its AT1 bonds were written down to zero, even though shareholders received some value. That upended the usual assumption that equity gets wiped out first. After that, European regulators said they would still follow the traditional order of losses, but investors demanded and got higher yields. A less extreme failure is a failed or pulled deal, when a bank cannot raise capital at a sensible price and has to postpone. If many issuers do that together, the market has closed for new AT1, and weaker banks may be forced to cut lending to protect capital ratios.

The last step is linking this to the rest of the market, as the video does. AT1 spreads belong with other measures of stress: credit spreads, sovereign yields, and bank net interest margins. When a bank pays more for hybrid capital, its cost of capital rises. That squeezes profits and can limit buybacks and dividend increases, which is the video's warning for anyone holding bank stocks or financial-sector ETFs. In a flight to safety, investors sell risky European paper and buy short-dated U.S. Treasuries. That pushes Treasury yields down, in this case the 10-year toward 4.96%, even though the economy has not improved. A reader who can explain that chain, from an order book in Milan to a Treasury yield in New York, understands why this niche market matters.
