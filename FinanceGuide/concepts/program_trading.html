Program Trading (Rules-Based Automated Trading)

Program trading means buying and selling securities by following rules written in advance, with a computer carrying out the orders. A person decides the rule once, and the machine applies it every day without opinions, hunches, or nerves. The rule might say "hold more when prices have been rising" or "cut exposure when the market gets bumpy." Exchanges also use the term for large baskets of stocks traded together in one order. The broader idea is what matters here: the trade is a mechanical consequence of inputs such as price and volatility. It is not a judgment about whether a company or an economy is healthy.

This matters because a mechanical trade looks exactly like any other trade on the screen. A price falls, and from the outside you cannot tell whether a thoughtful investor lost faith in the economy or a model simply hit a threshold. The presenter's warning is that investors often read mechanical moves as a verdict, then make emotional decisions about their 401(k) based on it. Program trades can also be enormous. When many funds follow similar rules, they lean the same way at the same moment, which adds supply or demand that has nothing to do with earnings, jobs, or inflation. Knowing who is on the other side of a trade is part of understanding the market.

Mechanically, a program trade has three parts: inputs, a rule, and an order. The inputs are numbers the machine can measure, usually price trends and volatility. The rule converts those numbers into a target position, such as "own this much of the S&P 500." The order is the difference between the target and what the fund holds now. If the target is smaller, the fund sells. The machine repeats this every day, and nobody has to feel brave or scared for it to happen. The video's trend-following funds ask two questions daily: which way is the trend, and how bumpy is the ride? The answers set how much stock they hold.

The video's main example is the group of funds called CTAs (commodity trading advisors), which are computer-run trend followers. Goldman Sachs's futures desk estimated they would sell about $31.2 billion of S&P 500 futures in a flat week, about $31.5 billion in a big drop, and still about $23.1 billion in a big rally. Selling into a rally looks absurd until you see the cause. The S&P 500 had just fallen 4.4% in a single day, and that day stays in the models' look-back windows for weeks. It keeps the volatility reading high even after a 2.35% bounce, so the rules still say to hold less stock.

"It isn't betting against you. It's shrinking to fit a bumpier road."

The presenter's driver analogy explains the logic behind volatility targeting. A driver who wants the same amount of risk on every road goes 70 mph on a dry highway and 35 in a storm, and has not changed his mind about the destination. Trend followers size positions against volatility in the same way. When the ride gets twice as bumpy, the position gets roughly cut in half. Nobody got frightened. The arithmetic of "constant risk" demands it. This is why a quiet, calm market can make these funds buyers, and a jumpy market, even a rising one, can keep them sellers. In the video's phrasing, the machine is matching its speed to the weather.

Investors can use this knowledge without trading it directly. The presenter's practical advice is to watch the inputs that drive the machines. First, volatility: he notes the VIX fell to about 19.9 after the rally, and if it stays calm the sizing math shrinks the selling, while another spike grows it. Second, the November 20th options expiration, when a lot of dealer positioning rolls off. Third, Goldman's next weekly update, to see whether the flat-market number shrinks. Fourth, whether money keeps flowing to Europe and Hong Kong while the S&P 500 sits out. Watching these helps you separate flow-driven moves from news-driven ones before you react.

There are several gotchas. The viral claim that these funds are "legally forced to sell" is wrong: nobody signed a contract to sell $31 billion. The figure is Goldman's estimate of what the funds' own rules would probably do, and the presenter compares it to a weather forecast, not a court order. Rules get tweaked, estimates miss, and every firm runs a slightly different model. Estimates can also swing quickly. On November 3rd, Goldman had trend followers as small buyers of about $1.3 billion of global stocks in a flat week. Seven days later the flat case was $23.3 billion of global selling, because one violent session changed the inputs.

Scale is another thing people get wrong. A number with a "B" on it sounds terrifying, but CME Group says E-mini S&P 500 futures trade nearly $500 billion of notional value a day, roughly $2.5 trillion in a five-day week. Against that, $31 billion is about 1.2% of normal volume: a headwind, not a tidal wave. The "dumping America" story is also only half true. In the flat case the S&P 500 shows $31.2 billion of selling, but the global total is only $23.3 billion, so the same machines are net buyers of about $7.9 billion elsewhere. France's CAC 40 gets about $4.8 billion, Hong Kong's Hang Seng about $3.4 billion, the Euro Stoxx 50 about $3 billion, and Germany's DAX about $2.2 billion. Each market is judged on its own chart. The timeframe matters too. If stocks rallied two standard deviations over a month, the same rules would flip to buying $14.5 billion of S&P 500 futures and $74 billion worldwide.

Program trading becomes dangerous when many participants follow similar rules and all fire at once. The video's historical case is portfolio insurance in 1987. This was a different strategy from today's trend following, but it was also a program trade: rules written in advance told it to sell as prices fell. On October 19, 1987, three portfolio insurers sold just under $2 billion of stock and futures, the S&P 500 fell about 20%, and SEC staff later estimated at least $55 billion of stock was covered by such insurance. The Brady Commission blamed part of the damage on this mechanical selling. The presenter is careful: he is not predicting a repeat, and today's market is far bigger and has circuit breakers. The narrow lesson is that crowded rules lean the same way at the same time. The scarier case in the video is a monthly drop of 9 to 10%, where Goldman's model has these funds selling $45.4 billion of S&P 500 futures and $149.5 billion of stock futures worldwide. Respect it, he says, not because it is likely, but because of how it would arrive.

Program trading connects to several other ideas in the video. Trend following and volatility targeting are the specific rules behind the CTA numbers. Gamma is a separate mechanism: options dealers who are short gamma hedge by selling as prices fall and buying as prices rise, which can magnify any move, including one that CTAs started. SpotGamma estimated on November 5th that dealers sat in negative gamma from roughly 7,000 to 7,550 on the S&P. The presenter corrects a common error here: CTA selling does not force dealers to short stock, because dealers hedge their own options books. He also notes that a seller who has already sold a lot has less left to sell, since Goldman put CTA stock positioning near $49 billion globally, below its average since 2017. Before you treat a drop as a verdict, ask who the seller is and what rule is driving them. Sometimes the answer is just a model still digesting last Thursday.
