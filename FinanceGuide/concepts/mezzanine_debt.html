Mezzanine Debt

Mezzanine debt is a layer of borrowing that sits in the middle of a company's capital structure: below the senior loans and above the owners' equity. The word comes from architecture. A mezzanine is the half-floor between the ground floor and the second floor, and the debt gets its name because it lives in the same in-between space. If a company goes bust and its assets are sold, the senior lenders are paid first. Mezzanine lenders are paid next, and only if money is left over. Shareholders get whatever remains after that, which is usually nothing. Because mezzanine lenders stand behind the senior lenders in line, they charge much more for lending, often 12% to 20% a year once every fee and sweetener is counted.

Mezzanine debt matters because it fills a gap that neither banks nor stock investors want to fill. A bank will lend against solid collateral up to a point, perhaps 50% or 60% of an asset's value. A growing company often needs more than that, but it does not want to sell a big slice of ownership to venture investors. Mezzanine debt fills the gap. It lets a business borrow more than a bank allows without giving up as much control. For lenders, mezzanine debt is a way to earn equity-like returns while still holding a contract that demands repayment. Today, large private credit funds are some of the biggest mezzanine buyers. They use it to hit the high payouts they promise to their own investors.

Mechanically, picture a company with a total capital stack built from the bottom up. At the bottom sits senior secured debt, backed by specific collateral such as equipment, buildings, or receivables. Above it sits the mezzanine tranche, which is usually subordinated. That means the borrower has signed a legal agreement that the senior lender must be paid in full first. Mezzanine loans normally pay a cash coupon, say 8% to 10%, plus a second piece of return. That second piece might be PIK interest (payment-in-kind, where interest is added to the loan balance instead of paid in cash) or warrants, which are options to buy company stock cheaply. Maturity is usually five to eight years, often with a single large repayment at the end.

The video gives a concrete picture of where this sits in today's market. It describes a roughly $50 billion market in GPU-backed debt that began with a $2.3 billion private credit facility from Blackstone to the cloud operator CoreWeave in 2023. In that structure, senior secured term loans are backed by racks of graphics chips. Mezzanine debt can be layered on top of that to push a borrower's total leverage higher. The video says private credit funds reach their 9% to 11% distributions partly by writing senior secured and mezzanine debt to "high-growth tech companies pledging hardware collateral." Borrowers in this corner of the market pay around 12% to 14%, according to the video. A fund holding that paper collects those yields, passes part of them to its own investors as distributions, and keeps a fee.

Investors and lenders use mezzanine debt for several practical purposes. A private credit fund uses it to raise a portfolio's average yield above what senior loans alone can deliver. A company's owners use it to finance an acquisition, a buyout, or an expansion without issuing new shares. Traders and analysts watch it as a signal. When mezzanine spreads widen, meaning lenders demand more yield, it means the market is nervous about the riskiest layer of corporate borrowing. When mezzanine lending is booming and terms are loose, it suggests that capital is chasing yield. A smart investor reads a fund's schedule of investments to see how much of the portfolio sits in the junior layer, because the headline distribution rate says nothing about that on its own.

The main trap is mistaking a high yield for a high return. The 9% to 11% a fund advertises is a promised payout, not a guaranteed one, and mezzanine paper is exactly where the risk hides. Another misconception is that "secured" means "safe." Mezzanine debt is often unsecured or only secured by a second claim on collateral that the senior lender already controls. In the video's example, the collateral is a GPU that loses an estimated 50% to 70% of its economic usefulness in under three years, while the loan has a four- or five-year term. If the senior loan's loan-to-value ratio drifts from 50% or 60% toward 80% or 90%, the senior lender's cushion disappears. The mezzanine layer, sitting above it, is wiped out first. Its value is the value of what is left over after the senior claim, and that residual shrinks fast.

PIK is where the video places its warning. When a borrower is squeezed, it may elect to pay interest in kind, which means it adds the interest to the principal instead of sending cash. On paper, the lender still records income, and the fund can keep reporting its yield. But no cash arrives. The video says:

"If the hardware cracks in value and private borrowers are forced to lean on payment-in-kind, or what we call PIK notes, to avoid cash defaults, well, those private debt funds face severe liquidity strains."

The fund still owes cash to its own investors in the form of distributions and redemptions, so it has to find that cash elsewhere. A rising share of PIK income in a fund's reports is therefore a yellow flag. It can mean the borrowers are quietly failing to pay while the fund still shows a profit on paper.

When mezzanine debt fails, the damage follows the order of the stack. First, the borrower triggers a covenant breach, such as a falling collateral value or a missed debt service coverage ratio. The senior lender can demand fresh equity or extra collateral, and a cash-strapped operator often cannot supply either one. If the company is sold or liquidated, the senior lenders take their money first, and the mezzanine lender may recover only cents on the dollar. Meanwhile, investors in the fund cannot leave quickly. The video points out that private debt vehicles typically cap redemptions at about 5% of assets per quarter, the so-called gates, so "your money is effectively trapped" while the portfolio is repriced. Mezzanine losses therefore reach ordinary people through retirement accounts and wealth-management products that held the fund.

Mezzanine debt connects to several other ideas worth knowing. It links to loan-to-value ratios and maintenance covenants, which decide how quickly the senior layer feels pain. It links to business development companies (BDCs) and private credit funds, which are the main holders, and to the redemption gates that limit how fast investors can exit. It also echoes the vendor-financing boom of the 2000–2001 telecom crash, when lenders funded fiber builders who bought equipment from the same industry funding them. In each case, the lesson is the same. The higher a lender sits in the structure, the safer its claim, and the further down it sits, the more its return depends on the borrower's business still working, not just the collateral. A practical habit for any investor is to ask of any high-yield fund: which layer of the stack does it own, and what happens to that layer if the collateral loses value faster than the debt is repaid?
