Average Hourly Earnings (Wage Growth)

Average Hourly Earnings (AHE) is a monthly economic statistic published by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics as part of the Jobs Report (the Employment Situation Summary) released on the first Friday of each month. It measures the average amount that private-sector, non-farm employees earn per hour of work across the economy. The headline figure comes in two forms: the month-over-month change (how much wages grew compared to last month) and the year-over-year change (how much wages grew compared to the same month last year). In August of the video's example, AHE rose 0.3% month-over-month, bringing the average hourly wage to $37.75, representing a 3.1% increase compared to August of the prior year.

Why does this number matter so deeply? Because wages are simultaneously the largest cost for most businesses and the primary source of income for most households. When wages rise faster than inflation, workers have more real purchasing power — they can buy more goods and services. When wages rise faster than productivity gains, businesses face pressure to raise prices to protect their margins, which can fuel inflation. This is the crux of the tension: moderate wage growth is healthy, but wage growth that consistently outpaces productivity becomes an inflationary engine. That inflationary potential is precisely what makes the Federal Reserve watch AHE so closely — it directly feeds into their dual mandate of price stability and maximum employment.

The mechanical pathway from wage growth to the broader economy works like this: employers compete for workers, pushing wages up. Higher wages increase household income, which boosts consumer spending (since consumption is roughly 70% of U.S. GDP). Businesses facing both higher labor costs and stronger demand may raise prices. Rising prices reduce the purchasing power of money. The Fed, seeing inflation pressures build, considers raising interest rates to cool demand. Higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive — mortgages, car loans, business credit lines all get pricier. This chain reaction connects a monthly payroll statistic directly to what someone pays for a home or carries on their credit card. It's a feedback loop, and AHE is one of the earliest visible links in that chain.

The August report in the video illustrates the concept with real precision. The headline jobs number — 162,000 — grabbed all the attention. But the wage data told its own story. A 0.3% monthly gain in average hourly earnings is neither alarming nor comforting. Annualized, 0.3% per month implies roughly 3.6% annual wage growth. The actual year-over-year reading came in at 3.1%, which is above the Fed's informal comfort zone (closer to 2-2.5% in alignment with their 2% inflation target) but not so far above it that it signals a wage-price spiral. As the host put it, wages were:

"not hot enough to trigger alarm but not cool enough to give the Fed comfort to cut."

That ambiguity is itself a policy signal. The Fed needs wage growth to be clearly cooling before it can justify cutting rates. At 3.1% year-over-year, it cannot make that case confidently.

Traders and investors use AHE data as a real-time input into their interest rate expectations, which in turn drive bond prices, equity valuations, and currency movements. The practical workflow looks like this: when AHE comes in hotter than expected, bond traders sell Treasuries (pushing yields up), because they anticipate the Fed will need to keep rates higher for longer. When AHE comes in cooler than expected, they buy bonds in anticipation of eventual rate cuts. Equity investors watch the same signal: higher-for-longer rates compress the discount rate used to value future earnings, which pushes stock prices down — particularly for growth stocks whose value is concentrated far in the future. A single 0.1% surprise in AHE can move markets meaningfully on Jobs Report Friday.

There are several important nuances and common misconceptions to understand. First, AHE is a simple average, not a median — it can be skewed by changes in the composition of employment. If a jobs report adds disproportionately many low-wage workers (as the August report did, with 59,000 food service jobs and 42,000 local government education jobs), the average wage can actually be dragged down even if no individual worker got a pay cut. Conversely, mass layoffs of low-wage workers make average wages appear to rise even with no actual raises. This compositional effect means AHE can mislead if read in isolation. Second, the 3.1% year-over-year figure means nothing without comparing it to inflation — real wage growth is what matters for living standards, and nominal AHE growth below the CPI means workers are actually falling behind.

The concept can also break down or mislead in edge cases. During the COVID-19 pandemic, AHE spiked dramatically — not because workers were getting raises, but because millions of low-wage workers in hospitality and retail were laid off, mechanically raising the average of who remained employed. This was a statistical illusion, not economic prosperity. Similarly, when the economy shifts toward gig work, part-time employment, or contract arrangements, AHE may understates true wage pressure because these workers are sometimes excluded or their hours are miscounted. Wage theft (unpaid overtime, off-the-clock work) is another gap — it creates a gap between reported wages and what workers actually take home, making official AHE appear healthier than the lived reality on the ground.

AHE connects most directly to several adjacent concepts worth understanding together. The Employment Cost Index (ECI), released quarterly, is considered by many economists to be a more accurate wage measure because it controls for compositional shifts. The Atlanta Fed Wage Growth Tracker tracks wage changes for the same workers over time, avoiding the composition problem entirely. Together with the Consumer Price Index (CPI) and Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) deflator, AHE forms part of the Fed's inflationary picture. And it ties directly to Federal Funds Rate expectations, which ripple into mortgage rates, auto loan rates, and the cost of carrying any variable-rate debt. When you understand AHE, you understand the first domino in a chain that ends at your monthly payment.
