Residual Market / Insurer of Last Resort

A residual market is the insurance system's safety net for properties that no private insurer will voluntarily cover. When a home sits in a hurricane zone, a wildfire corridor, or any other high-risk location that private carriers have decided isn't worth the exposure, the homeowner still needs coverage to get or keep a mortgage. States step in and create a state-backed entity — often called an insurer of last resort — to write policies for these otherwise uninsurable homes. Florida's version is called Citizens Property Insurance Corporation. California's is the FAIR Plan (Fair Access to Insurance Requirements). These aren't charities or government giveaways — they charge premiums, often steep ones — but they exist specifically to absorb the risk that the private market has refused to touch.

This matters because the size of a state's residual market is one of the cleanest real-time gauges of how healthy the private insurance market actually is in that state. When private insurers are confident, profitable, and willing to write new policies, homeowners migrate out of the state pool and back into the private market, and the residual pool shrinks. When private insurers are fleeing, raising rates, or refusing to renew, homeowners get pushed into the state pool by default, and it grows. Because mortgage lenders require insurance and escrow that insurance cost monthly, the size and trajectory of these pools flow directly into what millions of homeowners pay every month — and into the broader story about whether an "insurance crisis" is really about risk, or about something else.

Mechanically, the process works like this: an insurer decides whether to write or renew a policy based on its own cost of capital, which importantly includes the price of reinsurance — insurance that insurance companies buy for themselves to protect their own balance sheet against a catastrophic loss year. When reinsurance is expensive, primary insurers become stingier: they raise rates, drop marginal policies, or pull out of a state entirely, and displaced homeowners land in the residual pool. When reinsurance gets cheaper, insurers become more willing to write new business, they re-enter shrinking markets, and homeowners flow back out of the state pool and into private coverage. Regulators also play a direct role — a state that approves aggressive rate increases and reforms that make the private market attractive will see its residual pool shrink; a state that keeps rates artificially low or maintains a regulatory environment insurers dislike will see the opposite, regardless of the weather that year.

The video lays out a real-world natural experiment between two states experiencing the same underlying catastrophe-cost environment but producing opposite outcomes. Florida's Citizens pool has shrunk 73% from its peak of 1.42 million policies down to roughly 385,000, and Florida's residual carrier actually filed a 2.6% rate decrease this year, while Louisiana cut 73,000 active homeowner policies by 7.5%, with regulators explicitly attributing the relief to falling reinsurance costs. Meanwhile California's FAIR Plan has grown 157% in under four years to roughly 700,000 policies, with a 29% rate hike landing late this year. Both states face wildfire and catastrophe exposure, and both are pricing risk against the same global reinsurance benchmark — which is actually down 23% from its peak. The divergence isn't explained by weather; it's explained by regulatory policy and how welcoming each state's rules are to private capital.

"Same crisis, totally opposite directions. The driver is regulatory policy, not meteorology."

Traders and analysts who follow insurers, reinsurers, or catastrophe-bond markets watch residual pool size and growth rate as a leading indicator of regulatory and market health in a given state. A shrinking Citizens or FAIR Plan pool signals that private capital is returning, competition is increasing, and rate relief is likely coming to homeowners and to insurers' loss ratios. A growing pool signals the opposite — private insurers are in retreat, risk is concentrating on the state's own balance sheet (and ultimately taxpayers, since residual pools can levy surcharges on all policyholders statewide if they run a deficit), and further rate hikes are probable. This is also a signal used by mortgage-backed securities analysts and housing economists, since escrow shortfalls tied to insurance costs are now a major driver of monthly payment shocks and rising default risk in exposed metro areas.

The biggest misconception is treating rising insurance costs as pure proof that "the underlying risk got worse" — more storms, more fires, more expected losses. The data in the video directly contradicts that assumption: the global catastrophe reinsurance index fell 16% this year alone and 23% from its post-pandemic peak, back to 2021 pricing levels, at the very same time homeowner insurance costs hit an all-time high nationally. If pure risk economics were driving the whole story, insurance costs should be falling in step with reinsurance costs. Instead, the video argues the spread between falling wholesale catastrophe pricing and rising retail premiums is being captured as corporate margin expansion — insurers passing through cost decreases unevenly, and slowly, if a given state doesn't force the issue with rate filings or regulatory pressure.

Where this breaks down is when a state's regulatory environment discourages private insurers from returning even after reinsurance costs fall — leaving the residual pool structurally oversized and exposed to concentration risk. A state-backed insurer with hundreds of thousands of policies concentrated in the highest-risk geography is itself a systemic risk: if a major catastrophe hits before private capital returns, the pool can face a shortfall large enough to trigger surcharges (called "assessments") on every policyholder in the state, private and public alike, or even on other lines of insurance entirely. This is precisely why California's growing FAIR Plan and its recent 29% rate hike are being watched closely — a large, growing residual pool in a wildfire-prone state is a bet that hasn't paid off yet, while Florida's shrinking pool suggests its regulatory reforms succeeded in luring private capital back before a major loss event tested the system.

This concept connects directly to the broader escrow-squeeze story: rising homeowner insurance premiums are a primary driver of the escrow shortages now hitting roughly 65% of mortgaged homeowners, forcing average payment increases of about $175 a month. It also ties into reinsurance pricing cycles as a macro indicator, since reinsurance renewals reset every January and July and offer a forward-looking signal of where retail insurance costs are likely headed next. Watching whether residual pools keep shrinking (private market health, likely rate relief) or keep growing (private market retreat, likely further hikes) alongside the next wholesale reinsurance renewal cycle is, per the video, the clearest test of whether homeowners are paying for genuine catastrophe risk or simply for insurer margin.
