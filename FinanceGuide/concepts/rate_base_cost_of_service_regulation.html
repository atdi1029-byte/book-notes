Cost-of-Service Rate-Base Regulation

Cost-of-service rate-base regulation is the system most U.S. states use to set what a regulated electric utility may charge. The utility is a legal monopoly: it is the only seller in its territory, so customers can't shop around. In exchange for that exclusivity, a state public utility commission (PUC) controls its prices. The commission lets the utility recover its reasonable operating costs, such as fuel, wages and maintenance. It also lets the utility earn a set return on the capital it has invested in the physical system. That invested capital is the rate base: the wires, poles, substations, transformers and power plants, valued at what they cost less depreciation. Profit comes from owning and growing that asset pile, not from selling kilowatt-hours at a markup.

This matters because electricity is not optional, and the system's incentives shape your bill directly. The commodity itself, fuel and purchased power, is generally passed through to customers at cost. The profit is earned on the steel in the ground. So the regulator's decisions about what counts as a prudent investment, and what return to allow, quietly set how fast bills rise. They also decide who carries the risk of overbuilding. For investors, regulated utilities are some of the most bond-like stocks in the market, because their earnings follow the rate base rather than the weather or the economy. For households, the rate base is the main reason delivery charges can climb even when you use less power. The video's central claim is that this old machinery is now meeting the largest build-out in decades.

Here is the mechanism step by step. First, the commission determines the utility's revenue requirement, the total dollars it is allowed to collect. The standard formula is: revenue requirement = operating expenses + depreciation + taxes + (rate base × allowed rate of return). Second, the rate of return is a blend of the cost of debt and the allowed return on equity (ROE), weighted by the utility's mix of debt and equity. Third, the revenue requirement is divided across customer classes, residential, commercial and industrial, and turned into per-kWh and fixed charges. Fourth, the utility files a rate case, and regulators hold hearings and approve some or all of the request. Finally, new spending enters the rate base and earns its return from then on, until it is depreciated away.

The video puts the numbers plainly: regulators typically allow an ROE of roughly 9% to 10.5%, while the utility can borrow in the bond market at about 6% to 7%. That gap is the heart of the incentive. If a utility spends $1 billion on new transmission lines and substations, that $1 billion goes into the rate base. At a 10% equity return, the investment can be worth on the order of $100 million a year in allowed return on the equity-funded portion, plus recovery of the depreciation and the interest on the debt-funded portion, all collected from customers. The host's point is that the profit scales with the spending, not with efficiency. A utility that builds $2 billion earns roughly twice what one that builds $1 billion earns.

"The more money they borrow and spend on mega scale infrastructure projects, the larger their rate base becomes and the larger their absolute profit check at the end of the year."

Economists call this the Averch-Johnson effect: when the allowed return exceeds the true cost of capital, a regulated firm is pushed to over-invest in capital. The video's trigger is AI. From 2010 to 2020, U.S. demand was nearly flat, so the rate base grew slowly. Then hyperscalers like Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta and Amazon began building data centers, and a single gigawatt-scale campus can draw more power than hundreds of thousands of homes. The video cites a projected 300% surge in data-center consumption over five years. Utilities such as Duke, NextEra, Southern and Dominion are planning hundreds of billions of dollars in substations, high-voltage corridors and gas turbines. Under cost-of-service rules, all of it becomes rate base, which means all of it earns a return.

For traders and investors, the rate base is the number to watch, because it is the engine of earnings. Utilities often publish a rate-base growth target, say 7% to 9% a year, and guide earnings per share to match. Analysts track the capital expenditure plan, the allowed ROE in each state, and the gap between the allowed and the actually earned return. A constructive regulator, one that approves quick recovery and a high ROE, makes a utility more valuable, and states are ranked on exactly that. On the bond side, the video points to the heavy debt issuance funding the build-out. Signals to watch include rising capex guidance, new rate-case filings, and tariff riders, the line items that recover specific projects between full rate cases.

Several nuances get lost in the outrage. First, "guaranteed" is too strong. Regulators set an allowed return, not an earned one. The utility can still miss it through cost overruns, regulatory lag (the delay between spending money and getting it into rates), or disallowances, where the commission rules some spending imprudent and refuses to let the utility recover it. Second, the profit isn't purely "on the infrastructure and nothing else": operating costs are recovered too, just without markup. Third, not every new line is a boondoggle. Grids genuinely need upgrades, and real demand growth can justify the build. Fourth, the video's claim that the whole bill is socialized onto households is a simplification. Many commissions use cost-causation principles, large-load tariffs and special contracts that make big customers pay for the capacity they trigger. How well that works varies by state.

The system breaks in a few ways. If the utility builds for demand that doesn't show up, say a data center cancels or the AI boom cools, the assets become stranded costs. Someone has to absorb them, and regulators often leave much of that with the remaining customers, since the utility acted with approval. That is the household's fear in the video: paying for capacity that serves someone else. The opposite failure is regulators squeezing returns so hard that utilities stop investing, which risks reliability and blackouts. Politics matters too. Rate hikes of the size the video describes, with nearly $10 billion in new proposals in one quarter, invite backlash, intervention by consumer advocates, and cuts to the requested ROE. Wildfire liability and storm costs can also push a utility toward distress.

This concept connects to several others. The capacity auction in PJM, the grid operator covering 13 states and over 65 million people, is a different mechanism: it is a market price set in a deregulated wholesale market, not a regulated return. The video says it jumped more than 800%, and that cost reaches bills in a different way. Both feed the same bill, but they have different causes and different fixes. Rate-base regulation also ties to the cost of capital: when interest rates rise, the allowed ROE usually lags, and utility stocks behave like bond proxies. It relates to moral hazard and incentive design, since the structure rewards spending. And it connects to performance-based regulation, an alternative that rewards reliability or efficiency instead of asset growth. Knowing the rate base lets you read a utility's earnings and your own bill with the same lens.

The practical takeaway has two sides. As a household, split your bill into supply versus delivery, look at riders, check whether you're on a time-of-use plan that suits your habits, and know that rate cases have public comment periods and formal hearings where consumer advocates can challenge the ROE and demand that data-center costs be assigned to data centers. The video says organized intervention has trimmed requested increases in several jurisdictions. As an investor, remember that the same mechanism that raises bills raises utility earnings, and that the growth is only as durable as the regulator's patience. In a conversation about markets, the clear statement is this: a regulated utility earns a return on what it builds, so growth in the rate base is growth in profit, and the political question is who pays for it and how much return is fair.
