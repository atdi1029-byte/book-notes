Realized vs. Unrealized Gains and Tax Realization

Every investment you own has two profit numbers attached to it, and they are not the same thing. An unrealized gain (also called a paper gain) is the increase in an asset's value since you bought it, while you still hold it. If you bought a share at $100 and it now trades at $180, you have an $80 unrealized gain. It shows up in your brokerage statement, but it is only a number on a screen. A realized gain happens when you sell. The moment you sell at $180, that $80 becomes real cash and a real event in the eyes of the tax authority. Losses work the same way: unrealized losses are paper, realized losses are locked in.

This distinction matters because in the United States, and in most countries, you generally owe tax on gains only when they are realized. Your neighbor's house could double in value and Apple could triple in your 401(k)-adjacent brokerage account, and no tax bill arrives until someone sells. That gives investors real control over the timing of their tax bill. It also creates a hidden layer of decision-making in markets: what people choose to sell, especially when forced, depends not just on what they want to own but on what it will cost them in taxes to let go of it. The video leans on exactly this idea when explaining what gets dumped first in a cash crunch.

Mechanically, the tax calculation runs like this. When you sell, your cost basis (what you originally paid, adjusted for things like reinvested dividends) is subtracted from the sale price. The result is your realized gain or loss. How that gain is taxed depends on the holding period. Assets held longer than one year qualify for long-term capital gains rates, which in the U.S. are 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income. Assets held a year or less produce short-term capital gains, taxed as ordinary income at rates that can top 37%. So the same $80 gain can cost you very different amounts depending on how long you waited.

Now apply this to the video's scenario. When a prime broker issues a margin call, a fund must raise cash fast, and the narrator lays out the chief risk officer's options: corporate bonds are illiquid, so selling means taking a 10-cent haircut; big tech can be sold, but the video says liquidating it too fast "blows out your own equity book and they trigger tax disclosures." The fund's answer, per the video, is to go "straight to the assets sitting on the largest unrealized profits for the year" and hit the bid on COMEX gold and silver futures, which clear "instantly in hundreds of millions of dollars with fractional slippage."

"Nobody likes tax disclosure."

Traders and investors use this concept in practical ways. The most common is tax-loss harvesting: deliberately selling losers to realize losses that offset realized gains elsewhere, reducing the tax bill. Another is choosing specific lots when selling, so you sell the shares with the highest cost basis first and realize the smallest gain. Investors also hold winners past the one-year mark to get long-term rates, and they hold appreciated assets in tax-advantaged accounts like IRAs and 401(k)s, where selling triggers no immediate tax at all. The signal to watch is what an investor does under pressure: a forced seller ignores these optimizations, and that reveals which markets are deep enough to absorb the selling.

There are nuances and misconceptions worth getting right. First, the video's framing is a simplification. Large institutional funds are not all taxable individuals; many are pass-through entities, foreign, or tax-exempt, and futures contracts in the U.S. get special treatment under Section 1256, taxed 60% long-term and 40% short-term and marked to market at year end regardless of whether you sold. So "unrealized profit on gold futures" is not quite the same as an unrealized gain on stock. Second, the stronger reason those markets get sold first is liquidity, not taxes. Tax friction is a real factor, but the desk sells what it can sell quickly at tight spreads. The video itself says traders "sell what they can sell." Tax is one weight on the scale, not the whole scale.

Another gotcha is the wash sale rule. If you sell a security at a loss and buy the same or a substantially identical one within 30 days before or after, the IRS disallows the loss for the current year. Traders who try to harvest losses in a panic and re-enter right away can find the deduction erased. Also, remember that realizing a gain is not always optional. Mutual funds and ETFs sometimes distribute capital gains to shareholders even if you never sold a share, because the fund itself sold holdings internally. And a forced sale, such as a broker liquidating your account after a margin call, realizes gains whether you like it or not. The tax bill arrives on the broker's schedule, not yours.

What happens when this system breaks? The deferral of tax on unrealized gains creates a lock-in effect: investors sit on big winners because selling means paying tax, even when they would otherwise rebalance. That reluctance can keep capital stuck in concentrated positions, like a big tech stock that has grown to dominate a portfolio. When a forced-selling event hits, the lock-in disappears in a hurry. Margin calls override tax preferences, and holders sell whatever raises cash, including positions that produce ugly tax bills. That is how a single-day gain of deferred profit can turn into a realized liability, and why a leveraged investor who is forced out at the wrong time can owe tax on gains they have already watched evaporate in the same downturn.

This concept connects to several others in the video. Margin calls are the trigger: a broker demands cash, and the investor must decide what to sell. Liquidity is the deciding filter: the video's restaurant owner cannot sell the freezers or the stove, so he sells the family gold instead, the asset that converts to cash fastest. Leverage is the amplifier: with 10:1 or 20:1 ratios, small price moves force big sales. And cross-asset contagion is the result: because the winners get sold to fund the losers, even assets with no bad news, like gold on that day, drop sharply. The lesson for a personal investor is to remember that a falling price does not always mean bad fundamentals. Sometimes it means someone else needed cash, and your asset was the easiest thing they owned to sell.
