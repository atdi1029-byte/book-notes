Loan Covenants

A loan covenant is a promise written into a loan agreement. The borrower agrees to do certain things (affirmative covenants), such as deliver audited financials, keep insurance, and pay taxes. The borrower also agrees not to do others (negative covenants), such as take on more debt, sell key assets, pay big dividends, or pledge collateral to other lenders. The third type is the financial covenant, a numerical test the borrower must keep passing, such as a minimum cash balance or a maximum debt-to-earnings ratio. Think of a covenant as the lender's way of staying involved after the money leaves. The interest rate pays the lender for taking risk, and the covenants let the lender control how much risk the borrower can add afterward.

Covenants matter because they decide who holds power over a business when conditions change. A company with a loan and no covenants can do almost anything until it misses a payment. A company with tight covenants can be pushed into a conversation with its bank long before it runs out of money. That affects real decisions: whether a small manufacturer can buy a new machine, whether a restaurant group can open a second location, and whether a landlord can pay out profits to its owners. For the economy as a whole, covenants are how banks ration credit quietly. A bank rarely announces that it is tightening lending. It tightens the terms on loans it already has, and borrowers feel it as friction rather than as a headline.

Mechanically, it works in a few steps. When a loan or credit line is set up, the agreement lists the covenants and the thresholds. Say a business has a $2 million revolving line and must keep a debt service coverage ratio of at least 1.25, meaning cash flow must be 1.25 times its required loan payments. Each quarter or year, the borrower sends financials, and a compliance certificate shows the numbers. If the ratio is 1.4, nothing happens. If it falls to 1.1, that is a covenant breach (or violation). A breach is not the same as a missed payment. It is a technical default, which gives the lender the right to act, though the lender does not have to.

When a lender has that right, the usual options are a waiver (forgiving the breach, sometimes for a fee), an amendment (rewriting the terms, usually more strictly), a higher interest rate, extra collateral, or, in the worst case, acceleration, where the whole balance becomes due immediately. Most breaches end in a renegotiation, and that is where the lender's leverage shows. The lender is not really negotiating over the breach. It is using the breach to get a better position on the loan than the original terms gave it. Borrowers who are only slightly out of compliance often find that the fix costs them more than the violation did.

The video applies this to mid-tier and regional banks. Its claim is that banks sitting on about $325 billion in unrealized losses on low-coupon Treasuries and mortgage bonds, which the host compares to spoiled Wagyu beef locked in a freezer, cannot sell those assets without recognizing the losses. According to the video, such banks are said to insert restrictive covenants at the annual review and demand 40% cash collateral on operating facilities. Put in numbers, a business with a $1 million operating line would have to park $400,000 in cash at the bank to keep using it. That business can then really draw only $600,000 of its own line, because it deposited the other $400,000 itself. The host summarizes the pressure this way: banks are "quietly freezing your credit to keep from unlocking the vault."

"They're pretty much padlocking the door, pretending the losses don't exist and quietly freezing your credit to keep from unlocking the vault."

Why would a bank with trapped assets want cash collateral? The video's logic runs like this. The bank bought 10-year and 30-year bonds paying 1.5% to 2.2% in 2020 and 2021. With the 10-year Treasury now around 5.27% (per the video), those bonds are worth far less than what the bank paid, and the host says a 30-year, 2% bond can lose 30% to 40% of its value. Selling would turn paper losses into real ones and cut capital. So the bank needs its balance sheet to shrink or stay liquid without selling bonds. Cash collateral from borrowers does that, since it turns a loan into a mostly self-funded one, and it lowers the bank's exposure without touching the frozen assets. The video presents this as the bank's real motive. Whether that is the true reason in any given case is a claim to check, not a fact to assume.

Investors and traders use covenant information as a signal. Tightening terms across many borrowers, especially a shift from covenant-lite toward maintenance covenants, suggests that lenders feel more risk or less balance-sheet room. Sources include the Federal Reserve's Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey, which reports each quarter whether banks are tightening standards, and the quarterly call reports (Schedule RC and related schedules) that the video cites. For a bank stock, watch the gap between held-to-maturity securities' fair value and their book value, the share of deposits that are uninsured, and rising borrower demand for collateral. For a company stock, a mention of "covenant amendment," "waiver," or "going concern" in a filing deserves attention, because those phrases often show up before bad news.

There are several nuances that the video's framing can hide. First, covenants are ordinary. Almost every commercial loan has them, and most are tightened or reset at renewal for reasons that have nothing to do with a bank crisis. Second, requiring compensating balances (cash held at the bank in exchange for credit) is an old and legal practice, and 10% to 20% is common in some lending. It is a very different thing from 40%, which is unusually heavy. Third, one anecdote about one bank does not prove a system-wide pattern, and the video offers no survey data for the 40% figure. Fourth, unrealized losses on held-to-maturity bonds only become real if the bank is forced to sell, which usually happens when depositors run. A bank with stable deposits can hold those bonds until they mature at par. Whether that is likely is the real debate.

When covenants fail, the damage comes in stages. A borrower that breaches and cannot get a waiver may lose its credit line at the very moment it needs cash for payroll and suppliers. That can force asset sales, layoffs, or a rushed move to a more expensive lender, such as a private credit fund charging several points more. If many borrowers hit the same wall together, credit contracts across a region, which can hurt the local economy and, in turn, the bank's other loans. This is the loop that worries critics of the banking system. There is also risk for the lender: a bank that squeezes good borrowers may lose them to competitors, and a bank that is too lenient about breaches may hide problems until they are too large to fix.

Loan covenants connect to several other ideas in this guide. Collateral and compensating balances tie them to the bank's own balance sheet, and the held-to-maturity versus available-for-sale accounting choice described in the video shapes how much the bank cares about liquidity. Duration risk explains why the bonds lost value in the first place, and mark-to-market rules decide whether the losses show up on paper. Deposit flight and uninsured deposits are what could turn hidden losses into real ones, as at Silicon Valley Bank in 2023. If you run a business, the practical lessons are simple: read your covenants, track your ratios before the bank does, ask about renewal terms early, and keep a second lending relationship in reserve. If you are a depositor, understand that covenants are the visible surface of a bank's private balance-sheet worries.
