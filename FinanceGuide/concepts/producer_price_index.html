Producer Price Index (PPI)

The Producer Price Index (PPI) is a monthly economic report published by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics that measures the average change in prices that domestic producers receive for their goods and services over time. Think of it as inflation from the seller's side rather than the buyer's side. While the more famous Consumer Price Index (CPI) tracks what you pay at the grocery store or gas station, PPI tracks what the farmer, the refinery, or the factory receives when they sell those same goods upstream. It covers thousands of products across agriculture, mining, manufacturing, and services, organized into stages of production. When PPI rises, it means producers are getting more money — or, critically, that their costs are rising and squeezing their margins.

PPI matters because it functions as an early warning system for inflation in the broader economy. Prices don't appear from nowhere at your local supermarket — they travel a supply chain. Raw materials get extracted, then processed, then assembled, then shipped, then sold wholesale, and finally land on the retail shelf. Each step involves costs. When costs rise early in that chain, they almost always migrate downstream. A trucking company paying record diesel prices will eventually raise its rates. A food manufacturer facing higher grain prices will eventually raise its wholesale prices. Those wholesale prices then become your grocery bill. This transmission lag — typically weeks to a few months — is exactly why economists and traders watch PPI so closely. It tells you where consumer prices are probably heading before they get there.

Mechanically, the BLS surveys thousands of companies across the economy each month, asking what prices they actually received for their products — not list prices, but transaction prices, net of discounts and rebates. The data is organized into three key stages: crude goods (raw materials like crude oil or raw cotton), intermediate goods (partially processed materials like flour or steel sheets), and finished goods (products ready for sale like gasoline or packaged food). Analysts watch each stage independently because a spike in crude goods may take months to appear in finished goods. The headline PPI number and the core PPI (which strips out food and energy due to their volatility) are both reported, and the Fed pays careful attention to the core reading when calibrating its inflation expectations.

By the week of the recap, West Texas Intermediate crude oil had closed at $89.38 — up 7% in a single week, its best performance since July 13th. Brent crude was at $93.93. Simultaneously, diesel prices had hit a record high, driven by a U.S.-Iran conflict now in its seventh month that had reduced commodity vessel traffic through the Strait of Hormuz from a 10-day average of 15 ships per day down to just four on a single Thursday. Those diesel prices weren't abstract — they were embedded in every freight cost, every trucking route, every last-mile delivery. That energy shock was exactly the kind of input cost event that would first appear in PPI before trickling into CPI weeks later.

"This is the first inflation print that can capture what $89 oil and record diesel prices, which you're hearing about right now, are doing to our wallets." — Mark Malik

Traders and investors use PPI in several interconnected ways. First, they look at it as a leading indicator for CPI — if PPI is running hot, expect CPI to follow, which means expect the Federal Reserve to lean hawkish. Second, they use it to assess margin compression risk for publicly traded companies. If producers are paying more for inputs but can't pass those costs to customers, earnings get squeezed. Third, PPI helps explain sector rotation: rising PPI often benefits commodity producers and energy companies (who are the ones receiving higher prices) while hurting consumer discretionary companies and manufacturers stuck absorbing input costs. In the video's week, energy gained 2.2% while consumer discretionary lost 1.9% — a rotation that PPI dynamics help explain.

One of the most important practical signals traders extract from PPI is the relationship between PPI and CPI together. When PPI is rising faster than CPI, it means producers are absorbing costs and margins are shrinking — watch for earnings misses. When CPI is rising faster than PPI, it means retailers have pricing power and margins may be expanding. The spread between the two tells a story about who holds power in the economy at a given moment. In the context of the video, August PPI was flagged as a critical data point alongside CPI precisely because the combination of the two reports would either confirm or deny whether the energy shock was bleeding into consumer prices — a question with direct consequences for whether the Fed would hike rates at its September 16th meeting.

A common misconception about PPI is that it's simply a duplicate of CPI measured one step earlier. In reality, the two indexes cover fundamentally different economic populations and can diverge significantly. Services, which are a major component of CPI, are only partially captured in PPI. Additionally, PPI can spike sharply and then reverse quickly if commodity prices are volatile — a one-month surge in crude goods PPI doesn't always transmit fully to finished goods or consumers if producers decide to absorb the cost or if demand softens. Another nuance: core PPI, which excludes food and energy, is often more meaningful for Fed decision-making than headline PPI, because food and energy are globally priced and volatile in ways domestic monetary policy can't easily control.

PPI becomes particularly treacherous in environments where supply chains are disrupted and pricing power is uneven — exactly the environment described in the video. When only four commodity vessels are moving through a critical chokepoint against a normal average of fifteen, you have a supply shock that feeds PPI immediately but feeds CPI on a delay. Meanwhile, the Fed is making rate decisions in real time with incomplete information. As Mark Malik pointed out, the Fed was heading into its September meeting with jobs data from ADP (38,000 private payrolls) and the BLS (162,000) that were telling completely different stories. Add a PPI print distorted by an energy shock and a CPI print still catching up to it, and you have a central bank navigating with a foggy windshield. This is when rate decisions — and market reactions — can be most violent and least predictable.

PPI connects directly to several other concepts in market analysis. It is tightly linked to ISM manufacturing and services prices paid indexes, which the video cited at 71.1 and 72.6 respectively — both signaling that input costs were not cooling. It feeds into breakeven inflation rates derived from Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, which traders use to price inflation expectations into bonds. It influences Fed funds futures pricing, since a hot PPI raises the probability of a rate hike and reprices the entire yield curve. And ultimately, PPI is the first chapter in the inflation story that CPI finishes. When you see $89 oil and record diesel in one week's market recap, the trained reader immediately knows: watch PPI in two weeks, then watch CPI in four, and position accordingly before the Fed reads the same numbers you just did.
