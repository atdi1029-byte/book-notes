Currency Hedging Cost and Yield Spread Erosion

When a Japanese pension fund buys a US Treasury bond, it earns interest in dollars. But its liabilities — the retirement payments it owes — are in yen. That mismatch is dangerous: if the dollar weakens against the yen before the bond matures, the fund loses money on the currency move even if the bond itself performed fine. To protect against this, the fund buys a currency hedge — essentially a contract that locks in a future exchange rate, guaranteeing the yen value of its dollar-denominated returns. This is standard practice for any institution investing across borders. The problem nobody on television explains is that this protection is not free. It has a real, measurable cost — and that cost is what drives one of the most important structural forces in global bond markets.

The price of a currency hedge is driven by something called interest rate differentials — the gap between short-term borrowing rates in the two countries involved. If US short-term rates are significantly higher than Japanese short-term rates, then hedging dollars back into yen becomes expensive, because you are effectively borrowing in the higher-rate currency. This cost is baked into the price of cross-currency basis swaps and FX forward contracts, the instruments institutions use to hedge. When the US Federal Reserve raises rates aggressively — as it did from 2022 through 2024 — the cost of hedging dollars into yen or euros rises in lockstep. This is not a minor rounding error. At peak Fed tightening, the annualized cost of hedging dollar exposure back into yen exceeded 5% per year for Japanese investors.

Here is the mechanical sequence, step by step. A Japanese life insurer buys a 10-year US Treasury yielding, say, 4.81%. To lock in the yen value of those returns, it simultaneously enters an FX forward — agreeing to sell dollars and buy yen at a fixed rate one year from now. The cost of that contract reflects the interest rate differential between the US and Japan. When US rates are 5.3% and Japanese rates near zero, the forward contract costs roughly 5% annually. Subtract that hedging cost from the 4.81% Treasury yield and you get approximately negative 0.2%. The Japanese investor has paid more for the hedge than the bond yields. The hedged yield — the actual return after currency protection — has been completely wiped out, or gone negative. This is yield spread erosion in its most extreme form.

This is not theoretical. The video anchors this dynamic to a specific moment: Japanese domestic government bonds — JGBs — were paying 3% as the Bank of Japan finally allowed yields to normalize after years of yield curve control. Meanwhile, US 10-year Treasuries sat at 4.81%. On the surface, that looks like a nearly two-percentage-point advantage for the American paper. But once you subtract the 5%-plus annual cost of hedging the dollar back into yen, the math inverts completely. A Japanese institution holding hedged US Treasuries was earning something close to negative territory. A Japanese institution buying domestic JGBs was earning a clean, unhedged 3%. The choice became obvious. The most important marginal buyers of US government debt were walking away from the market — not because of geopolitics, but because of arithmetic.

Traders and portfolio managers monitor this through a metric called the currency-hedged yield spread — the yield on a foreign bond minus the cost of the FX hedge, compared to the equivalent domestic instrument. When this spread is positive, foreign buying flows in. When it turns negative, capital repatriates. Sophisticated fixed-income desks at Japanese banks and insurers run these calculations daily. The hedge cost is tracked through the cross-currency basis, a real-time market price that reflects supply and demand for currency swaps. When you see the basis widen — meaning dollar hedging gets more expensive — experienced traders interpret that as a signal that foreign demand for Treasuries is about to soften, which puts upward pressure on US yields. It is a leading indicator hiding in plain sight.

The most common misconception is that yield comparisons across borders are apples-to-apples. Financial media constantly compares raw yields — "the US 10-year at 4.81% versus Japan's 1.5%" — as if a Japanese investor can simply pocket that difference. They cannot, not without taking currency risk. The relevant comparison is always the hedged yield. A second misconception is that hedging is optional. For regulated institutions — insurance companies, pension funds, banks — it is not. Regulatory capital rules in Japan and Europe require that foreign currency exposure be matched or hedged. These entities cannot choose to go unhedged just because the cost is high. When hedging costs rise, they face a binary choice: accept negative hedged returns, or stop buying foreign bonds entirely. Most choose the latter.

When this dynamic breaks down at scale, the consequences are systemic. If Japanese and European institutions simultaneously reduce or eliminate their purchases of US Treasuries — because hedged yields have turned negative — the US government loses a critical source of demand for its debt at exactly the moment it needs it most. The Treasury must then attract buyers with higher yields, which means borrowing costs rise for everyone in the US economy. This is the transmission mechanism the video describes: a quiet shift in Japanese domestic bond yields, combined with high Fed rates inflating hedge costs, caused the financial arithmetic for the world's largest pool of institutional savings to flip. That single structural change had more lasting power over the 10-year Treasury yield than any missile strike in the Middle East. Geopolitical shocks are acute. Hedge cost erosion is chronic.

This concept connects directly to quantitative tightening, the Federal Reserve's balance sheet reduction, and the broader phenomenon of global dollar funding stress. When the Fed raises rates, it simultaneously makes US assets nominally more attractive while making them operationally inaccessible to the foreign institutions that have historically been their largest buyers. It is a paradox built into the plumbing of the international monetary system. Understanding currency hedging costs also illuminates why mortgage rates can stay elevated even after the Fed signals rate cuts — if Japanese and European demand for Treasuries remains suppressed, the 10-year yield stays high, and the 30-year mortgage rate, which is priced off the 10-year, stays with it. The Tokyo time bomb is not metaphorical. It is a ledger entry that resets the price of money for every American borrower.
