Private Credit / Direct Lending

When most people think of business loans, they picture a bank. A company needs capital, walks into a bank, and the bank lends money from its deposit base. Private credit, also called direct lending, cuts the bank out entirely. Instead, specialized funds — typically run by asset managers like Ares, Blackstone, or Blue Owl — lend money directly to companies, usually mid-sized businesses that are too large for a community bank but too small or too risky to issue publicly traded bonds. These funds raise capital from institutional investors like pension funds and endowments, then deploy it as loans. The borrower gets their money, the fund earns interest, and no public market ever touches the transaction. That opacity is both the appeal and the danger.

Private credit matters because it has quietly become a load-bearing wall in the U.S. corporate debt structure. The Federal Reserve estimates the market has grown to $1.4 trillion, representing roughly 10% of all U.S. non-financial corporate debt and, more strikingly, about 33% of all below-investment-grade debt. That means a third of the riskiest corporate borrowing in America now happens completely outside the public bond market, with no exchange, no daily price discovery, and no real-time transparency. When this market is healthy, it provides vital financing to businesses that public markets would ignore. When it cracks, there is no obvious signal — no flashing ticker, no Bloomberg headline — until redemptions spike and dividends get cut.

The mechanism works like this: a fund raises, say, $5 billion from investors. It then lends that capital to dozens of companies as senior secured loans, typically at floating interest rates — often pegged to SOFR plus a spread of 5–7%, resulting in all-in rates of 10–13%. The loans are illiquid by design; there is no secondary market where you can easily sell them. The fund earns interest income and passes most of it to investors as dividends, which is why private credit funds often advertise yields of 8–12%. The companies borrowing are almost always leveraged — meaning they already carry significant debt relative to their earnings — and are frequently owned by private equity firms that used debt to buy them in the first place. So the borrower is already stretched thin before the private credit loan even closes.

The most common retail wrapper for private credit exposure is the Business Development Company, or BDC. BDCs are publicly traded or publicly registered funds that Congress created in 1980 specifically to channel investment into small and mid-sized businesses. They are required to distribute at least 90% of taxable income to shareholders, which is why they pay those attractive dividends. Reuters audited 44 American BDCs and found that at the close of Q2 2025, they held investments valued at $92.88 billion against an original reported cost of $95.19 billion — a ratio of roughly 97–98 cents of fair value for every dollar originally lent. Six months earlier that ratio was closer to 99 cents. That 2-cent decline sounds small until you realize it represents $2.31 billion in value evaporating from a single category of fund in under six months.

The specific stress driving today's private credit problems is concentrated in software lending. For roughly a decade, private credit funds underwrote loans to Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) companies using a straightforward model: count the seats. If a company had 10,000 employees all paying for a software license, lenders treated that as near-guaranteed recurring revenue — a fixed annuity. They lent aggressively on that assumption, often at 6–7 times EBITDA. The Bank for International Settlements found that roughly $115 billion of private credit sits in horizontal software tools — project management, workflow automation, workforce applications. Then AI arrived. When five autonomous agents can do the work of fifty employees, enterprise clients don't cancel the software; they cancel 45 of the 50 seats. Revenue per customer collapses even as customer counts grow.

"HubSpot is acquiring customers 3.5 times faster than it can grow revenue per user, and management only admitted that enterprise clients want outcome-based pricing instead of paying per head."

This is where the nuance gets critical, because the crisis in private credit is not a classic cash-flow crisis. Almost nobody has actually stopped paying. The Bank for International Settlements found that less than 1% of those software borrowers are behind on a contracted payment. What's happening instead is an underwriting breakdown: lenders priced these loans assuming per-seat revenue was permanent, and they never added a single basis point of spread to account for AI disruption risk. Now that the structural threat is obvious, they are marking down the value of loans that are still technically performing — 81% of the entire software loan book has been marked down in 2025, compared to only 40% of non-tech borrowers. The loss isn't in cash flow yet. It's in the updated assessment of what that future cash flow is actually worth.

One of the most important mechanics to understand is Payment-in-Kind (PIK) loans, because this is how a fund can appear healthy while quietly deteriorating. In a PIK structure, when the borrower cannot pay cash interest, the unpaid interest is simply added to the loan principal. The lender records this as income on paper. The fund then uses that paper income to justify paying investors a real cash dividend — often funded by new investor subscriptions rather than actual cash from borrowers. Federal Reserve Bank of Boston research shows PIK usage in private credit jumped from roughly 6% of loans in early 2022 to over 10% by early 2026 — a 67% surge. When more than 5% of a BDC's total investment income is PIK, the fund is effectively borrowing against the future to pay today's dividend. That yield is manufactured, not earned.

The liquidity structure of these vehicles creates a trap that most retail investors don't understand until it's too late. Unlike a stock or a public bond that you can sell in seconds, private credit funds — particularly interval funds — typically allow redemptions only quarterly, and cap total redemptions at 5% of fund assets per quarter. When more investors want out than the fund can accommodate, it imposes a redemption gate: you submit your exit request, and you wait. In Q1 of 2026, accepted redemption requests across semi-liquid private credit vehicles exceeded new investor inflows for the first time in history. Blackstone's $77.2 billion private credit fund faced $4.3 billion in redemptions in Q3 — roughly 10% of shares outstanding — against that 5% quarterly repurchase cap. New subscriptions had slowed to $750 million. The math is merciless: at that pace, investors who want out could wait years to fully exit.

The regulatory dimension is what transforms a contained institutional problem into a potential household crisis. In March 2026, the Department of Labor introduced a proposed safe harbor rule that would give retirement plan fiduciaries legal cover to include private credit inside 401(k) plans — including as a sleeve inside target-date funds, the default investment for most American workers who never actively choose where their retirement savings go. The public comment window closed in June 2026. The timing is not coincidental: institutional investors are hitting redemption gates just as Washington is preparing to open a new pool of retail capital to absorb supply. If you hold a 2045 target-date fund, you may soon be allocated to the same illiquid, gated, PIK-laden vehicles that sophisticated institutions are currently trying to exit. Check your fund's prospectus for language about "alternative credit," "senior secured debt sleeves," or "private debt allocations" — that's the signal that this has already arrived in your account.

Private credit connects to several adjacent concepts worth understanding together. It is part of the broader category of alternative assets that institutional investors use to chase yield when public bond markets offer inadequate returns — a direct consequence of the decade of near-zero interest rates following 2008. It competes with and intersects leveraged loans and high-yield bonds, which are the public-market equivalents serving similar borrowers. The credit quality concerns connect to credit spreads: when spreads in public markets widen, it signals stress, but private credit has no such public signal. Most importantly, private credit is deeply intertwined with the private equity ecosystem — most private credit borrowers are PE-owned companies loaded with acquisition debt, meaning a slowdown in PE exits or a deterioration in portfolio company performance ripples directly into private credit quality. When both stress simultaneously, as they are now, the feedback loop can accelerate faster than quarterly valuation marks reveal.
