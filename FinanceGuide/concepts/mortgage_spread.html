Mortgage Spread (Treasury-to-Mortgage Rate)

When you apply for a 30-year fixed mortgage, the bank doesn't invent your interest rate out of thin air. It starts with a benchmark — the yield on the 10-year U.S. Treasury note — and adds a cushion on top called the mortgage spread. That cushion exists because lending to a homeowner is riskier than lending to the U.S. government. Treasuries are backed by the full faith and credit of the federal government, meaning the government can tax or print to repay. A homeowner can lose their job, walk away, or die. So lenders demand extra compensation for those extra risks. The spread is simply the gap, measured in basis points (one basis point equals 0.01%), between what the government pays to borrow and what you pay to borrow.

This spread matters enormously to the real economy because it directly determines how affordable housing is for tens of millions of households. When the spread widens, mortgage rates rise even if Treasury yields stay flat — homes become more expensive to finance, buyers pull back, and the housing market cools. When it narrows, rates fall relative to Treasuries and activity heats up. Policymakers, central bankers, and Wall Street analysts track the spread as a real-time gauge of credit market stress and housing market health. It also matters to institutional investors who hold mortgage-backed securities (MBS), because changes in the spread directly affect the price and yield of those instruments.

Mechanically, the process works like this. The U.S. Treasury issues bonds at auction, and the market determines their yield based on supply, demand, and inflation expectations. Separately, banks and mortgage companies originate home loans, then bundle them into MBS — pools of mortgages sold to investors. The yield investors demand on those MBS is what ultimately sets consumer mortgage rates. Because MBS carry risks Treasuries don't (prepayment risk, credit risk, liquidity risk), investors require a higher yield. That additional yield requirement, expressed relative to the 10-year Treasury, is the spread. The current 30-year mortgage rate at any moment is roughly: 10-year Treasury yield + mortgage spread. Both halves move independently.

Here is where it gets subtle and where the Norway story from the video becomes a perfect teaching example. Norway's sovereign wealth fund proposed shifting roughly $80–85 billion out of U.S. Treasuries and into agency MBS — the mortgage-backed securities issued by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. The headline interpretation was that this would lower American mortgage rates because more demand for MBS pushes MBS prices up and yields (the spread) down. That logic is technically correct in isolation. But the video's core insight is that it ignores the other half of the equation. If selling Treasuries drives Treasury yields up, the starting benchmark rises even as the spread narrows. The all-in rate for a homeowner may not move at all — or could even rise.

"One moves up and one moves down."

Let's put actual numbers on this. Say the 10-year Treasury yield is 4.50% and the mortgage spread is 220 basis points, giving a 30-year mortgage rate of 6.70%. Norway's MBS buying compresses the spread by 20 basis points to 200 bps — positive news for borrowers in isolation. But if Norway simultaneously sells $80 billion in Treasuries, that supply pressure pushes Treasury yields up, say from 4.50% to 4.70%. Now the mortgage rate is 4.70% + 2.00% = 6.70%. Identical. The homeowner gained nothing. This is why the video argues the financial media "got the story completely backwards" — headline writers focused on one variable while ignoring the offsetting movement in the other.

Traders and investors use the Treasury-to-mortgage spread as a practical signal in several ways. MBS traders track the spread constantly — when it widens beyond historical norms, MBS are "cheap" relative to Treasuries and represent a buying opportunity; when tight, the opposite. Mortgage originators use it to price rate locks. Fed watchers scrutinize it because the Federal Reserve directly purchased MBS during quantitative easing programs specifically to compress the spread and lower consumer mortgage rates. When the Fed stopped buying and started shrinking its balance sheet (quantitative tightening), the spread widened, contributing to the mortgage rate spike in 2022–2023. Watching the spread tells you how much of any mortgage rate move is a Treasury story versus a credit/liquidity story.

The most common misconception about mortgage rates is treating them as synonymous with Fed policy. The Federal Reserve controls the federal funds rate — overnight bank lending — not the 10-year Treasury or the mortgage spread directly. The Fed can cut rates aggressively and mortgage rates can still rise if inflation expectations push Treasury yields up, or if credit concerns widen the spread. This happened visibly in late 2024 when the Fed cut rates and mortgage rates actually increased. A second misconception is the one the video highlights: assuming that any new buyer of MBS automatically helps homeowners. It only helps if the MBS demand isn't offset by Treasury selling — and for large sovereign funds, those two trades often happen simultaneously as part of a single reallocation.

The spread can break down in severe stress scenarios. During the 2008 financial crisis, the mortgage spread exploded as investors panicked about default risk inside MBS pools — particularly non-agency MBS backed by subprime loans. Spreads that had run 150–200 basis points over Treasuries blew out past 500 basis points as trust in the underlying collateral collapsed. Even agency MBS (backed by government-sponsored enterprises) saw spread widening as liquidity evaporated. This is when the Fed stepped in as a buyer of last resort, explicitly buying MBS to force spreads back down. The lesson: the spread is not just a passive arithmetic gap — it encodes the market's live assessment of housing credit risk, prepayment uncertainty, and the depth of the secondary market for mortgage debt.

Understanding the mortgage spread connects directly to several adjacent concepts. Prepayment risk is a core reason the spread exists at all — when rates fall, homeowners refinance, returning principal to MBS investors earlier than expected, forcing reinvestment at lower yields. Investors demand compensation for that uncertainty upfront. The spread also connects to duration risk: MBS have uncertain effective duration because prepayment speeds change with rates, making them harder to hedge than plain-vanilla bonds. And it links to sovereign wealth fund strategy — the Norway story is ultimately about how the world's largest pools of capital make allocation decisions that ripple through interconnected markets, where selling one asset class and buying another doesn't happen in a vacuum. In a globally integrated bond market, every large move has an equal and opposite reaction somewhere else in the yield curve.
