CPI-W vs. CPI-E (Wage-Earner vs. Elderly Price Indexes)

A price index tracks what a fixed basket of goods and services costs over time, so you can say prices rose 3% without listing every item. The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) publishes several of them. The CPI-W is the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers. It is built from the spending of households where most income comes from wages and at least one person works in a clerical or similar job. The CPI-E is the "Experimental" index for the Elderly, which reweights the basket to reflect households headed by someone 62 or older. Both measure inflation. They differ in who they imagine is doing the shopping, and that difference matters because Social Security uses one of them.

It matters because the CPI-W sets the cost-of-living adjustment (COLA), the annual raise for about 71.5 million Americans on Social Security. A choice of basket sounds technical, but it decides how many dollars arrive each month for decades. If the index understates the inflation retirees feel, their purchasing power erodes a little every year, and no law or vote is needed to make it happen. Beyond Social Security, CPI-W also adjusts federal retirement benefits and some tax and contract provisions. Whoever is arguing about "the right inflation number" is arguing about how much money moves from taxpayers to beneficiaries, and in which direction the error falls.

Mechanically, the COLA works like this. Each month the BLS publishes the CPI-W level. The Social Security Administration averages the readings for July, August and September, then compares that average with the same three months a year earlier. The percentage increase, rounded to one decimal, becomes the COLA, announced in October and paid starting in January. The video lays out how thin the margin gets: July's CPI-W was up about 3.4% year over year and August's about 3.5%, so September decides it. If September matches August, the COLA rounds to 3.5%. If prices are flat from August, it rounds down to 3.4%. A tenth of a percentage point moves roughly $2 a month for the average retiree.

The difference between the two indexes comes from the weights. Wage earners spend relatively more on transportation, apparel, and work-related items. Households headed by someone 62 or older spend a bigger share on medical care and housing, and less on things like commuting. If medical costs and shelter rise faster than gasoline and clothing, the CPI-E climbs faster than the CPI-W. If gasoline spikes, the reverse can happen. Neither index is "wrong." Each correctly measures a basket, and the question is whose basket resembles a typical retiree's. The BLS still labels the CPI-E a research series, which means it is published for study and not used in law.

The video gives concrete numbers. According to the Senior Citizens League, the CPI-E would have produced a larger COLA in seven of the last ten years. This year's actual COLA was 2.8%, while the elderly index would have delivered 3.1%. On the other side, in 2022 and 2023 the official CPI-W actually paid retirees more, because energy-driven inflation weighs more heavily in the worker basket. Over the decade, the gap averages only about 0.2 percentage points a year. The host's framing is memorable: it's not a conspiracy but a slow leak, and a small leak compounded over a 20- or 30-year retirement is still a leak.

Here is how to see the compounding. Take a benefit of $2,880 a month, near the video's average retired worker figure. Suppose true retiree inflation runs 0.2 points a year faster than the COLA you receive. After 10 years you are about 2% behind where you would have been, roughly $55 a month. After 25 years the shortfall approaches 5%, close to $140 a month, every month, for the rest of your life. It is not dramatic in any single year, which is why it goes unnoticed. For a couple depending on Social Security for most of their income, that is a real change in what the benefit buys, particularly because healthcare is the category that usually pulls ahead.

"The government calls that index a research series. And in 2022 and 23, the official gauge actually gave retirees more. So this isn't a conspiracy... it's more like a slow leak."

How do investors and planners use this? First, as a planning input: do not assume the COLA keeps you whole. Many advisers model retiree expenses with inflation of a point or so above headline, especially for medical costs, and treat the COLA as a floor, not a guarantee. Second, as a signal: watch the monthly CPI release, especially the September report, because it fixes the COLA and therefore a large piece of retirement income and consumer demand. Third, compare CPI-W and CPI-E yourself on the BLS site. When the CPI-E runs persistently hotter, medical and shelter inflation is the cause, which can matter for healthcare and senior-housing investments. Also, TIPS and other inflation-linked instruments track CPI-U, not CPI-W, so they are another slightly different yardstick.

The nuances are where people get tripped up. The CPI-E is not "the real inflation number." It is built from far smaller samples, since the BLS reweights data collected for the general index, so it is noisier and the BLS does not claim it is a better measure for the elderly. Housing treatment matters too: many retirees own homes without a mortgage, so a shelter-heavy weight may overstate their actual exposure. Also, the COLA is backward-looking. As the video says, it is "a receipt," repaying price increases already suffered, such as gasoline at roughly $4.36 versus about $3.08 a year earlier by the video's numbers, and it arrives only in January. And a bigger COLA is not a bigger net raise: Medicare Part B premiums and taxes take their share first.

What happens when the measure fails? The index can miss in either direction, and it can miss for an individual even if it is right on average. A retiree with heavy prescription costs or rising rent can feel inflation far above the published COLA. Because the COLA is a single national number, there is no adjustment for your own basket. Interactions make it worse: a larger COLA raises provisional income, and the tax thresholds set in 1983 ($25,000 single, $32,000 married) and 1993 ($34,000 and $44,000) are not indexed, so inflation pushes more benefits into taxation. Medicare's hold-harmless rule protects only when the COLA is tiny. Meanwhile the 2026 trustees report warns the combined trust funds can run dry in 2034, with about 83% of scheduled benefits payable, which puts pressure on the formula itself.

Finally, connect it to the rest of the retirement picture. The CPI-W/CPI-E debate is one piece of a chain: an inflation gauge sets the COLA, Medicare Part B premiums and IRMAA surcharges subtract from it, and the untouched 1983 thresholds tax part of what remains. Switching to the CPI-E, or to the chained CPI that Congress has floated in the opposite direction, would change that chain with a single line in the law, which is why the choice of index is a political question as much as a statistical one. The practical lesson from the video is to plan around your own spending, compare the net deposit and not the headline raise, and keep part of your portfolio growing faster than the prices you actually pay.
