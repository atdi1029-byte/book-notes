Insolvency vs. Illiquidity

Every financial failure comes down to one of two problems, and confusing them is the most common mistake people make when they read about banks. Insolvency means your liabilities are worth more than your assets. Your net worth, or equity, is negative. If you sold everything you own at fair prices and paid off everything you owe, you would still be short. Illiquidity means you cannot come up with cash right now to meet payments that are due, even though your assets are worth more than your debts. A homeowner with $400,000 of equity and an empty checking account is illiquid but not insolvent. The first is a problem of value. The second is a problem of timing. Banks can suffer from either one, and the two often feed each other.

The distinction matters because the cures differ, and because banks are built to be illiquid by design. A bank takes deposits that can be withdrawn on demand and turns them into loans and bonds that pay back over years. That maturity mismatch is the whole business. It means every bank is always somewhat illiquid, since it could never pay all depositors at once. A solvent bank that runs short of cash can borrow against its assets, from other banks, from the Federal Home Loan Bank system, or from the Fed. An insolvent bank cannot fix its problem by borrowing, because lenders know the collateral is worth less than the claims on it. Regulators, depositors, and stock investors all need to know which situation they face, and that is why the video's claim is so provocative.

Mechanically, solvency is measured by the balance sheet identity: assets minus liabilities equals equity. The accounting question is what number to put on the asset side. Under mark-to-market accounting, you value an asset at what the market would pay today. Under amortized cost accounting, you keep it at what you paid, adjusted slowly over time. The video points to ASC 320, the FASB standard that lets a bank sort bonds into two buckets. Available for sale (AFS) securities are marked to market, and the losses flow through accumulated other comprehensive income, reducing tangible equity. Held to maturity (HTM) securities are carried at amortized cost, provided management attests it has both the intent and the ability to hold them until they mature.

The bond math behind the losses is simple. A bond's price is the present value of its fixed payments, discounted at the current market yield. If a bank bought a long Treasury or mortgage-backed security paying a 2% coupon, and market yields on comparable bonds rise to 5%, no buyer will pay full price for a bond that pays 2% when 5% is available elsewhere. The price must fall until the buyer's return matches the market. The longer the bond's duration, meaning its sensitivity to rate changes, the bigger the drop. The video says a 30-year, 2% instrument can lose 30% to 40% of its value when yields surge past 5%. That is not a forecast or an opinion. It is arithmetic, and it happens whether or not the bank's accounting shows it.

Here is the video's central example. The FDIC's Quarterly Banking Profile reported that unrealized losses on securities across US commercial banks rose $19 billion in one quarter, to $325.1 billion, as the 10-year Treasury yield pushed toward 5.27%. The speaker argues that a bank holding a Treasury trading at 60 cents on the dollar can still carry it at 100 cents inside the HTM bucket. His conclusion is that banks unable to sell assets without recognizing losses are effectively insolvent despite their reported capital. Notice the phrasing. The claim is not that banks are insolvent on paper. It is that they are insolvent in economic substance, and the paper hides it.

"Once it's in that bucket, a magical statutory veil drops over the asset. Even if that 30-year Treasury is trading at 60 cents on the dollar... the bank carries it on its official regulatory balance sheet as 100 cents on the dollar."

The trap works as a chain. Depositors, especially corporate treasurers and sophisticated savers, notice that a checking account paying 0.2% to 0.4% loses to Treasury bills yielding over 4%, and they move their money. The bank needs cash to pay them. Ordinarily it would sell bonds, but selling even a small slice of the HTM portfolio can "taint" the whole portfolio, forcing the bank to reclassify and mark all of it to market. That could wipe out its regulatory capital and invite supervisors in. So the bank borrows instead, from the FHLB system or the overnight market, at 4% or more. It is now funding 2% assets with 4% liabilities. Its net interest margin turns negative, and it bleeds capital each quarter. This is how an illiquidity problem, deposit flight, is turned by accounting rules into a slow insolvency problem.

Investors and traders watch for several signals. Compare a bank's HTM and AFS unrealized losses to its tangible common equity. If the losses are a large fraction of equity, the reported capital ratio flatters reality. Watch the share of deposits that are uninsured, since those are the ones that run first. Watch growth in wholesale borrowing, which shows a bank replacing cheap deposits with expensive funding. Watch net interest margin trends and the cost of deposits. On the personal side, the video's advice follows from the same logic. Hold cash above the $250,000 FDIC limit in Treasury bills, where there is no bank counterparty risk. Check how much regional bank exposure sits inside your index funds. Draw on credit lines before a lender can freeze them.

There are real nuances, and a careful reader should hold them alongside the video's sharper claims. First, unrealized losses are only losses if they are realized. If a bank truly holds a Treasury to maturity, it gets par back, so the paper loss reverses over time. Insolvency is a claim about what would happen if the bank were forced to sell, and forced sales happen only when funding runs dry. That is why the argument depends entirely on deposit flight. A bank with stable, sticky deposits can hold its bonds for years and be fine. Second, Treasuries and agency securities carry no credit risk, so the problem is about timing and rates, not about borrowers defaulting. Third, "insolvent" has a legal meaning, and regulators judge banks on regulatory capital, not on fair-value net worth. The speaker's tone, with words like "hiding" and "chicanery," goes beyond what the disclosed numbers show. The losses are published, in the FDIC data and in the banks' own footnotes.

When it breaks, it breaks fast, because the two problems reinforce each other. Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023 is the textbook case. It held a large HTM portfolio with big unrealized losses, faced withdrawals from a concentrated base of uninsured depositors, and was forced to sell AFS bonds and realize a loss. Confidence collapsed within days, and the Fed responded with emergency lending facilities that valued the bonds at par. That response is worth remembering: when authorities lend against assets at face value, they turn a solvency question into a liquidity one and buy time. The slower version, which the video describes, is credit rationing. A bank protecting its capital shrinks lending, tightens HELOC approvals, and lowers loan-to-value limits, so the damage reaches households and small businesses long before any failure.

This concept connects to several others in the guide. Duration risk and the inverse relation between yields and bond prices explain where the losses come from. Net interest margin explains how they turn into earnings pressure. Bank runs and the maturity mismatch explain why the illiquidity comes in the first place. Lender of last resort policy explains why central banks step in and how they can blur the line between the two failure types. The working rule is to ask two questions of any financial institution. Could it survive if asked for its cash tomorrow? And would it still have positive equity if every asset were sold at today's price? A bank that answers "no" to the first has a liquidity problem. A bank that answers "no" to the second has a solvency problem. A bank that answers "no" to both is the one the video is warning about.
