Reserve Currency Status of the US Dollar

A reserve currency is a currency that governments, central banks, companies, and banks around the world hold in large quantities and use for everyday international business. The US dollar is the dominant one. It is used to invoice and settle trade, price commodities like oil and copper, denominate loans and bonds, and sit in central bank vaults as a store of value. The video puts it bluntly: over 85% of commodity trade, merchandise trade, and cross-border financing is dollar-denominated. The exact share depends on the measure, but the point holds. A Japanese insurer or a German pension fund that wants to buy assets or settle obligations abroad usually needs dollars, not yen or euros.

This matters because it makes the dollar a global utility, not just America's money. When the whole world needs dollars to function, demand for them is structural. US borrowing costs are lower than they would otherwise be, and American households and the government can finance themselves more cheaply. The flip side is that the world's financial system becomes dependent on a currency whose supply is controlled by one central bank, the Federal Reserve, and one banking system. If dollars are plentiful, global trade and credit flow easily. If they get scarce, trade finance seizes up, foreign currencies fall, and borrowers everywhere scramble. A decision in Washington can hit a Korean importer or a Brazilian company harder than it hits most Americans.

Here is the mechanical problem the video focuses on. Foreign banks and institutions need dollars, but they don't have access to American retail deposits. A bank in Frankfurt has no branch network in Ohio gathering checking accounts. So it borrows dollars in the market, and one of the biggest tools is the foreign exchange swap. The bank hands euros to a counterparty and receives dollars today. At the same time, both sides agree to reverse the trade on a set future date at a locked-in rate. In effect it is a collateralized dollar loan, and the extra cost of getting those dollars shows up in the exchange-rate terms of the swap.

The video's central claim is that this system has created a huge hidden liability. FX swaps are accounted for as derivatives, not as debt, so they don't show up in a bank's reported leverage ratios. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) has estimated that non-US institutions owe tens of trillions of dollars through these off-balance-sheet contracts. The video uses a figure of $65 trillion and says over 70% of the swaps mature in under a year, so enormous amounts must be renewed constantly. This is a maturity mismatch. Long-term assets are funded with short-term dollar borrowing that has to be rolled over, often weekly. As long as the roll works, nobody notices. The danger is in what happens when it doesn't.

"The US dollar is the indisputable reserve asset of the entire globe… If a Japanese life insurer or European pension fund wants to buy assets or match liabilities, it must transact in US dollars."

The video's "global dollar toll booth" analogy makes the squeeze concrete. Every truck on the highway must pay in physical dollars. For years a currency booth handed out dollars cheaply. Then the toll jumps and the booth closes its window. The truckers have valuable cargo but no way to pay, so they dump the cargo at steep discounts for any dollars they can find. In real markets, the booth is the FX swap market, and the price of the toll is the cross-currency basis. When that basis turns more negative, foreign banks are paying a premium over normal rates for dollars. In the video's scenario, with 10-year Treasury yields near 5.34% and the 30-year near 5.60%, US dealer banks hoard cash and stop supplying dollars cheaply abroad.

Investors can use this concept as an early-warning system. The video's playbook starts with tracking three-month and one-year euro-dollar and yen-dollar cross-currency basis spreads. When they sink deeply negative, foreign banks are desperate for dollars. Other signals include funding stress in SOFR (the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, the benchmark for overnight dollar borrowing) and usage of the Fed's standing swap lines. A rising dollar index, weakness in emerging-market currencies, and central banks selling Treasuries to defend their currencies fit the same pattern. Practically, this means checking whether international holdings are currency-hedged, because a rising dollar erodes unhedged foreign returns, and keeping some emergency money in short-term T-bills or Treasury-only money market funds.

There are several nuances and common misconceptions. First, "reserve currency" does not mean the dollar always rises or that America is immune from trouble. Second, the headline figures need care: the dollar is on one side of close to 90% of FX trades, but it is a smaller share, roughly half, of trade invoicing, and about 57–60% of disclosed central bank reserves. The "85%" is a rough statement, not a precise statistic. Third, the large FX swap number is a gross notional amount, meaning the face value of contracts, not a measure of losses or of money that is simply missing. Most of it is routine hedging and is largely offset by foreign banks' dollar assets, so it is a pressure point, not a guaranteed crash. Finally, the claim that every opening-bell dip comes from foreign bank selling is an overreach; many things move stocks.

What happens when it breaks? The 2008 financial crisis and the March 2020 pandemic panic are the textbook cases. In both, dollar funding dried up around the world, the dollar spiked, and foreign institutions sold anything liquid, including Treasuries and stocks, to raise cash. The system's safety valve is the central bank liquidity swap line. The Fed gives the Bank of Japan, the European Central Bank, the Bank of England, and others dollars in exchange for their currencies, and those central banks lend the dollars on to local banks. The video presents this as "bailing out" foreign institutions, but its real purpose is to stop an offshore freeze from spilling back into US credit markets. The risk is that stress arrives faster than the backstop, or that markets lose faith in it.

This concept connects to many others. The cross-currency basis is the price gauge of dollar scarcity, while FX swaps and off-balance-sheet leverage are the plumbing. Repo markets and SOFR show how tight domestic dollar funding is, and Treasury yields reflect how much the world values holding dollar safe assets. Currency hedging determines whether a rising dollar hurts your international investments. Over the long run, talk of "de-dollarization" asks whether anyone can replace this network. So far the answer is no: the dollar's advantages are deep, liquid markets, rule of law, and sheer habit. Understanding it means seeing the dollar as global infrastructure, one that is cheap and stable until the day it is suddenly scarce.
