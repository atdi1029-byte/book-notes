Business Development Company (BDC)

A Business Development Company (BDC) is a special type of closed-end investment fund created by Congress in 1980 specifically to channel capital into small and mid-sized American businesses that can't easily access traditional bank loans or public bond markets. Think of a BDC as a publicly traded lending machine: it raises money from ordinary investors on a stock exchange, then turns around and deploys that capital as loans or equity investments into private companies. The structural genius — and the structural risk — is that BDCs are required by law to distribute at least 90% of their taxable income to shareholders as dividends, which is why they often advertise eye-catching yields of 8%, 10%, or even higher. That income comes primarily from interest payments on the loans they make.

BDCs matter because they sit at the center of what's become a $1.4 trillion private credit market — the vast shadow banking ecosystem that now provides roughly 10% of all U.S. non-financial corporate debt and a staggering 33% of all below-investment-grade debt. When traditional banks pulled back from risky lending after the 2008 financial crisis (partly due to tighter regulation), BDCs and private credit funds rushed in to fill the gap. They became the primary lenders to thousands of leveraged buyouts, growth-stage companies, and middle-market businesses across America. That means conditions inside BDCs ripple outward: rising defaults affect jobs, business expansions, and the retirement accounts of millions of ordinary Americans who own BDC shares, sometimes without even knowing it.

Mechanically, a BDC raises equity capital through a public stock offering, then typically borrows additional money at lower rates to amplify its lending capacity — a process called leverage. It deploys this combined pool into a portfolio of loans, usually senior secured debt at floating interest rates tied to benchmarks like SOFR. Because borrowers are typically sub-investment-grade companies, the BDC charges high spreads — often 10% to 14% annually — to compensate for default risk. Each quarter, the BDC receives interest payments, covers its own borrowing costs and management fees, and distributes the remainder as dividends. Crucially, BDCs must also mark their loan portfolios to fair value every quarter, which means independent valuation firms assess what each loan would actually sell for in the current market — not just what the borrower owes on paper.

The fair-value marking process is where BDCs become a transparent window into private credit stress — and where the current crisis becomes visible. A Reuters audit of 44 BDCs revealed that at the close of Q2 2026, their combined portfolio was worth $92.88 billion against an original cost of $95.19 billion — roughly 97 cents on the dollar. Six months earlier, that ratio was closer to 99 cents. That 2-cent drop represents approximately $1.68 billion in lost value in 180 days. More alarming, non-accrual loans — where borrowers have stopped making timely interest payments — jumped from 2.5% to 3.4% of portfolio cost in the same period. A separate audit of 35 rated private credit funds confirmed the same trend, with median non-accrual rates surging from 1.81% in Q1 to 2.75% by June 30th.

To understand why the software sector specifically triggered this deterioration, you need to understand the underwriting assumption that broke. For a decade, BDCs lent aggressively to per-seat SaaS (Software-as-a-Service) companies — firms whose revenue model charges customers a fixed monthly fee per user or "seat." Lenders treated this as a near-annuity: if a company employs 10,000 workers, they pay for 10,000 licenses, month after month, predictably. BDCs underwrote these loans at 7x EBITDA with 12% floating rates, treating the seat count as a stable floor. Then AI agents arrived. When five autonomous AI agents can replace the workflow of 50 human employees, enterprise clients don't cancel the software tool — they cancel 45 of the seats. HubSpot's Q2 2026 earnings illustrated this precisely: revenue up 20%, customers up 14%, but average revenue per customer up only 4%. Even a market leader is losing pricing power per user faster than it gains new users.

The two largest BDCs in the world illustrate the real-world damage. Ares Capital Corporation, the largest BDC globally, disclosed that just two individual software loan exposures accounted for more than 33.3% of its entire net unrealized losses for the year. Oaktree Specialty Lending went further: management disclosed that 26% of its entire portfolio carries some degree of AI disruption risk and proactively cut the fair value of its performing software loans by 3%. Note that word "performing" — these aren't loans where the borrower has missed a payment. They're loans where the lender is preemptively writing down value because the business model underpinning repayment capacity has structurally weakened. One valuation firm's sector breakdown confirmed the scale: 81% of the entire software book across private credit was marked down in 2026, versus only 40% of non-tech borrowers.

The most insidious mechanism hiding this deterioration from retail investors is Payment-in-Kind (PIK) income. When a borrower can't generate enough cash to pay interest, some loan agreements allow them to instead add the unpaid interest to the outstanding loan balance — essentially borrowing to pay the interest. The BDC records this accrued interest as revenue on its income statement and pays it out as part of your dividend. No cash ever changed hands, but the fund's books show income and the shareholder receives a yield check. According to Federal Reserve Bank of Boston research, the share of private credit loans using PIK structures exploded from roughly 6% in early 2022 to over 10% by early 2026 — a 67% surge in three years. A BDC paying a 10% dividend that derives more than 5% of its investment income from PIK is essentially manufacturing yield through accounting rather than cash generation.

When BDC stress crosses a threshold, the structure can create a redemption gate trap for investors. Unlike a stock you can sell at any moment, many semi-liquid private credit vehicles — including perpetual BDCs ($36 billion in assets) and interval funds ($119 billion) — restrict how much money investors can withdraw each quarter, typically capping redemptions at 5% of shares outstanding per quarter. In Q1 2026, accepted redemption requests across these vehicles exceeded new investor inflows for the first time in history. Blackstone's $77.2 billion private credit fund BCR faced $4.3 billion in redemption requests — roughly 10% of shares — against a 5% quarterly cap, while new subscriptions had slowed to just $750 million. When the gate closes, your exit becomes a queue, not a decision. Fitch Ratings now puts the private credit default rate at a record 6%, and if PIK-dependent yields get cut, the investors who stay behind may find themselves holding a smaller piece of a deteriorating book while earlier institutional investors have already escaped.

The broader connection that makes this more than a niche finance story is the Department of Labor's proposed safe harbor rule, introduced in March 2026, which would give retirement plan managers legal cover to include private credit products — including BDC debt sleeves — inside 401(k) plans and target-date retirement funds. The public comment period closed in June 2026. The timing is striking: at the precise moment institutional investors are hitting redemption gates and exiting gated private credit funds, a regulatory change is being prepared that would open the retirement savings of working Americans as a new source of inflows. Understanding BDCs means understanding that the dividend yield advertised on the front page is only as reliable as the cash actually flowing from the borrowers behind it — and right now, 81% of the software book has already been quietly marked down while the yield checks keep arriving in mailboxes.

"Wall Street didn't misprice artificial intelligence. It never priced it at all."

For any investor holding a BDC or interval fund today, three diagnostics matter immediately. First, pull the Schedule of Investments from the fund's most recent SEC filing — the summary prospectus won't show you sector-level markdowns, but the raw schedule will. Second, calculate what percentage of total investment income is PIK; anything above 5% means the fund is borrowing against the future to pay your present dividend. Third, read the redemption terms in full before a crisis forces you to read them under pressure. A quarterly 5% repurchase gate sounds abstract until the headline hits and you discover the exit line stretches for years. BDCs are not inherently dangerous instruments — Congress designed them to serve a real economic function — but like any high-yield vehicle, the yield is the price of the risk, and the risk right now is hiding in plain sight on page 190 of the SEC filing nobody was supposed to read.
