Interest Income on Cash Holdings

Interest income is the money a company earns simply by parking its cash somewhere that pays a return. A company with a large pile of cash and cash equivalents (bank deposits, money market funds, Treasury bills maturing in under three months) and short-term investments (slightly longer-dated Treasuries, commercial paper, high-grade corporate bonds) doesn't leave it idle. It lends it out, indirectly, to governments, banks and strong corporations, and collects interest. Think of your own savings account, scaled up by a factor of millions. On the income statement, this return doesn't appear as revenue. It shows up below the operating line, in a section usually labeled "other income" or "interest income, net," because it comes from owning money, not from running the business.

It matters because it changes how you read profitability. A company's operating income tells you whether the core business, selling products and paying the costs of making and marketing them, makes money. Interest income tells you whether the balance sheet makes money. The two are separate engines, and they respond to different forces. Operations respond to customers, competition and costs. Interest income responds to the size of the cash pile and to interest rates set by the central bank. When rates are high, a big cash hoard can quietly pay for a lot of expenses. When rates fall to near zero, the same hoard earns almost nothing, and a business that leaned on that income suddenly looks worse without anything in operations having changed.

Mechanically, the calculation is simple: interest income ≈ average cash balance × average yield × time. If a company holds $10 billion in Treasury bills yielding 4.5% for a full year, it earns about $450 million. The accountant records it as it accrues, meaning day by day as the interest is earned, even if the cash payment arrives later. Bills bought at a discount are accreted, so the gap between purchase price and face value is recognized gradually as income. Two levers drive the result: how much cash there is, and what it earns. Note that "average" matters. A company that raises $20 billion in March earns far less in that year than one that held it since January, so interest income often lags the headline cash figure.

The video gives a vivid real-world case. As of the end of June, Anthropic held $31.7 billion in cash, cash equivalents and short-term investments. In just six months, that pile earned about $1.1 billion of interest. Annualized, that's roughly $2.2 billion on $31.7 billion, a yield near 7%, which looks high for Treasuries. That tells you the cash balance was probably much smaller earlier in the half, with fundraising pushing it up toward June, so the average balance was lower and the true yield was more ordinary. The comparison the host drew is the point:

"It earned $1.1 billion of interest on that pile in just 6 months. That's more than its entire operating loss. The interest on its savings account covered the cost of running the business."

Anthropic's operating loss for the half was $693 million. Interest income of $1.1 billion exceeds that by roughly $400 million. In plain terms, if you add the two, the company's ordinary business plus its savings account came out ahead before the headline-grabbing accounting charges below the line (the $72.4 billion fair value change on convertible notes) are considered. That doesn't make the business profitable on its own merits, since the operating line is still negative. But it shows the company wasn't bleeding cash from operations in a way that threatened it, and that the cushion was real money, not paper.

Investors use interest income in several practical ways. First, they separate operating earnings from non-operating earnings, asking how much profit would remain if the cash pile vanished. A company showing $500 million of net income, $400 million of it from interest, is a bank account with a side business. Second, they analyze quality of earnings: interest is steady and predictable while rates hold, but it isn't a sign of business strength. Third, they check the implied yield (interest divided by average cash) as a sanity check on whether management is parking money safely or reaching for yield in riskier instruments. Fourth, in valuation, analysts often subtract cash from enterprise value so that the cash isn't counted twice, once as a balance-sheet asset and again through the income it produces.

There are several gotchas. The biggest misconception is that interest income signals a healthy business. In the video's case, the cushion is genuine, but a company can also look profitable overall while its operations lose money every quarter, hiding a deteriorating core behind a treasury desk. A second trap is treating interest income as permanent. It is rate-sensitive: if the central bank cuts rates from, say, 4.5% to 2%, the same $31.7 billion earns less than half as much. A third is forgetting that cash gets spent. Burning billions on chips and data centers shrinks the balance, and interest income shrinks with it. A fourth is confusing interest income with the interest expense a company pays on its debts. Analysts often look at net interest, the difference between the two.

When it breaks, it breaks in a few ways. The most direct is a rate drop combined with a cash drawdown: income falls from both sides at once, and a company that treated the interest as a stable offset suddenly sees the operating loss show through. A second risk is credit or liquidity trouble in what the cash is invested in. In March 2023, depositors with balances far above the insured limit at Silicon Valley Bank faced real uncertainty about access to their money, a reminder that "cash" sits at a particular institution or in a particular fund. A third is the opposite problem, where lots of cash is accumulating but the business burns it faster than interest can replace it. A cushion only helps while it lasts, and the filing's enormous multi-year compute commitments show how fast obligations can outrun it.

Interest income connects to several other ideas from the video. It sits below the operating line, in the same region of the income statement where the $72.4 billion fair value change on convertible notes appeared, which is why the host kept pushing readers to look beneath the headline net loss. It differs from that charge, though: interest is real cash arriving, while the fair value mark-up is a non-cash accounting entry. It also ties to liquidity and runway, since the $31.7 billion balance is what lets the company absorb losses and fund commitments. And it links to interest rates and monetary policy, because the central bank's decisions determine what that balance earns. Together, these let you read a filing in layers: operations first, then the balance sheet's contribution, then the non-cash noise.
