Multi-Causal Market Move Attribution

Multi-causal market move attribution is the discipline of figuring out what actually caused a price move when several plausible explanations are landing on the market at the same time. A single-cause story is easy to sell on television — "stocks fell because of X" — but real sessions are rarely driven by one input. Prices are the sum of every participant repricing risk, growth expectations, interest rates, and liquidity all at once, and on most volatile days two, three, or four separate shocks arrive in the same 24-hour window. Attribution means separating those inputs and weighing how much of the move belongs to each one, rather than crediting the whole thing to whichever story is loudest or most recent.

This matters because misattributing a move leads to the wrong trade. If you believe a selloff was only about an AI industry essay, you will watch AI-adjacent stocks for a reversal signal and ignore everything else. If the real driver was a bond market shock, then chips recover only when yields recover — an AI headline fading does nothing. Getting the cause wrong means watching the wrong indicator, sizing the wrong hedge, and being surprised when the "resolution" of the story you believed in doesn't produce the price action you expected. Correct attribution is what lets a trader build a falsifiable thesis: it names ahead of time what should happen if the theory is right, so it can be checked against the tape.

Mechanically, attribution starts by identifying every candidate cause that plausibly hit the market in the relevant window, then checking each one against a cross-section of assets that would respond differently depending on which cause is dominant. A single narrative should move everything correlated to it in the same direction and leave uncorrelated assets alone. A macro shock — like a rate or oil move — should hit duration-sensitive and rate-sensitive assets broadly, regardless of sector narrative. The test is whether the pattern in the data matches the pattern you'd expect if your story were the whole explanation. Where it doesn't match, you have found evidence of a second (or third) cause operating underneath the obvious one.

The video's Monday selloff is a clean, numbers-heavy example. The headline story was that Dario Amodei's "We Must Pace the Frontier" essay, echoed within hours by Sam Altman and Elon Musk, triggered a global chip selloff — SoftBank down about 11%, Nvidia down 2.9%, the semiconductor ETF off nearly 5%. But the narrator lays three other inputs on the table from that same morning: Brent crude traded above $108 a barrel, the 10-year Treasury pushed through 5% for the first time since October 2023, and futures markets were pricing nearly 90% odds the Fed would hike rates. Those are a commodity shock and a rate shock, both capable of hitting risk assets on their own, landing on the same morning as the AI essay.

The attribution test here is exactly the cross-section check described above. If the selloff were purely an AI panic, you'd expect broad indices without heavy AI exposure to be roughly flat or unaffected, while anything AI-adjacent gets hit hard. Instead, the video points out the Dow was only down about a quarter of a percent and the Russell 2000 was actually up — a small-cap index with little AI concentration moving opposite the "AI panic" narrative. That divergence is the signature of a rate-and-oil shock sitting underneath a narrower, essay-triggered rotation inside tech, not evidence of one dominant cause sweeping the whole market. As the narrator puts it directly:

That essay landed on a sector that was already bleeding from an oil shock and rate shock.

Traders use this kind of attribution to decide which signal to actually watch going forward, rather than reacting to whichever headline is easiest to explain in a segment. The video's own forward-looking test is a good template: watch whether the AI-specific rotation — money moving out of chip names like Marvell and Lam Research and into security and enterprise software names like CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks — survives once the Fed decision is out of the way. If the rotation holds after the rate uncertainty resolves, that's confirmation the AI story was a real, independent driver. If everything snaps back once yields ease, the AI narrative was mostly noise riding on top of a rate move, not a standalone cause. Attribution isn't just diagnosis after the fact — it generates a specific, checkable prediction.

The biggest gotcha is that multiple true causes are not mutually exclusive, and conflating "multi-causal" with "therefore nothing really happened" is its own error. The video is careful about this with its CrowdStrike example: the stock was up about 15% partly on sector rotation, but also had its own catalyst — its CEO publicly pushed back on Amodei's pacing argument, and that got amplified on cable news. The narrator flags this explicitly rather than crediting the whole move to rotation: "don't read the whole move as pure rotation." A rigorous attribution has to isolate stock-specific catalysts from sector-wide ones even within a single name, or you'll overcredit a clean macro story with noise that has nothing to do with it.

Attribution breaks down when correlated inputs move together often enough that you stop bothering to separate them, or when you anchor on the first plausible explanation and stop looking — what the video calls mistaking "oil and rates day wearing an artificial intelligence costume." The risk of getting this wrong compounds because the wrong attribution produces a wrong hedge: someone convinced the entire move was an AI capex panic would hedge with AI-specific instruments, missing that duration risk from the 10-year and energy costs from Brent were doing real work too. This concept connects directly to reading cross-asset divergence (Dow versus Russell 2000, chips versus software) as a diagnostic tool, and to distinguishing a genuine sector rotation from a broad-based risk-off macro shock — two patterns that can look identical in a single index chart but demand completely different trades.
