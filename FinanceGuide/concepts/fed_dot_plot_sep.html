Fed Dot Plot & Summary of Economic Projections (SEP)

The dot plot is a chart the Federal Reserve publishes four times a year showing where each of the nineteen members of the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) thinks the federal funds rate will be at the end of the current year, the next two years, and over the "longer run." Each member gets one anonymous dot per year on the grid. The median dot — the middle value when you line up all nineteen — becomes the headline number markets react to. It's not a promise or a vote on policy; it's a snapshot of individual forecasts, most of which will be revised at the next meeting. It's published as part of the broader Summary of Economic Projections (SEP), a quarterly packet released alongside the March, June, September, and December Fed meetings.

The dot plot matters because it's the closest thing the Fed gives markets to a roadmap. Bond yields, mortgage rates, credit card APRs, and stock valuations all get priced partly off where investors expect the federal funds rate to sit in the future — not just where it is today. If the median dot shows more hikes coming than the market expected, yields tend to rise immediately because bond prices adjust to reflect a higher expected rate path. If it shows fewer hikes or earlier cuts, yields tend to fall. In the week covered by this recap, the new dot plot put the median year-end rate at 4.1%, which implied one more quarter-point hike was still coming before the year was out — a signal that rattled the bond market within hours.

Mechanically, here's what happens: at each SEP meeting, every FOMC member privately submits their own projection for the fed funds rate at each year-end, plus their own forecasts for inflation (measured by PCE, the Fed's preferred gauge), unemployment, and GDP growth. The Fed's staff plots every rate projection as an anonymous dot on a grid with years along the bottom and rate levels up the side, then publishes the chart along with a separate table showing the range and median of the inflation, unemployment, and GDP forecasts. Markets immediately compute the median dot for each year and compare it to where futures markets had already priced the rate path. The gap between the two — not the dots themselves — is usually what drives the initial market reaction.

In this specific case, the September SEP showed the committee raising its inflation forecast: PCE inflation at 3.7% for 2026, up from 3.6% projected back in June, with inflation not expected to return to the Fed's 2% target until 2029. At the same time, the SEP showed unemployment at 4.1% and GDP growth at 2.3% for the year — a combination of solid growth and low unemployment alongside inflation that's stuck well above target. That combination is exactly what justifies further hikes in the Fed's own framework: if the economy isn't slowing and prices aren't cooling, there's no economic argument for the Fed to stop raising rates. The rate hike itself — 25 basis points to a range of 3.75% to 4%, passed unanimously — was fully priced in and barely moved markets. The dot plot and the projections behind it were the surprise.

Traders and investors use the dot plot as a forward-looking input, not a lagging confirmation. The practical move is to compare the new median dot to the old one from the prior quarter's SEP, and compare both to what interest rate futures markets were already pricing before the release. If the new dots show more hikes than futures had priced, that's a hawkish surprise and yields typically rise across the curve. If they show fewer, that's dovish and yields fall. Professional traders will also study the "dot dispersion" — how spread out the nineteen dots are — because a tight cluster signals committee consensus, while a wide spread signals real disagreement about where policy is headed, which itself is useful information about how much conviction is behind the median.

The biggest misconception about the dot plot is treating it as a Fed commitment or promise. It isn't. It's simply what each individual member currently expects, given their own economic forecast, and those forecasts change constantly as new data comes in. This is exactly the distinction the Fed chair drew in the press conference following this meeting: the dots are "the forecast of his 18 colleagues," and he explicitly said he isn't "in the forward guidance business." That framing matters — it means the Fed reserves the right to hike, cut, or hold regardless of what the dots showed three months ago, and investors who trade purely off the dot plot without tracking incoming data (jobs reports, CPI, retail sales) are trading a snapshot that's already going stale.

The dot plot breaks down as a useful signal when the Fed chair explicitly declines to endorse it, which is what happened here.

Chair Kevin Walsh said he would be "hard-pressed to describe broad financial conditions as restrictive," calling the projections "the forecast of his 18 colleagues" and said he's "not in the forward guidance business."

Translation: no promises, no ceiling. When the chair refuses to anchor market expectations to the dots, the market has to find its own anchor — and in this case it found one in the bond market itself. The 10-year Treasury yield hit 5.01% that Wednesday, its highest level since 2007, and closed the week at 5%. The 2-year yield climbed from 4.63% to roughly 4.74%, a 52-week high. When the Fed won't provide forward guidance, long-term yields effectively become the market's own forward guidance — a real-time vote on where rates are headed that can move faster and further than the dots ever suggested.

The dot plot connects directly to several other core monetary policy concepts: it's the numerical expression of the Fed's forward guidance (or lack thereof, in this case), it shapes the yield curve because longer-dated Treasuries price in the expected path of short-term rates for years into the future, and it interacts with quantitative inflation data like PCE and CPI, since any dot-plot inflation forecast is only as credible as the actual prints that follow it. It also explains why a single Fed meeting can move markets more than a hike itself does — the hike is backward-looking (it already happened), while the dot plot and the chair's tone are forward-looking, and markets are pricing machines built almost entirely on expectations about the future, not confirmations of the past.
