Yield Curve Inversion

The yield curve is simply a picture of interest rates across time. Plot the yield on a 3-month Treasury bill on the left, work your way out to the 2-year note, then the 5-year, the 10-year, and the 30-year bond on the right, and you have the curve. Normally it slopes upward — lenders demand more interest for locking money away longer, because more can go wrong over a decade than over a quarter. A yield curve inversion happens when that slope flips: shorter-maturity bonds start paying more than longer ones. When a 2-year Treasury yields more than a 10-year Treasury, investors are effectively saying they expect rates to fall sharply in the future — which historically means they expect the economy to weaken.

Why does inversion matter? Because it has an almost eerily reliable track record of preceding recessions. Every U.S. recession since 1955 has been preceded by an inversion of the yield curve, typically by six to eighteen months. The mechanism is partly self-fulfilling: when banks can borrow short-term at high rates but only lend long-term at lower rates, their net interest margin — the profit spread between what they pay depositors and what they earn on loans — collapses. Lending becomes unprofitable. Credit tightens. Businesses can't fund growth. Consumers can't buy homes. The economy slows. The curve isn't predicting a recession so much as it's describing the conditions that tend to create one.

The mechanics work through expectations. The 10-year Treasury yield is not set by the Fed directly — it reflects what the bond market collectively expects short-term rates to average over the next decade. If investors believe the Fed will be forced to cut rates aggressively in the coming years because growth will stall, they're willing to accept a lower yield today to lock in that rate before cuts arrive. Meanwhile, the 2-year Treasury hugs the current Fed funds rate closely, because two years is short enough that today's policy dominates the math. When the Fed hikes aggressively and the market simultaneously prices in future cuts, the 2-year rises faster than the 10-year — and the curve flattens, or inverts.

Here's a concrete example from the video's own data. After the Fed's unanimous rate hike on September 16th — raising the fed funds rate to 3.75–4% — the 2-year Treasury jumped from 4.67% to 4.74%, while the 10-year barely moved from 5.00% to 5.01%. That compressed the 2y-10y spread from roughly 33 basis points to 27 basis points. Headlines immediately screamed "yield curve flattening" as a warning sign. But the spread was still positive: the 10-year was still paying more than the 2-year. Flattening is not inversion. The curve was moving in the wrong direction, but it had not crossed the line that historically signals recession.

This is where the nuance that the video hammers becomes critical. There are multiple yield curve spreads traders watch, and they are not interchangeable. The spread that has the strongest empirical track record as a recession signal is the 3-month vs. 10-year spread, not the 2-year vs. 10-year. Research from the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco has consistently shown the 3m-10y spread outperforms as a forecasting tool. On the day the video was recorded, that spread stood at positive 82 basis points — meaning the 10-year was paying 0.82% more than the 3-month bill. By this measure, the curve was not even close to inverted, regardless of what the flattening 2y-10y narrative implied.

Traders use the yield curve as both a macro signal and a direct trading vehicle. A curve steepener trade profits when the spread widens — the long end rises faster than the short end, or the short end falls faster. A flattener trade profits from compression. Macro funds track the 3m-10y spread religiously because inversion has historically given 6–18 months of lead time before a recession officially begins — enough runway to reduce equity exposure, extend bond duration, rotate into defensive sectors, or buy long-dated Treasuries before they rally as rates eventually fall. The practical signal is not "sell everything the day it inverts" but rather "start positioning for a slowdown within the next year to eighteen months."

The most common misconception — the one the video explicitly sets out to kill — is conflating curve flattening with inversion, and conflating one spread with another. When the 2y-10y spread compresses from 33bp to 27bp, that is newsworthy, but it is categorically different from the spread going negative. A second misconception is treating all inversions as identical. The 2019 inversion was brief and shallow; the 2022–2023 inversion was deep and prolonged, with the 2-year at times yielding more than 100 basis points above the 10-year. Depth and duration both matter for interpreting severity. A third misconception is timing: the recession doesn't start the day the curve inverts — it typically starts after the curve re-steepens, which often happens as rate cuts arrive too late to prevent the downturn.

Inversions can also mislead when structural factors distort the long end of the curve. Quantitative easing — the Fed's large-scale bond purchases — artificially suppressed 10-year yields for years, making the curve flatter than pure rate expectations would suggest. Foreign demand for U.S. Treasuries (from Japan, China, and central banks seeking safe assets) similarly pushes long yields down regardless of U.S. economic conditions. The video notes that the current 10-year around 5% reflects an unusually fat term premium — the extra yield investors demand for holding long-duration bonds — partly driven by oil prices and supply concerns. Strip that out and the underlying rate expectation picture changes. Context always matters: the yield curve is a powerful signal, but it operates within a broader framework that includes Fed policy, global capital flows, and inflation expectations all at once.

"The spread that actually matters for recession signals — that's the 3 months against the 10-year. It's positive by about 82 basis points at the moment. Anybody telling you this week put us near inversion has not pulled the curve."

The yield curve connects directly to mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs, and bank profitability — which is why it sits at the center of so much market commentary. The video makes a crucial distinction: the Fed controls the overnight rate, but your mortgage is priced off the 10-year Treasury. These are not the same number, and you cannot skip the step between them. Historically, the 10-year runs about one percentage point above the Fed funds rate, and the mortgage spread — the extra yield lenders demand above the 10-year — runs another 1.8 to 2 points. Knowing where the yield curve sits, and whether it's steepening or flattening, tells you far more about where mortgage rates are headed than any single Fed meeting announcement ever will.
