Medicare Open Enrollment and Medicare Advantage

Medicare Open Enrollment is the yearly window, October 15 through December 7, when anyone already on Medicare can review and change their coverage for the following calendar year. Medicare itself is the federal health insurance program for people 65 and older, and for some younger people with disabilities. It comes in two basic flavors. Original Medicare (Part A for hospital care, Part B for doctor visits and outpatient care) is run directly by the government and lets you see almost any provider who accepts Medicare. Medicare Advantage (Part C) is a different route: private insurers contract with the government to deliver your Part A and B benefits through their own plan, usually with a network of doctors, and often with a drug plan bundled in. Open enrollment is the moment you can switch between these routes or change plans within one.

This matters because health care is one of the largest and least predictable costs in retirement, and retirees spend a bigger share of their budget on it than working households do. The video makes the point that the Social Security cost of living adjustment (COLA) is measured on a basket of goods built for workers, not retirees, so a cost that grows faster for seniors, like health care, quietly eats the raise. Open enrollment is one of the few levers a retiree actually controls. The choices made in these seven weeks ripple outward: they decide how much cash leaves your household each month, and, in aggregate, they direct hundreds of billions of federal dollars toward private insurers, which is why Medicare Advantage is a major force in the health insurance and hospital industries.

Mechanically, the process works like this. In the fall, each plan publishes its next-year details: monthly premium, deductible, copays, drug list (called a formulary), and network of doctors. Existing members get an Annual Notice of Change spelling out what is shifting. Between October 15 and December 7 you can enroll in, drop, or swap a Medicare Advantage plan, move from Advantage back to Original Medicare, or add or change a standalone Part D drug plan. Whatever you pick on or before December 7 takes effect January 1. If you do nothing, you are generally auto-renewed into your current plan on whatever new terms it published, which is the single most common and most expensive way to "decide."

The video gives the key number: CMS projects that the average Medicare Advantage premium will fall to about $12 a month next year from about $14.37. That looks like good news, and on average it is a small one, roughly $2.37 a month or about $28 a year. But notice how small that is next to the other numbers in the story: the Part B premium is projected to rise from $22.90 to about $29.50 a month, and the Part B deductible from $283 to $292. The Advantage premium is only one line on the bill. A plan with a $0 premium can still carry a high deductible, steep specialist copays, or a drug list that excludes your medications, which is where the real money goes.

Which brings us to the advice in the video, which is the heart of the matter:

"That average will not be your plan. Compare your actual drugs, your actual doctors, and your actual costs."

In practice, that means a three-part checklist. First, your drugs: enter every prescription, with dose and pharmacy, into the Medicare Plan Finder at Medicare.gov and see what each plan would charge you for the full year, not just the monthly premium. Second, your doctors: confirm each specialist, hospital, and clinic is in the plan's network, since a plan can be cheap precisely because it is narrow. Third, your costs: add premium, deductible, copays, and the plan's out-of-pocket maximum (the yearly cap on what you pay) to estimate your realistic total under a healthy year and a bad one. An hour of this homework is, as the video puts it, one of the few places in retirement where effort turns directly into money.

There are real gotchas. First, a low premium is not a low cost: Advantage plans often advertise $0 premiums because the insurer recovers its costs through copays and network limits. Second, you still pay your Part B premium to Medicare in nearly all cases, even on an Advantage plan; the Advantage premium is on top of it. Third, Part B is usually deducted straight from your Social Security check, which is why the video calls Medicare the first "hand" reaching into your COLA. Fourth, the hold harmless rule keeps a Part B increase from exceeding your COLA dollar increase, but it only matters when the COLA is small, and it doesn't protect higher earners or new enrollees. Fifth, Advantage and Original Medicare are not interchangeable: Original Medicare has no network, while Advantage often requires referrals or prior approval.

The biggest risks come from switching without thinking, or not switching at all. If your doctor leaves a plan's network or a drug moves to a higher cost tier, you can be stuck with the plan until the next open enrollment, with few exceptions. Another trap is one-way doors: if you leave Original Medicare for Advantage and later want to go back, you may be able to return to Original Medicare easily, but buying a Medigap supplement policy afterward can require medical underwriting in many states, meaning you could be denied or charged more. There is also a separate Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period in early in the year for people already in an Advantage plan, but it is narrower, so December 7 is the real deadline to plan around.

Income also plays a hidden role through IRMAA, the income-related monthly adjustment amount. The video warns that this surcharge is based on your income from two years earlier. Starting above roughly $109,000 for a single person or $218,000 for a couple, the standard Part B premium jumps from $22.90 to about $284.10 a month, and a surcharge is added to your drug plan as well. Notably, that surcharge applies on top of whatever plan you choose, so open enrollment shopping doesn't remove it. What does help is planning large income events, such as selling a house, a big IRA withdrawal, or a Roth conversion, with the two-year lookback in mind.

Connecting it all: open enrollment sits at the intersection of the video's three "hands" on your COLA. Medicare premiums come out first, taxes on benefits (the 1983 and 1993 thresholds on provisional income) come next, and an inflation index that undercounts senior costs sets the size of the raise to begin with. The average retiree's roughly $73 monthly raise shrinks to about $66 after the projected Part B increase alone. Shopping for the right plan is the one defensive move that is fully in your hands and needs no act of Congress. For investors, it also shows how retirement planning is really cash-flow planning: a predictable health care budget, paired with a portfolio that can grow faster than the prices you pay, is what keeps a "late and partial refund of inflation" from becoming a shortfall.
