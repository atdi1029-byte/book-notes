Dry Powder (Uninvested Cash Reserves)

Dry powder is a term borrowed from old warfare. Soldiers had to keep their gunpowder dry, because wet powder wouldn't fire when the fight came. In finance it means cash, or cash-like assets, that you have deliberately left uninvested so it is ready to deploy when an opportunity appears. It isn't cash you forgot about, and it isn't your everyday checking balance. It is capital with a job: waiting. Private equity firms report their dry powder as committed but unspent capital. Individual investors use the same idea for the slice of their portfolio held in money-market funds or Treasury bills (short-term US government debt) so they can buy when prices fall instead of selling something else to raise money.

It matters because markets rarely give you good prices and good timing at once. The best bargains show up during stress, when credit freezes, forced sellers dump assets, and fear pushes prices below what the underlying businesses are worth. Those are the moments when almost everyone is short of cash. The people who can buy are the ones who prepared in advance. Dry powder is a bet on optionality, the ability to act rather than react. It also shapes the wider market: when large pools of dry powder sit on the sidelines, they put a soft floor under prices in a selloff, because buyers eventually step in. When that cash is depleted, declines can run much further.

Mechanically, the process has four steps. First, you decide how much of your portfolio to hold in reserve, and you take it out of the assets you expect to fluctuate. Second, you park it somewhere that pays interest, holds its value, and can be sold quickly. Third, you define in advance what counts as an opportunity: a price drop of a certain size, a valuation level, or a specific event. Fourth, when the trigger arrives, you move the money into the target asset in stages. The video's version is simple. Hold the reserve in 1 to 3 month T-bills, which mature so quickly that each one hands back your principal at par in weeks. If you wait, you simply roll the proceeds into a new bill and keep collecting yield.

The choice of T-bills is deliberate, and the video spells out the logic. Short Treasury bills have no meaningful credit risk, because the US government issues them. They have almost no interest rate risk, because a bill maturing in 30 to 90 days can't lose much value when rates move. And they carry no refinancing risk, meaning your money isn't dependent on some borrower being able to roll its own debt. The speaker puts it this way:

"Maintain your core dry powder in direct 1 to 3 month US Treasury bills. That paper gives you unencumbered liquidity, zero refinancing contagion, and the buying power to acquire high-quality distressed assets at discounts when the refinancing cliff breaks."

Now apply it to the scenario in the video. The claim is that roughly $1.8 trillion of corporate debt issued at 2% to 3% in 2020 and 2021 comes due between late 2026 and the end of 2027, and must be refinanced at 7.5% to 9%. The video's example: a company with $500 million of debt pays $12.5 million a year in interest at 2.5%, but about $42.5 million a year at 8.5%, a $30 million drain on cash flow. Companies that can't absorb that may cut dividends, lay off staff, or file for Chapter 11. Their bonds, loans, and shares can then sell at steep discounts. An investor holding T-bills when that happens has cash in hand while others are scrambling. That is the "buy distressed assets at discounts" setup the video describes. Note that this is the video's forecast, not a certainty.

Investors use dry powder in a few practical ways. The simplest is a fixed target, say 10% to 20% of the portfolio, which you rebuild after each deployment. A more active approach is a laddered deployment: you commit to buying in tranches, for example a third when the market is down 15%, another third at 25%, and the rest at 35%. That guards against the mistake of spending everything on the first dip. Signals people watch include widening credit spreads, a rising rate of high-yield defaults, and forced selling such as fund redemptions. The video's own screens, like interest coverage below 3 to 1, help you decide which names to buy once the cliff breaks. They also help you decide which ones to avoid even at a discount.

The gotchas are significant. The biggest is cash drag: if markets keep rising while you wait, your reserve earns T-bill yield and misses the equity return, and that cost can compound for years. The second is that "cheap" is not the same as "bottom." Distressed assets often get cheaper, and an investor who deploys early can watch a 30% discount become 60%. The third is a common misconception that dry powder is a prediction. It isn't. It is insurance plus an option, and it pays off even if you never use it, because it keeps you from being a forced seller. The fourth is that T-bills carry reinvestment risk: when each bill matures, the new one may pay less if the Fed cuts rates. Finally, the interest is taxable at the federal level, though exempt from state and local income tax.

Dry powder can fail in several ways. The most common is behavioral. The cash is there, but when the crash comes, fear stops you from using it. Plenty of investors have said "I'll buy when it's cheap" and then sat frozen at the bottom, waiting for more certainty that never arrived. The second is inflation: if prices rise faster than T-bill yields for a long period, the reserve loses purchasing power while it waits. The third is that the event simply doesn't happen. If companies refinance successfully, or growth lifts earnings enough to cover the higher interest, no bargain appears, and you've held lower-returning assets for the whole stretch. The fourth is size: too small a reserve can't buy anything meaningful, and too large a reserve becomes a drag that quietly wrecks your long-term return.

Dry powder connects to several other ideas in this guide. It is the practical response to refinancing risk and the maturity wall, since it is how you take advantage of the stress those create. It pairs with the interest coverage ratio and zombie company screens, which tell you which stocks to sell now and which distressed names might be worth buying later. It relates to liquidity risk, because T-bills can be sold in any market, while corporate credit funds and high-yield pools can freeze exactly when you need them. It also sits next to asset allocation and rebalancing: reserve cash is one asset class with a specific job. Used with discipline, it turns a market panic from a threat into a chance to buy.

One last point: dry powder is separate from your emergency fund. Your emergency fund covers job loss, medical bills, and car repairs, and you shouldn't spend it on investments. The video's "contingent emergency capital" wording blurs the line, but the two do different jobs. An emergency fund protects your household from forced selling in your own life. Dry powder is the extra layer you hold for opportunity. Keep them mentally and, ideally, in separate accounts, so a tempting market dip never talks you into spending the money that is keeping your life stable. Size the emergency fund first, then decide how much optionality you can afford to carry on top of it.
