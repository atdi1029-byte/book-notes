Preferred Stock (Preferred Shares)

Preferred stock is a hybrid security that sits between a company's bonds and its common stock. When you buy a share, you own a claim on the company, but it is a different kind of claim from the one common shareholders hold. A preferred share typically pays a fixed dividend, set as a percentage of a stated face value, usually $25 or $1,000 per share. The dividend works like a bond coupon: it is predictable, and it is paid before the company pays anything to common shareholders. In exchange for that priority and steady income, preferred holders generally give up voting rights and most of the upside. The company's stock can double and your preferred share will mostly sit still.

Preferreds matter because they are the quiet plumbing of the financial system, especially for banks. After 2008, regulators decided banks needed thicker cushions of loss-absorbing capital so that taxpayers and depositors would not be first in line to eat losses. Common equity is the best cushion but the most expensive to raise, because issuing new common shares dilutes existing owners. Preferred stock is cheaper and does not dilute ownership. It is also a major income product: many retirees, income funds, and ETFs hold large preferred positions. So when the price of preferred capital moves, it affects bank profits, lending appetite, dividends to common shareholders, and the portfolios of ordinary income investors.

Mechanically, a preferred is defined by its liquidation preference and its dividend terms. Picture the capital structure as a ladder. At the top are depositors and senior bondholders, who are paid first if the company fails. Next comes preferred stock. At the bottom is common equity, which gets whatever is left, often nothing. Preferreds usually sit on the second rung. Dividends are paid in the same order: the board must pay preferred dividends before it can pay a cent to common holders. Many preferreds are cumulative, meaning missed dividends pile up as a debt owed to holders. Non-cumulative preferreds, which is how most bank preferreds are structured, let the issuer skip a dividend without owing it later. That is a big difference, and it is how banks get regulatory credit for the security as capital.

Bank preferreds are often fixed-to-reset. For the first five or ten years, the dividend is a fixed rate, say 6.5%. On a set date, the bank can call (redeem) the shares at face value. If it does not, the rate resets to a new level, typically a spread over a benchmark like the five-year Treasury yield. That spread is set on the day the preferred is first sold, and it is the number that matters in the video. The market reprices the risk of a bank's junior capital through that spread every time the bank comes to sell new shares. A wider spread means the market demands more compensation for the chance that the bank stumbles.

The video gives a clean example. Bank of New York Mellon came to market to sell dollar-denominated preferred shares, and to get the deal done it had to pay 32.4 basis points more than it had at its post-crisis low in the summer. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent, so 32.4 bps is about 0.32 percentage points. That sounds small until you scale it. On a $1 billion issue, 0.324% extra is roughly $3.2 million per year, every year the security stays outstanding. That is a visible tax on one of the safest, best-run banks in the world, and it was the price of getting investors to take on more risk than they were willing to take a few months earlier.

The surrounding context is the video's main point. The same week, the average spread on the Bloomberg global contingent-convertible index widened from below 200 basis points to 253, and year-to-date returns on that junior bank paper dropped from 3.6% to 1.6%. In Europe, the equivalent instruments are AT1s (additional tier one bonds), also called CoCos. The presenter compares them to airbags: they sit between senior creditors and common equity, they pay 7% to 9% because they absorb the first loss, and you hope you never need them. When investors suddenly want a lot more yield to hold the airbag, it tells you they are worried about the crash, not about the car's paint.

"Think of AT1s as preferred shares like there are the airbags in your car. They sit directly between senior bond holders and common equity."

Investors and traders use preferred spreads as a stress gauge. Credit spreads on bank capital react quickly because this paper is first in line for losses, so it often wobbles before the senior bonds or the stock do. Income investors use preferreds for yield, typically comparing a preferred's dividend yield against the same bank's senior bonds, then asking if the extra income is worth the extra risk. Bank analysts watch new-issue spreads because they feed straight into the bank's cost of capital. If a bank must pay more to raise junior capital, its funding costs rise and net interest margins can shrink. The video also flags downstream effects: slower buybacks, restrained dividend increases, and tighter lending standards, since expensive capital makes a bank cautious.

The gotchas are where people get hurt. First, a preferred is not a bond. A bond that misses a payment is in default and creditors can force a restructuring. A preferred issuer, especially on non-cumulative shares, can often skip dividends with no default at all. Second, the quoted yield can mislead. Preferreds trade above or below face value, and a high "current yield" may assume the bank will call the shares on schedule, which is not guaranteed. Third, preferreds carry interest-rate risk like any long-dated fixed-income product: if rates rise, prices of fixed-rate preferreds fall. Fourth, many bank preferreds trade thinly, so price swings in stress can be larger than the headline credit news seems to justify. Finally, in the US, many preferreds pay qualified dividends taxed at lower rates, but this depends on the specific security.

The failure case is the reason for the yield. In a bank crisis, preferred holders are among the first to take losses. Dividends can be suspended, prices can gap down, and in a resolution the shares can be wiped out or converted into common stock at a terrible moment. The video's reminder is Credit Suisse in March 2023, when Swiss regulators forced a takeover and wrote $17 billion of AT1 bonds to zero overnight, even while shareholders received some value. That upended the assumed order of the ladder and showed that the legal documents and the regulator's powers matter as much as the textbook ranking. US preferreds are not identical to Swiss AT1s, since they are equity-like rather than contractual write-down bonds, but the lesson is shared: in a real crisis, the junior capital is the airbag, and it deploys.

Preferred stock connects to several other ideas in the video and in markets generally. It is tied to credit spreads, since its pricing is essentially a bet on the issuer's credit quality plus its place in the loss queue. It links to sovereign risk: when French and German yields diverge sharply, bank capital that depends on those governments' health gets repriced, because European banks hold large amounts of their home government's debt. It connects to basis points and spread analysis, to the capital structure ladder, and to the flight to safety that pushes Treasury yields down even as risky credit sells off. The practical takeaway is that falling government yields alongside widening bank-capital spreads is not good news. It means money is running toward safety, and the market is charging banks more to stand at the front of the loss line.
