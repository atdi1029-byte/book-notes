MBA Mortgage Applications Survey

The MBA Mortgage Applications Survey is a weekly report from the Mortgage Bankers Association, the trade group for the U.S. real estate finance industry. Every week, the MBA collects data from lenders that together handle a large share of American mortgage originations. It counts how many people applied for a home loan, whether to buy a house or refinance an existing one. It also reports the average interest rate on those applications. It comes out every Wednesday at 7:00 a.m. Eastern, which makes it one of the earliest housing readings of the week. Applications are the first step in the home-buying process, so the survey shows what households are doing before the sales, construction and price data catch up.

The survey matters because housing is among the most rate-sensitive parts of the economy. A home is the biggest purchase most people ever make, and almost all of them finance it. So the monthly payment, which depends on the mortgage rate, often decides whether a deal happens. When applications fall, existing home sales usually fall a month or two later. Builders then see weaker orders, and furniture, appliance and home-improvement spending cools. Mortgage rates also show how Fed policy and bond yields reach ordinary households. That makes the survey a bridge between market data and real life. The mortgage rate is the number a family actually feels, while the 10-year yield is the number traders watch.

Mechanically, the survey is built from lender data. Participating lenders report how many applications they received during the week, which runs Saturday through the following Friday. The MBA weights this sample to reflect the whole market and publishes several numbers. The Market Composite Index measures total application volume. The Purchase Index and the Refinance Index split that volume by loan purpose. The report also gives the average contract rate on a 30-year fixed mortgage with a conforming balance, along with points and loan-to-value ratios. The headline percentages are week-over-week changes, seasonally adjusted to strip out predictable patterns like holidays and the spring buying season. Any single weekly figure also gets compared with the same week a year earlier.

The November 12th video gives a good example. The presenter called it the "shadow data point of the day." Total applications rose 6.2% and purchase applications rose 8%, with the 30-year rate at 7.21%, the lowest since mid-September. Headlines called that a housing rebound. The presenter disagreed:

"It's not a housing rebound. Purchase applications are still 18% below where they were a year ago. But any progress is good progress."

The two numbers are about different timeframes. A rise of 8% in one week is a short-term bounce. Being 18% lower than last year is a statement about the overall level of activity. A market can bounce for several weeks and still be far below where it was. The 8% gain does not offset the 18% shortfall.

Traders and investors use the survey in a few ways. First, it is an early signal for housing-linked stocks such as homebuilders, mortgage lenders, title insurers and building-product companies. A run of rising purchase applications supports a positive view on those names, and a run of falling ones argues for caution. Second, it works as a real-time check on how much the bond market's moves have reached consumers. Third, it helps forecast the existing home sales report, which the video's daybook scheduled for 10:00 that same morning, expected at 3.87 million annualized. Experienced watchers focus on the four-week moving average and the purchase index rather than the headline. They care more about the trend over several weeks than about one week's change.

The survey has several gotchas. The first is the one in the video: it measures applications, not completed sales. Many applicants never close because of rejected financing, failed inspections or a lost bid. The second is the refinance index, which swings wildly because refinancing responds sharply to small rate changes. A drop of 0.25 points can send a wave of homeowners to refinance, so the headline total can jump while the housing market does nothing. This is why the purchase index is the better gauge of actual demand. The third is timing. The survey week ended November 6, which was before Tuesday's hot CPI report. As the presenter noted, that report sent the 2-year yield up 17 basis points in a single session. The rate relief the survey captured was already stale by the time the data came out. The fourth is that weekly numbers are noisy, and holiday weeks can distort even the seasonal adjustment.

The survey can mislead in a few ways. Seasonal adjustment can fail around holidays, as happens around Thanksgiving and year-end, producing readings that later get revised or ignored. The sample is large but not complete, and the MBA changes its methodology and seasonal factors from time to time, which can make comparisons across years unreliable. The survey also misses cash buyers, who skip mortgages entirely. In a market with a lot of cash buying, such as one with older, wealthy purchasers, applications understate real activity. Finally, the rate it reports is an average from a particular loan type, not a quote anyone is guaranteed. Other sources, such as Bloomberg's mortgage rate series, can differ by around 10 basis points, as the presenter noted.

The survey connects to many other ideas in the video. Mortgage rates track the 10-year Treasury yield, which sat just above 5% that morning, with a spread on top for credit risk and servicing costs. Because futures were treating a December Fed hike as nearly certain, the market was pricing higher rates, not cuts. That means mortgage rates are more likely to rise than fall from here, which would cut into the applications recovery. The 30-year bond auction at 1:00 p.m. mattered for the same reason: weak demand there pushes long yields up, and mortgage rates follow. Existing home sales, the lagging indicator, tell you what applications looked like a month or two earlier. In short, read applications as a leading but noisy signal, and always check them against the rate environment.
