Buy the Dip

Buying the dip means purchasing an asset after its price has fallen, on the bet that the drop is temporary and the price will recover. The idea is simple: if something you wanted was worth $100 last month and now costs $85, you are getting a discount. In stock markets, the "dip" is usually defined by a percentage decline from a recent high. A fall of 5% is a pullback. A fall of 10% or more is a correction. A fall of 20% or more is a bear market. People who buy the dip are betting that falling prices are an opportunity and not a warning, and that belief has been rewarded often enough that it has become close to a cultural reflex among retail investors.

The strategy matters because it shapes where real money goes at the moments of greatest stress. When an index like the NASDAQ has its worst day in 19 months, as it did when it fell 5.5% in a single session and finished more than 10% below its October 6 record, millions of people ask the same question at once: do I buy now? Their answers move cash out of savings and into stocks, affect 401(k) contributions, and decide whether a household locks in a loss or rides out a storm. It also affects people who never trade, because index funds in retirement accounts hold the same giant technology companies that get hit hardest in a selloff.

Mechanically, buying the dip rests on one assumption: that the price decline is not a permanent change in value. Stock prices combine two things, the profits a company is expected to earn and the rate used to convert those future profits into today's dollars. A dip can come from a fear that fades, such as a panic, a bad headline, or a forced seller, in which case prices tend to snap back. Or it can come from a real change in value, such as higher interest rates or shrinking earnings, in which case the "discount" is just the new fair price. The whole strategy depends on correctly telling those two situations apart, and the price drop alone cannot tell you which one you are in.

The famous "buy the dip" table makes the case. It lists each time over roughly the last decade that the NASDAQ first closed in correction territory, and what the index did over the following 12 months. October 2018 was followed by a 15% gain. February 2020, the pandemic crash, was followed by 54%. August 2024 gained 25%, and March 2025 about 24%. Averaged across the whole table, the result is 21%. Read quickly, the message writes itself: corrections are sales, and the NASDAQ always comes back. For most of those rows, that really was true, which is why the table is so persuasive and so widely shared.

But an average is only as honest as the rows behind it, and the video points to two problems. First, the article says the NASDAQ hit a correction 10 times in the decade, yet the table has only nine rows. Second, the March 2025 row, listed at 19% in the source, began on March 26, so 12 months have not passed and it cannot be a true 12-month return. Neither flaw is fatal, but they are a reminder to check the data before trusting a headline number. The sample is also small. Nine observations, many clustered in one long bull market, is a thin base for a rule you plan to bet your savings on.

The real lesson sits in the one red row. On January 19, 2022, the NASDAQ confirmed a correction. Twelve months later it was down another 24% from that day's close, not from the peak, but from the very day investors were told to buy the dip. Someone who put cash in that day was underwater by nearly a quarter a year later. The reason was not that the companies suddenly became bad businesses. It was interest rates. The Fed's target sat at 0 to 0.25% on the day of the correction, then rose seven times, reaching 4.25 to 4.5% by December, and each rally ran into a higher discount rate.

"The average is not the story. The exception really is."

The mechanism is discounting. A growth stock is mostly a claim on profits that arrive years from now, and investors shrink those distant profits by an interest rate to find what they are worth today. When rates rise, the denominator in that calculation gets bigger and the present value gets smaller, and the further out the profits sit, the harder the hit. That is why the NASDAQ, full of long-duration growth companies, is the index that feels the Fed first. The Fed's own forecast makes the point: in December 2021 the median official projected about 0.9% for the end of 2022, and the actual figure was near 4.4%, an error of roughly 3.5 percentage points. Even the people setting rates did not see it coming, which is why leaning on any forecast is dangerous.

So what separates the winners from the loser? Not simply whether the Fed was hiking. October 2018 was a hiking year: the Fed raised rates a fourth time on December 19, and by Christmas Eve the NASDAQ sat 23.6% below its record close, a bear market. Yet 12 months after that correction began the index was up 15%. The difference is that the Fed stopped. On January 30, 2019, it said it would be "patient," and by July it was cutting. Every completed row in the table either had a Fed that was cutting, sitting at zero, or about to stop hiking. 2022 was the only one where tightening kept going. The better rule is that dips recover when the Fed stops, not that hikes always kill them.

Practically, a thoughtful investor uses this in several ways. First, ask what caused the drop, and whether the cause is still active. Second, watch the specific signals: in the video, the October CPI report, the December 9 Fed decision, the new dot plot, and the language of the Fed chair. A median projection for 2027 that moves higher resembles 2022, while one that holds flat resembles 2018, and a single word like "patient" can signal that hiking is over. Third, watch mortgage rates and the bond market, which often vote before the Fed does. Fourth, size your purchases. Spreading money across several dates, known as dollar-cost averaging, protects you if the first dip is not the last.

The gotchas are mostly psychological. Survivorship and recency bias make the strategy feel safer than it is: after years of recoveries, a few quick rebounds feel like a law of nature. An average also hides a distribution, and a 21% mean that includes a 54% pandemic rebound and a 24% loss tells you little about what a typical outcome looks like. A dip is also not a price. A stock down 10% can fall another 30%, so "cheap" depends on value, not on how far it has dropped. Finally, "buy the dip" is easy advice for people who never pay for being wrong. The people who published the average are not the ones holding the loss. The investor who moved cash in is.

When the strategy fails, it fails in a specific way: the dip keeps dipping. Rising rates, rising inflation, a recession that has not yet shown up in earnings, or a credit event can all turn a correction into a long decline. The risk is also personal, since it depends on your timeline. An investor with 25 years can sit through a 2022 and recover, but someone needing the money for a house in two years, or facing a mortgage at about 7.18% against 6.22% a year ago, may not be able to wait. The video is careful to say that none of this is a prediction. The Fed's September forecast showed one more hike and a long pause, a 2018 shape, and the March 2025 correction recovered to a record by October 6 despite fading rate-cut hopes. A priced hike is not a delivered hike, and 77% is not 100%.

Buying the dip connects to several other ideas worth knowing. It rests on discounted cash flow valuation and the link between interest rates and stock prices, so it is inseparable from understanding the Fed, the dot plot, and rate expectations in Fed funds futures. It sits beside dollar-cost averaging, position sizing, and risk management, which decide how much a wrong call can hurt. And it is a close cousin of the psychological traps of FOMO and anchoring, where a past peak becomes the reference point that makes today's price feel like a bargain. The takeaway is simple: a 21% average is not a law of nature. It is a bet that the Fed stops hiking.
