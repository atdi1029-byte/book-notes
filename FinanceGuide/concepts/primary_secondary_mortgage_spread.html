Primary-Secondary Mortgage Spread (Lender Gain-on-Sale Margin)

The primary-secondary mortgage spread is the gap between the interest rate a bank quotes you at closing (the "primary market" rate) and the yield on the mortgage-backed security (MBS) that same loan gets bundled into and sold as on Wall Street (the "secondary market"). Think of it as the lender's cut. When you sign a 30-year note, your bank almost never keeps it. It packages your loan with thousands of others into an MBS, guaranteed by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, and sells that bundle to pension funds, insurers, and foreign reserve managers. The spread is what's left over between what you pay and what the bank can actually get for the security in that sale — it is the lender's gross profit margin, sometimes called the gain-on-sale margin, before operating costs are even subtracted.

This spread matters because it is the shock absorber sitting between you and the bond market. Your mortgage rate isn't really set by your local loan officer or by what home shoppers can afford — it is set mechanically off a benchmark, and the size of the spread determines how much of that benchmark's movement actually reaches your rate sheet. When the spread is wide, a lender has room to eat a bad afternoon in the bond market and hold its quoted rate steady, protecting borrowers from volatility. When the spread is compressed to its structural floor, there is no cushion left. Every basis point of movement in the underlying benchmark passes through to your preapproval letter, dollar for dollar. That is exactly the condition the video describes: a market with the padding stripped out.

Mechanically, the spread is built in layers. Start with the 10-year Treasury yield, the foundational benchmark because mortgage cash flows behave similarly to a 10-year bond once you account for average prepayment timing. Add a markup to get the current-coupon agency MBS yield — this first layer compensates investors for prepayment risk and the guarantee wrapper. Then add a second markup, the primary-secondary spread itself, which compensates the originating lender for hedging costs, servicing rights, balance sheet risk, and origination overhead, and which also contains its profit. Stack those two layers on top of the Treasury yield and you get the rate on your rate sheet. Historically that full stack — Treasury to primary mortgage rate — runs about 170 to 180 basis points over the 10-year in normal times, ballooning past 300 basis points during acute stress like 2022–2023, then grinding back down as panic fades.

"Against agency MBS benchmarks, primary to secondary spreads are tightening down near their multi-year floors."
That's the video's shorthand for the second layer — the lender's own margin — hitting its structural bottom.

Here's the real-world arithmetic from the video. On the Thursday described, the 10-year Treasury spiked to an intraday 5.17%, and daily mortgage rate locks hit 7.45% on conventional 30-year paper — the year's high print. That put the total Treasury-to-mortgage spread at roughly 2.28 percentage points, which the host frames as sitting near a multi-year floor for the lender's own gain-on-sale piece of that spread. Two years earlier, during the 2022-2023 panic, that same spread had blown out past 300 basis points as lenders charged a heavy premium to cover Fed-driven volatility. The industry had spent two years waiting for that spread to "normalize" back toward historical levels, on the theory that normalization alone would hand borrowers a rate cut of more than a full point. It normalized — and mortgage rates went up anyway, because the Treasury yield underneath it kept climbing.

Traders and mortgage desks watch this spread as a health gauge for the housing-finance plumbing, not as a trading instrument retail investors touch directly. A widening spread signals lender risk aversion, hedging costs rising, or balance-sheet stress at loan originators — a warning sign for housing-linked equities and homebuilder stocks. A spread compressing toward its floor, as described here, signals the opposite kind of warning: it means mortgage rates have lost their independent cushion and will now move in lockstep with whatever the Treasury market does. Bond desks and MBS traders use the spread to price the "option-adjusted spread" on mortgage securities and to decide how much prepayment and duration risk they're being paid to hold. For an ordinary borrower, the practical use is simpler: watching whether this spread is wide or narrow tells you whether a spike in Treasury yields will hit your rate lock hard or get partially absorbed.

The biggest misconception is treating "spread normalization" as automatically good news for affordability. Commentators spent two years promising that once the spread came back down from its 2022 blowout, mortgage rates would fall sharply. That reasoning silently assumed the Treasury yield underneath would stay put. It didn't — the 10-year kept climbing toward 5%-plus territory, so a normalized, tighter spread on top of a higher base yield still produced a higher final mortgage rate. Another common error is confusing the lagging Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey, which the video notes cut off data collection on a Wednesday morning and got reported as "hovering near 7%," with live daily lock quotes, which had already touched 7.45% by Thursday afternoon. The spread and the underlying benchmark both need to be read in real time, not off a weekly survey with a built-in lag.

When the spread compresses all the way to its structural floor, as described here, the failure mode is that lenders lose their ability to smooth volatility for borrowers at all. In ordinary conditions a lender can absorb a rough Treasury session by shaving its own margin for a day or two to keep quotes stable and keep loan volume flowing. At the floor, that option disappears — margins are already thin enough that shaving further would mean originating loans at a loss, so lenders simply pass every move straight through. This is compounded, per the video, by the Federal Reserve no longer buying MBS and by heavy Treasury issuance crowding dealer balance sheets, meaning the parties who would normally absorb mortgage duration risk are already saturated with government paper and demand extra compensation to take on more. The result is a mortgage market with no cushion left in any layer of the stack — benchmark, MBS spread, or lender margin.

This concept connects directly to a few others worth understanding together. The 10-year Treasury yield is the base layer this spread sits on top of, so anything driving Treasury issuance or demand — like the deficit financing and quantitative tightening dynamic described in the video — flows straight through. Agency MBS pricing is the middle layer, reflecting prepayment risk and the value of the Fannie/Freddie guarantee. And the broader concept of a rate lock — the window during which a lender guarantees you a quoted rate before closing — is priced directly off how wide or narrow this spread is at the moment you lock, which is why locks can move meaningfully day to day even when headline "average rate" surveys look flat.
