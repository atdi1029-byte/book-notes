Earnings Per Share (EPS)

Earnings Per Share, or EPS, is the portion of a company's profit assigned to each individual share of stock. You calculate it by taking net income and dividing it by the number of shares outstanding. If a company earns $284 million and has 240 million shares outstanding, EPS is roughly $1.19. It's the single most quoted number in corporate earnings because it translates a company-wide dollar figure into something a shareholder can actually compare to the price they paid for their piece of it. Every "earnings beat" or "earnings miss" headline you see is really an EPS number measured against what analysts expected, not the raw net income figure.

EPS matters because it's the bridge between a company's total profitability and what an individual investor experiences. Total net income tells you how big the profit pie is; EPS tells you how big your slice is. This distinction sounds academic until you realize the size of the pie and the size of your slice can move in different directions, or at different speeds, in the same quarter. That gap is exactly what happened with Lennar, the homebuilder, in the earnings report discussed here — and understanding why the gap exists is the whole point of learning to read EPS correctly rather than just glancing at whether it went up or down.

Mechanically, EPS has two moving parts: the numerator (net income) and the denominator (share count). Net income falls when revenue drops, margins compress, or costs rise. Share count falls when a company buys back its own stock and retires those shares. Because EPS is a division problem, a shrinking denominator can offset a shrinking numerator. If profit falls 20% but share count also falls 10%, EPS only falls about 11%, not 20%. This is not fraud or manipulation — it's arithmetic — but it means EPS growth (or a softer EPS decline) doesn't always mean the underlying business got healthier. It can mean the company simply has fewer slices to divide the same, or smaller, pie into.

Lennar's third-quarter report is a textbook case. Net earnings fell to $284 million from $591 million a year earlier — a drop of about 52%. But EPS fell from $2.29 to $1.19, a drop of about 48%. Those two percentages should, in theory, move together if share count were constant. They didn't, because Lennar spent the year buying back its own stock, shrinking the share count and therefore cushioning the per-share number. The video's host does the math directly:

Earnings per share went from $2.29 to $1.19, which is a drop of about, here's my math, 48%. And the reason those two percentages are different is that Lennar bought back enough stock to cushion about four points of it.

Four percentage points may sound small, but it's the difference between a headline that reads "profit collapsed" and one that reads "profit came in roughly in line." That gap is entirely a function of the buyback, not of the housing business performing any better than the raw net income number suggests.

Traders and analysts use EPS constantly, but the sophisticated ones never look at it alone. They pair it with net income growth, revenue growth, and margin trends to see whether EPS is being driven by real operating improvement or by financial engineering — share buybacks, one-time tax benefits, or accounting changes. When a company's EPS beats expectations but net income and revenue both missed, that's a signal to dig into the share count trend before treating the "beat" as good news. Sell-side estimates are built around EPS specifically because it accounts for dilution and buybacks, theoretically making it the more "investor-relevant" number — but that same feature is what makes it manipulable through share buyback programs timed around earnings season.

The common misconception is treating EPS as a pure measure of business health. It isn't — it's a measure of business health as filtered through capital allocation decisions. A company can grow EPS for years while its actual operating business stagnates, simply by consistently retiring shares faster than profit erodes. Lennar's case shows the flip side: a genuinely bad quarter (net earnings down 52%, gross margin down from 17.5% to 15.8%, and a staggering drop from the pandemic peak of 29.5%) still produced an EPS decline that looked comparatively less severe. Anyone reading only the EPS headline would underestimate how much the core business actually deteriorated.

Where this breaks down is when buybacks are funded by debt or by draining cash that would otherwise support the business, rather than by genuine free cash flow. In that scenario, a company can prop up EPS quarter after quarter while quietly weakening its balance sheet — a strategy that works until it doesn't, often revealed only when credit conditions tighten or the business hits a real downturn and there's no cash cushion left. It also breaks down in comparative analysis: if you're comparing two homebuilders and one has been aggressively buying back stock while the other hasn't, comparing their EPS growth rates directly will mislead you about which company's underlying operations are actually stronger. This connects directly to gross margin and net income as the more fundamental profitability measures — EPS is what you check second, after you've confirmed the top-line and margin story actually supports the per-share number being reported.
