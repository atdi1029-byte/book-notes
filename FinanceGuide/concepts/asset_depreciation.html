Asset Depreciation

Asset depreciation is the decline in an asset's value over time as it ages, wears out, or becomes obsolete. Accountants use the word for a scheduled expense: a company spreads the cost of a truck or machine across its useful life, so each year's income statement carries a slice of it. Anyone who owns something that loses value is dealing with the economic version of the same idea. A car is the most common example. The moment a new vehicle leaves the lot, buyers will pay less for it than you just did. Each year of mileage, wear, and newer models arriving makes it worth less. Depreciation is not a fee anyone charges you. It is the quiet erosion of what you own.

Depreciation matters because it creates a gap between what you owe and what you own, and that gap decides whether you are financially safe or trapped. If an asset holds its value, a loan against it is secured by something real. If the asset loses value faster than you repay, the debt outlives the collateral. This affects far more than individual households. Auto lenders, bond investors who buy asset-backed securities, and banks all price their risk on how fast the underlying collateral loses value. In the video's framing, the car is "not at all a store of value." It is a depreciating asset, and financing it as if it were not is how credit problems build quietly.

Mechanically, depreciation works in two layers. The first is accounting: a business takes the purchase price, subtracts the expected salvage value, and divides the rest across the useful life. This is straight-line depreciation. Some firms use faster schedules that front-load the expense. The second layer is market value, which is what a buyer will actually pay. It does not follow a neat schedule. Cars usually drop hardest in the first few years, then flatten out. Supply, interest rates, fuel prices, and the used-car market all push it around. The two layers can diverge sharply. A book value on paper may say one thing while the wholesale auction price says something much lower.

Now connect depreciation to a loan. A loan balance falls according to the contract. Early in a long loan, most of each payment goes to interest, so the principal barely moves. The car's value, meanwhile, drops steeply from day one. The result is negative equity, also called being "underwater" or "upside down": you owe more than the asset would sell for. Stretching a loan to 72 or 84 months makes this worse because the principal falls more slowly while the car keeps losing value at its own pace. Lengthening the loan lowers the monthly payment, but it does not change the depreciation curve. The video's line says it plainly:

"Depreciation does not pause simply because you extended a financing contract to 84 months."

The video gives concrete numbers. Average new-vehicle transaction prices reached about $50,000 after the pandemic supply crunch, when dealers added markups on top of window stickers. To make those prices fit median incomes, lenders pushed terms out: about 22% of new originations over the prior 36 months ran 72 to 84 months, where a conservative loan once ran 36 to 48. The example household bought three years earlier and now owes $31,000 on a car the dealer values at $24,000 at wholesale. That is $7,000 of negative equity. The average gap for trade-ins reached a record above $6,100. Seven years of interest charged against a falling asset is the trap the title describes.

Investors and lenders use depreciation as a signal in several ways. Auto lenders watch the loan-to-value ratio, which compares the balance to the collateral's worth. Anything above 100% means the lender is already short if the car is repossessed. The video notes that lenders now issue repossession orders at 60 days past due instead of 90 to 120, because a recovered car loses another 10 to 15% of auction value while waiting. Investors in auto asset-backed securities track used-car price indexes, such as the Manheim index, along with delinquencies and charge-offs. A falling wholesale index means lower recoveries on defaulted loans. For individuals, the practical test is the one the video recommends: compare your payoff to a wholesale appraisal, not a dealer's retail ad, and treat a gap above $2,000 as real risk.

There are several nuances people get wrong. First, depreciation is not the same as a loss you realize. If you keep driving the car and pay it off, you never "lose" the difference in cash. The damage shows up when you need to sell, trade, refinance, or the car is totaled. Second, retail and wholesale values differ, and lenders and insurers use the lower one. Third, a good credit score does not protect you. The video points out that 700-score borrowers are stuck too, because no bank will refinance a loan at 130% of the vehicle's value. Fourth, accounting depreciation is a non-cash expense, so it lowers reported profit without reducing cash. For a household, nothing so gentle applies.

When this breaks, it breaks through the negative equity itself. A common failure is rolling negative equity into the next loan: the $7,000 shortfall gets added to the new car's financing, so the buyer starts the next loan already underwater, with a larger balance and often another long term. The cycle repeats and the gap compounds. If the car is wrecked or stolen, insurance pays only actual cash value, leaving the owner liable for the shortfall unless they carry gap insurance. If income is squeezed by fuel, groceries, or insurance costs, the owner has nothing to sell or refinance, so surrendering the keys becomes the rational choice. The video describes this as historically unusual behavior: people now abandon the car loan before other debts.

Depreciation ties into several other ideas in this guide. It is the reason net worth can mislead: a car listed at its purchase price overstates what you own. It connects to amortization, since the loan's payment schedule is what fails to keep pace with value. It links to loan-to-value and collateral risk in lending, to subprime credit and delinquency cycles, and to opportunity cost, because money spent servicing a shrinking asset cannot compound elsewhere. The practical defenses follow from the mechanism: shorter terms, a meaningful down payment, extra principal payments (even $50 to $100 a month), never rolling negative equity forward, and gap coverage if you are underwater. Depreciation is predictable, so you can plan around it. The trouble starts when a loan is built as though it did not exist.
