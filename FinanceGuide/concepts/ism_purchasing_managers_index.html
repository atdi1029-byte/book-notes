ISM Purchasing Managers' Index (PMI) — Prices Paid

The ISM Purchasing Managers' Index (PMI) is a monthly survey published by the Institute for Supply Management, one of the oldest and most respected trade organizations in the United States. It polls purchasing and supply executives at hundreds of companies across either the manufacturing or services sectors, asking them a deceptively simple question: compared to last month, did conditions get better, worse, or stay the same? The "Prices Paid" component — sometimes called the ISM Prices Paid Index — specifically asks whether the prices those managers are paying for their inputs, raw materials, components, and services, went up, down, or held steady. It is one of the earliest, most reliable reads on inflation pressure that arrives well before official CPI or PPI data does.

Why does this matter? Because purchasing managers are the people who actually write the checks. They negotiate contracts for steel, plastic, diesel fuel, software licenses, and labor. When they collectively report that prices are rising, it means costs are moving through the supply chain right now, not two months ago when the government's price indexes were being assembled. Businesses absorb some of those input cost increases through margin compression, but eventually they pass them on to consumers. That passthrough is the mechanism of inflation. So the Prices Paid component functions as an early warning system: rising readings tell economists, traders, and central bankers that inflation is still being fed at the source, before it shows up in the grocery store or gas station data everyone else watches.

Mechanically, the index is constructed using a diffusion index methodology. Respondents answer one of three ways: prices are higher, prices are the same, or prices are lower. The index is then calculated as the percentage reporting higher prices, plus half the percentage reporting no change. A reading of exactly 50 is the neutral line — it means the same number of respondents saw prices rise as saw them fall. Any reading above 50 signals that more managers are paying higher prices than lower ones, meaning input costs are expanding. A reading below 50 means costs are broadly contracting. The further the reading strays from 50 in either direction, the more intense the price pressure. A reading of 72 or 73 does not mean prices rose 72% — it means roughly 72% net of respondents are experiencing upward price pressure, which is historically quite elevated.

The specific numbers from the video make this concrete and urgent. Mark Malik flagged that ISM Services Prices Paid came in at 72.6, up from 70.3 the prior month, while ISM Manufacturing Prices Paid held at 71.1. Both of these readings are historically high. For context, during the post-pandemic inflation surge of 2021 and 2022, Prices Paid readings regularly hit the high 70s and even broke above 80. Readings in the low 70s indicate that input cost pressure remains intense and broad-based — not a one-sector quirk but a signal echoed across both goods producers and service providers simultaneously. The fact that services prices were rising while manufacturing prices held tells a particularly uncomfortable story: the inflation is not confined to the volatile goods side of the economy where supply chains get blamed. It is embedded in services, which tend to be stickier and harder to unwind.

Traders and investors use the ISM Prices Paid data as a real-time input into their Federal Reserve policy expectations. The Fed's dual mandate — maximum employment and price stability — means that stubbornly high input costs directly influence whether the central bank raises rates, holds, or cuts. When Prices Paid readings remain elevated, markets price in a higher probability of additional rate hikes or a longer "higher for longer" rate environment. In the video's context, the strong jobs report combined with still-hot Prices Paid readings caused September rate hike odds to jump to roughly 62%, which in turn pushed the 2-year Treasury yield to 4.37%, its highest since January 2025. Rising yields reprice every future cash flow in the market downward — that is the direct mechanism by which a PMI subcomponent can move stock prices on a Friday afternoon.

One of the most important nuances to understand is that the Prices Paid component is a directional signal, not a level measurement. A reading of 72.6 does not mean prices are 72.6% higher than last year — it means that a net large majority of purchasing managers are seeing their costs move higher right now. This distinction trips up casual readers who conflate the diffusion index with an inflation rate. A second nuance: the index can remain above 50 even as the rate of inflation slows, because slowing inflation and falling prices are very different things. If 60% of managers report prices still rising but at a gentler pace, the index might drift from 75 down to 62 — still expansionary, still feeding into CPI, just less aggressively. The Fed does not declare victory when the reading falls from 75 to 72. It watches for a sustained move toward and below 50.

The Prices Paid data can also send misleading signals in certain edge cases. During supply chain disruptions, readings can spike dramatically even when underlying consumer demand is weak — the 2021 semiconductor shortage drove Manufacturing Prices Paid above 85 not because demand was infinite but because supply had collapsed. Conversely, during demand destruction events like the 2008 financial crisis, Prices Paid can plummet below 30 almost overnight as businesses slash orders. In those environments, the collapse in Prices Paid signals deflation risk, not economic health. The video's situation illustrates the opposite and arguably more dangerous scenario: prices not cooling despite the Fed having already raised rates significantly. That combination — sticky input costs plus a resilient labor market — is precisely what a central bank fears most, because the standard tools are already deployed and yet the patient is not responding as expected.

The ISM Prices Paid data connects directly to several adjacent concepts that compound its significance. It feeds into the Producer Price Index (PPI), which the video flags as the very next key report due Thursday of the following week. PPI captures prices at the wholesale level, and because ISM Prices Paid leads PPI by weeks, a hot PMI reading raises the probability of a hot PPI print. PPI in turn feeds into the Consumer Price Index (CPI), the most widely watched inflation gauge. The daisy chain is Prices Paid → PPI → CPI, and each link takes time to transmit — which is exactly why sophisticated market participants watch the ISM data so closely. As the host summarized: input costs are not cooling, energy is running hot, the labor market surprised to the upside, and the Fed has not yet gotten permission from the data to stop. Until CPI confirms otherwise, everything else, as he put it, is just noise.

"ISM services prices climbed to 72.6 from 70.3 and manufacturing prices held at 71.1. Input costs, my friends, are not cooling."

Understanding the ISM Prices Paid reading is ultimately about understanding the inflation pipeline at its origin point. Most people watch CPI because it tells them what already happened to prices by the time goods and services reached consumers. The purchasing managers surveyed by ISM are telling you what is happening right now in the back offices of American businesses, where tomorrow's prices are being negotiated today. When those readings sit in the low 70s across both manufacturing and services simultaneously, as they did in the week covered in the video, it means the pipeline is still pressurized. The Fed cannot claim the fire is out when the fuel supply is still flowing. That is the precise analytical weight that a two-point move in a PMI subcomponent carries — not dramatic on its face, but freighted with consequence for interest rates, asset valuations, and the economic outlook that every market participant is trying to price in real time.
