Reserve Build (Allowance for Credit Losses)

A reserve build happens when a bank increases the cushion of money it has set aside for loans that might go bad. That cushion is formally called the allowance for credit losses. Every bank knows that some borrowers will not repay, so it does not wait for the defaults to arrive. It estimates, ahead of time, how much of its loan book will eventually be lost and holds a balance for it. The balance sits on the balance sheet as a deduction from the value of the loans. When the bank raises that balance, analysts call it a "build." When it lowers the balance, they call it a "release." Think of it as a rainy-day fund, but one the accountants require.

The reason it matters is that a reserve build is one of the few places where a bank tells you what it fears, not just what has already happened. Reported losses are history. Reserves are a forecast, written by the people who see every loan, every payment, and every late notice before anyone else does. Because banks are the biggest creditors in the economy, their forecasts leak into everything: how easily companies borrow, what interest they pay, and how willing funds that depend on bank credit are to keep lending. In the video's framing, it is "the banker's version of an umbrella on a sunny day." You carry one only if you suspect the weather will change.

Mechanically, the process has a few steps. First, the bank estimates expected losses on its loans over their lives, using borrower credit quality, loan type, economic forecasts, and its own history. Under the current accounting standard, called CECL (Current Expected Credit Loss), banks must book lifetime expected losses on day one of a loan, not wait until trouble appears. Second, the bank compares that estimate to its existing allowance. Third, it books the difference as a provision for credit losses, an expense on the income statement that cuts profit. Fourth, when loans actually fail, the bank records a charge-off (write-off), which uses up the allowance rather than hitting earnings again. The formula is simple: provision minus net charge-offs equals the change in the reserve.

The video gives a clean example from JPMorgan's third-quarter report. The bank set aside $2.74 billion as provision for future losses, while it actually wrote off about $2.2 billion of loans. The gap of roughly $539 million went into the reserve, and that is the reserve build. What made it notable was the direction of the other numbers. Credit card write-offs fell to 2.91% of balances from 3.34% the quarter before, and 90-day delinquencies dropped to 0.96%. Losses were shrinking, yet the bank was adding to its cushion. Management said the build was mainly on the corporate side, not with consumers.

"That is called a reserve build. The banker's version of, well, the best way to put it is, an umbrella on a sunny day."

Investors and traders read reserve builds as a signal, and the most useful reading is relative. Compare the provision to charge-offs: if the provision runs well above write-offs, the bank expects trouble ahead. Then check which portfolio the build is in. Consumer builds point to household stress, while corporate builds, as at JPMorgan, point to worries about business borrowers and things like AI infrastructure lending. Then look across banks. The video says that if Bank of America and Morgan Stanley also built reserves while losses fell, it would mean "not one cautious CEO" but "an industry carrying umbrellas." One bank's build can be idiosyncratic. A pattern across the sector is information about the economy. Traders also pair it with leading indicators like the 30-day delinquency bucket, which JPMorgan showed rising to 1.97% from 1.91%.

There are several gotchas. A reserve build is not a loss. It is an estimate, and it can be reversed later; if the feared defaults never come, the bank releases the reserve, and that release boosts profit. This makes reserves a lever for managing earnings: after the pandemic, banks released billions and padded results. Second, a build does not by itself mean the bank is weak. Banks that reserve generously are often the ones that are more conservative. Third, reserves can rise for mechanical reasons, such as loan growth or a change in the economic forecast fed into the CECL model, with no deterioration in any borrower. The signal is strongest when losses are falling and the build happens anyway, as it did here, but you still need the bank's explanation.

Reserves can fail in two directions. If a bank under-reserves and a downturn hits, it must book a huge provision all at once, which crushes earnings and capital exactly when markets are fragile; the 2008 crisis showed how late and how large that catch-up can be. Over-reserving has costs too: it holds back profit and can look like pessimism that spooks investors. CECL was designed to make banks recognize losses sooner, but critics say it makes reserves procyclical, meaning they jump when the economy turns, tightening credit at the worst time. And reserves only cover the losses the bank can foresee. A sudden shock, such as collateral like AI chips losing value, can exceed the cushion.

The reserve build connects to several other ideas in the video. It ties to the net interest income story: the loan desk earned $27.15 billion, so the bank has strong earnings to absorb a build. It links to private credit and BDCs, since many of those funds borrow from banks like JPMorgan, and a cautious bank lends less or demands more yield, as shown by the 11.2% to 11.75% pricing on the $5 billion Volta loan, where JPMorgan and Goldman kept only $1.3 billion on their own books. And it connects to maturity transformation and the It's a Wonderful Life bank run: the reserve is the bank's "honeymoon money," the cash cushion that matters when everyone comes knocking at once. Understanding the build lets you see caution in a bank's behavior before it appears in its losses.

Also called: Current Expected Credit Losses (CECL)
