Prediction Markets

A prediction market is a marketplace where people trade contracts whose payoff depends on a future event. A typical contract asks a yes-or-no question, such as "Will Democrats win the House?" It pays $1 if the answer is yes and $0 if it is no. The contract trades freely at any price between those two numbers, and that price is read as a probability. A contract trading at 94 cents implies the market thinks the event is about 94% likely. The two big U.S. venues in the video are Kalshi, a federally regulated exchange, and Polymarket, a crypto-based platform. The idea behind them is simple: instead of asking people what they think, make them put money behind it.

These markets matter because their prices have escaped the trading screen and become public information. On the morning of the 2026 midterms, Kalshi priced Democratic control of the House at 94%, with more than $237 million riding on that one question. Numbers like that show up in headlines, on social feeds, and even on a Kalshi billboard in New York City showing its Senate odds. When millions of people see a percentage, they treat it as a fact about the world and may adjust their business plans, their investments, or their expectations accordingly. There is also a bigger story. Intercontinental Exchange, which owns the New York Stock Exchange, has invested $1.6 billion in Polymarket, and Kalshi has raised money at a $22 billion valuation. Odds have become a product.

Here is the mechanism, step by step. First, someone creates a contract with a precise question and a clear rule for how it settles. Second, traders buy "yes" or "no" shares. If you think the true chance is 94% and the price is 90 cents, buying looks like a bargain. Third, every buy pushes the price up and every sell pushes it down, until buyers and sellers roughly agree. Fourth, when the event is decided, winning shares pay $1 and losing shares pay nothing. Every trade needs someone on the other side, so one person's profit is another's loss. The price is therefore the point where the marginal buyer and the marginal seller disagree just enough to trade. It is not a vote, and it is not an average of everyone's opinions.

The sales pitch is that "people don't lie with their own money." A pundit or a poll respondent pays nothing for being wrong. A trader does. In theory, this pulls information into one number faster than any survey can. The video's host, who has more than 35 years in capital markets, concedes the point: prices really can pool information quickly. The 2024 election is the trophy case. Polling averages showed a roughly even presidential race, while the markets had Donald Trump as the favorite for weeks. One French trader, nicknamed the "Polymarket whale," put about $40 million into Trump-related bets and stood to collect around $80 million. Polymarket called it proof of the wisdom of markets over polls, and that story is the foundation of the industry's credibility.

"A poll counts people, my friends. A market counts dollars. That one difference is the whole entire game."

That difference is the first big gotcha. In a poll, a retiree in Ohio and a hedge fund manager each get one answer. In a market, influence scales with the size of your checkbook. Reuters, citing the Anti-Corruption Data Collective, reported that on Polymarket's international site the top 1% of wallets placed about 70% of the money bet on Congress this cycle. Only 39,820 wallets had traded the 2026 congressional races, and just 10 wallets drove 17% of the volume while betting in 426 of the 470 seats up for election. In thin markets, one confident trader can effectively be the whole price. The number looks like a consensus but may be one person's opinion with a large check attached. That is why the video concludes that a 94% price is not the voice of the country.

The second gotcha is what a probability even means. A 94% price is not a prediction that something will happen. It says that in 100 elections that look like this one, the underdog would win about 6, or roughly once in 17 tries. If a weather app says 94% chance of sun and it rains, the app was not necessarily wrong. So calibration is the right test: collect every contract priced near 80% and check whether about eight in ten came true. If only six did, the market was overconfident, no matter how any single headline race turned out. One election night cannot prove these markets right or wrong, just as one rainy day cannot refute a forecast.

The video also shows how the track record cuts both ways. In 2024 the markets beat the polls. In 2022, PredictIt had Republicans as high as 82% to win the Senate on election day. Then Democrats held the chamber, the price collapsed from 82% to 39% as results came in, and the final answer waited until Nevada was called the following Saturday. Polymarket's spokesperson claims its election markets are accurate 98% of the time one day before an election, but that figure can be flattering, because most races are lopsided and easy to call. The informative cases are the 70% to 90% contracts, which is where the calibration test does its real work. Cherry-picking either 2024 or 2022 proves nothing, which is the trap the video warns about.

Product design adds another weakness: slicing questions into thinner markets. Reuters found that in 2024, 71% of election markets simply asked who would win a race. This cycle only 16% did, and 58% were bets on the winning margin. Cutting one question into a dozen smaller ones creates more things to trade but less money behind each price, and 80% of the Polymarket markets examined had fewer than 100 wallets. Then there are integrity risks. Kalshi suspended a handful of candidates for betting on their own races, and NPR reported campaign staffers trading on races they worked on. The video is careful to say nobody has shown any 2026 midterm market was manipulated, and that the watchdog group is an advocacy organization critical of these markets, so its tone deserves some discounting.

Practically, a sensible user treats these prices as one input, weighted by how much money stands behind them. The video's host builds a weekly "boom versus doom" index from Kalshi and Polymarket prices and weights each by the money in the market, because a price backed by $237 million deserves more trust than one backed by a few thousand dollars. Useful checks include looking at volume and the number of traders, watching for thin markets that swing hard late, and waiting for the calibration results once every race is called. There is a feedback-loop risk too: a thin market gets quoted on television, the quote attracts new money, and the new money makes the price look more convincing. That is not fraud, just a small market handed a big microphone. If you trade, remember the person on the other side of your $50 bet may be a whale with more money and better data.

Prediction markets connect to several core ideas in finance. They are an application of the efficient market hypothesis, the idea that prices absorb available information, and they inherit its limits: efficiency requires deep liquidity and many independent participants. They resemble options and futures, which are also contracts whose value depends on a future outcome. They raise questions about liquidity, market concentration, and regulation, since the CFTC dropped its court fight in 2025 and allowed election contracts. Finally, they echo how Wall Street turns anything measurable into a tradable product. The video's closing line makes the point: no single election night can tell you whether the big checks were smart or just loud.

"A 94% price is not the voice of the country. It is the opinion of whoever wrote the biggest checks."
