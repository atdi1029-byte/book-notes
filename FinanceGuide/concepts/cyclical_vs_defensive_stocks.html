Cyclical vs. Defensive Stocks

Every company's profits rise and fall with something, and the question is how hard. A cyclical stock belongs to a business whose sales and earnings swing sharply with the economic cycle, or with a cycle in its own industry. Cars, airlines, homebuilders, steel, hotels and semiconductors are the classic examples. When times are good, customers buy more and earnings surge. When times turn, orders get cut or delayed and earnings can collapse. A defensive stock belongs to a business whose demand barely moves with the cycle. Groceries, toothpaste, electricity, medicine and paper towels get bought in good years and bad. Defensive profits are not immune to trouble, but they are steadier, and that steadiness is what investors are paying for.

This distinction matters because it changes what a price tag means. A share price is a bet on future profits, and the more volatile those profits are, the less reliable any forecast of them becomes. That is why cyclical and defensive stocks get valued so differently, and why you can't compare their multiples directly. It also matters for your own portfolio. Cyclicals tend to lead markets up in recoveries and fall hardest in recessions, while defensives tend to fall less in downturns and lag in rallies. Knowing which kind of company you own tells you what kind of ride you have signed up for, and it affects how much risk you carry when the economy changes direction.

Here is the mechanism. Cyclical businesses usually have two features: customers can postpone the purchase, and the company has large fixed costs. A carmaker or chip manufacturer must run expensive factories whether or not demand is strong. When demand rises, each extra sale drops largely to profit, so earnings leap by far more than revenue. When demand falls, the same fixed costs are spread over fewer sales, and profit falls faster than revenue. Customers also overreact. In a boom they order extra inventory, then in a slowdown they stop ordering until the stockpile clears, which exaggerates the swings. A defensive company faces the opposite pattern: purchases are needed, frequent and cheap, so volume holds up and earnings drift rather than lurch.

The most counterintuitive consequence involves the price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E, which divides the share price by profit per share. For a steady business, a low P/E can signal a bargain. For a cyclical, it often signals the opposite. At the peak of the cycle, orders are booming and profits are enormous, so the denominator is inflated and the P/E looks tiny. Then orders slow, profits shrink, and the stock that seemed cheap turns out to have been full price all along. Investors call this the value trap. The video put it plainly:

"When orders are booming, profits are huge, and the PE looks tiny. Then the orders slow, the profits shrink, and that bargain price tag turns out to have been full price all along."

The video's comparison makes the point with real numbers. On the Friday before Nvidia's earnings, Walmart traded at 42.79 times expected profit, while Nvidia traded at 13.46 times, cheaper than the S&P 500's 18.3. Analysts expected Walmart's earnings to grow about 12%, from $2.89 to $3.23 a share, and Nvidia's to grow about 70%, from $9.32 to $15.83. Nvidia's quarterly revenue had more than doubled in a year, to $96.2 billion, with data center sales above 92% of the total and one customer making up 16%. Yet the market paid more than three times as much for a dollar of Walmart's profit. Investors were pricing the stability of Walmart's earnings and the uncertainty of Nvidia's.

The host's point is about believability, not just size: "People buy groceries in a boom, and they buy groceries in a bust. A forecast of 12% growth from Walmart is a lot easier to believe than a forecast of 70% growth from anybody." Defensive earnings can be projected with some confidence because last year's demand is a good guide to next year's. Cyclical earnings sit on top of customer spending that can vanish when budgets tighten. A chip maker's forecast depends on a handful of big buyers continuing to build data centers, and the video noted the host's memory of Lucent around 2000, when forecasts looked terrific right up until customers stopped calling. The market therefore applies a discount to cyclical profits, because the "E" is less trustworthy, and a premium to defensive ones.

Investors use the distinction in several practical ways. First, they judge where a cyclical company sits in its cycle rather than trusting the current P/E. Many analysts use normalized or mid-cycle earnings, an average over several years, instead of one peak or trough year. Second, they watch leading signals: order backlogs, inventory levels, capital spending plans, and guidance for the next quarters. Third, they rotate. Cyclicals are often bought when the economy is bottoming and earnings look awful, which is when the P/E looks highest, and trimmed when earnings look spectacular. Defensives are favored when growth worries rise. Interest rates matter too: the video noted the 10-year Treasury at 5.15%, which competes directly with the earnings yield of safe stocks.

There are several gotchas. First, the labels are not permanent boxes. Many companies sit in between: Nvidia has a growth story and a cyclical-looking customer base at once, and a retailer like Walmart can be defensive in its groceries while its general merchandise is more discretionary. Second, defensive does not mean cheap or risk-free. The video noted that Walmart traded around 15 times expected profit in early 2017, and that its profits per share rose about 80% over the decade while the price paid for each dollar of profit nearly tripled. Safety can be overpriced, and a stock at 42 times earnings has "no room for a stumble." Third, a cyclical is not automatically a bad investment, and a low P/E is not automatically a trap. The video itself said the market might be down on Nvidia for the wrong reason or the right one.

It helps to see how this fails in each direction. When a cyclical stock breaks, earnings fall instead of rising as forecast, the multiple that looked low jumps, and shareholders lose twice: once on the profit decline, and again if investors then pay less for each remaining dollar. When a defensive stock breaks, it is usually through valuation rather than the business. If bond yields climb or growth disappoints, an expensive safe stock can be "marked down, too," even though people keep buying its products. The video framed it as two doors for Nvidia: either analysts cut the $15.83 forecast and "the stock was never cheap," or the profits arrive anyway and the selloff was a mistake. Both doors apply to any cyclical, and neither one is a free lunch.

This concept connects to several others. It builds on the forward P/E, since the cyclical problem is that forward earnings are the shakiest number in the fraction, and on the earnings yield, the inverse of P/E, which the video used to compare Nvidia's 7.4% and Walmart's 2.3% against a 5.15% Treasury. It ties to risk premium and discount rates: uncertain cash flows deserve a higher required return, which means a lower price. It links to concentration risk, since Nvidia is roughly 7% of the S&P 500, so index investors own the cyclical bet whether they chose it or not. The takeaway: a low P/E isn't a sale tag, and a high P/E isn't quality. Each is the price of a judgment about how much to trust the earnings.
