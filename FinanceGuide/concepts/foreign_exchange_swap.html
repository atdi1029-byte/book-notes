Foreign Exchange (FX) Swap

A foreign exchange swap is two currency trades bolted together. In the first leg, two parties exchange one currency for another at today's spot rate. In the second leg, they agree to reverse the exchange on a set future date at a rate fixed today, the forward rate. Economically, it works like a collateralized loan. Each side holds the other's currency for a while, then hands it back. Neither side is betting on where the exchange rate goes, because the return trade is locked in. A European bank that needs dollars but holds euros can use a swap to borrow dollars for a week or three months. It doesn't have to sell its euros outright or take on currency risk.

It matters because the dollar sits at the center of global finance, and most of the world's banks and funds can't simply print or deposit-gather their way to dollars. The video points out that a huge share of trade invoicing, commodity pricing, and cross-border lending is denominated in dollars. A Japanese life insurer holding US bonds, or a European bank lending dollars to a shipping company, needs a steady supply of dollars to fund those positions. FX swaps are one of the main pipes that carry dollars to them. The market is enormous. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) reports that FX swaps are the most traded instrument in the world's currency markets, with trillions of dollars changing hands on an average day. When this pipe works, nobody notices it. When it clogs, the effects show up everywhere.

Here is the mechanism, step by step. First, the bank and its counterparty agree on the amount, the maturity, and the spot rate. Second, on the start date the bank sends euros and receives dollars. Third, during the life of the swap the bank uses the dollars, perhaps to fund a dollar loan or to meet a dollar liability. Fourth, on the maturity date the bank sends back the same dollar amount it received and gets back the same euro amount it gave up. The forward rate is not a forecast. It is set by the interest rate differential between the two currencies, so the gap between spot and forward (the forward points) is effectively the interest paid for borrowing dollars against euros. That is why the contract can be seen as a loan.

Take a hypothetical case with round numbers. Euro/dollar spot is 1.1000. A bank swaps €100 million for $110 million for three months. Suppose dollar interest rates are 5% and euro rates are 3%. Interest-rate parity then implies a forward rate near 1.1055, so the bank pays back about $110.55 million to get its €100 million back. Now suppose dollars are scarce, and the extra cost of dollar funding, called the cross-currency basis, is 30 basis points (0.30%) a year. Over three months that adds roughly 0.075%, pushing the forward to about 1.1063. The bank pays about $83,000 more for the same loan. The numbers are illustrative, but the logic is the same one the video describes.

"A European bank takes its euros, hands them to an American counterparty overnight, and receives US dollars in return. Simultaneously, the two parties enter a forward contract, requiring them to reverse the transaction at a specified future date at a locked-in exchange rate."

The video makes two claims about size and tenor, and both need care. It says over 70% of swaps mature in under a year and that a large portion roll over weekly. The short-maturity part is broadly consistent with BIS data, which shows most FX swap turnover is at very short tenors, from overnight to a week. This is the crux of the risk. A loan that matures in days must be refinanced constantly, so the borrower is always one failed rollover away from trouble. The video's headline figure of $65 trillion is harder to pin down. The BIS's widely cited estimate for dollar obligations from FX swaps, forwards, and currency swaps has been larger, around $80 trillion in 2022. It also covers non-banks, such as funds and insurers, not only commercial banks. Treat the exact figure as an estimate, because it varies by date and definition.

Practitioners use FX swaps in several ways. Banks use them to fund dollar assets from euro or yen deposits. Asset managers and insurers use them to hedge currency risk on foreign bonds: a Japanese investor holding Treasuries sells dollars forward through a rolling swap, so yen moves don't wipe out the return. Corporations use them to manage cash across subsidiaries. Traders use them to read the market's stress level. The key gauge is the cross-currency basis, which the video tells you to watch. The more negative the basis on euro/dollar or yen/dollar, the more extra a borrower must pay to get dollars through swaps. A basis drifting deeply negative is a signal that dollar funding is tightening, even when stock indexes look calm.

Several nuances get lost in dramatic retellings. First, "off-balance-sheet" does not mean "unregulated" or "hidden from supervisors." Banks are regulated, and swap exposures are reported and carry capital and liquidity requirements. What the BIS flagged is that standard statistics and balance sheets do not show these dollar obligations as debt, because accounting treats the swap as a derivative, so the true dollar leverage of the system is easy to underestimate. Second, the large notional figure is not a pile of losses waiting to be realized. Each swap is matched by an asset, the currency the lender holds as collateral. Third, the swap itself carries little exchange-rate risk. The danger lies in rollover risk, the chance the funding can't be renewed.

The failure scenario is a dollar shortage. If lenders hoard dollars, or fear for the borrower's creditworthiness, the basis blows out and the swap becomes very expensive or simply unavailable. The borrower still owes dollars on the maturity date, so it must find them elsewhere, often by selling dollar assets such as Treasuries, equities, or loans. That forced selling can hit markets that have nothing to do with the original problem. March 2020 and the 2008 crisis are the classic examples, when basis spreads blew out and central banks stepped in. The video's toll-booth analogy captures this: truckers with plenty of cargo but no dollars to pay the toll must sell their goods at fire-sale prices. The analogy is vivid, but its "70% discount" detail is rhetorical, not a measured market figure.

The backstop is the central bank liquidity swap line, which the video describes as the Fed "printing dollars" to bail out foreigners. The mechanism is more constrained than that framing suggests. The Fed swaps dollars for an equivalent amount of foreign currency with a partner central bank, such as the ECB, Bank of Japan, Bank of England, Swiss National Bank, or Bank of Canada. The partner then lends those dollars to its own banks, and it bears the credit risk. At maturity the swap is reversed at the original exchange rate, and the Fed earns interest. Its purpose is to put a ceiling on dollar funding stress, which protects US markets from the kind of fire sales described above. It is a safety valve, not a gift. When the Fed cuts the price or widens access, it usually means the stress is real.

FX swaps connect to many other ideas in this guide. They are tied to covered interest parity, the principle that the forward rate should reflect interest differentials, and the cross-currency basis measures how far reality drifts from that principle. They share plumbing with the repo market, because both are short-term secured funding that must be rolled constantly, and stress in one often spreads to the other. They connect to Treasury demand and dollar strength, since scrambling for dollars tends to lift the dollar and pressure other assets. For your own portfolio, the practical takeaways are to watch funding-stress gauges and to know whether your international holdings are currency hedged. Treat any single "$65 trillion" headline as a prompt to look at the underlying data, not as a prediction.
