Fuel Surcharge Clause

A fuel surcharge clause is a provision built into freight, shipping, and logistics contracts that automatically adjusts the price a customer pays based on movements in a published fuel benchmark, most commonly the Energy Information Administration's (EIA) weekly national average diesel price. Instead of a trucking company or freight broker having to renegotiate rates every time diesel prices move, the contract contains a formula: as the reference diesel price rises above (or falls below) a baseline, the surcharge percentage on top of the base freight rate rises or falls with it. It's essentially a pre-agreed pass-through mechanism, so neither party has to argue about fuel costs mid-contract — the math just happens on a schedule, usually weekly.

This clause matters because fuel is one of the largest and most volatile cost inputs in any business that moves physical goods — trucking fleets, food distributors, farms shipping crops, manufacturers relying on freight, even retailers paying for last-mile delivery. Diesel is uniquely inelastic: as the video explains, a truck hauling frozen food "cannot carpool," a locomotive can't skip a trip, and a combine has a narrow harvest window. There's no substitute fuel and no waiting for a better price. That means diesel cost spikes don't get absorbed quietly — they get passed down the supply chain to whoever is next in line, eventually landing in grocery prices and other consumer goods. The fuel surcharge clause is the literal mechanism by which that pass-through happens contractually.

Mechanically, it works like this: the contract references a specific public data point — almost always the EIA's weekly retail diesel price, sometimes regional rather than national — and defines a surcharge table or formula tied to it. For example, a contract might specify that for every $0.05 increase in the EIA diesel price above a $2.50 baseline, the surcharge adds 1% to the freight bill. Because the EIA publishes this number every Monday, the surcharge resets automatically on a fixed schedule, with no negotiation, no phone call, and often no explicit notification to the shipper. It's baked into the paperwork and triggers itself.

The video's host ties this directly to the record diesel spike: the EIA put the national average retail diesel price at $6.53 a gallon for the week ending the 21st — an all-time record, 12% above the prior record of $5.81 set in June 2022, and up about 74% from roughly $3.75 a year earlier. Regionally it was worse: the Midwest, where combines were actively running, sat at $6.68 and had jumped 43 cents in a single week. Any shipper or carrier with a fuel surcharge clause tied to that EIA number saw their surcharge reprice automatically that week — regardless of whether they'd been following the news about crude oil, refinery utilization, or the Hormuz crisis. The host makes the point explicitly:

"If you run a business that moves anything, go find the fuel surcharge clause in your freight contracts. Most reset off this exact weekly government number — that surcharge is already repricing whether anybody told you or not."

For business owners and operators, the practical application is straightforward but often neglected: know your index and know your lag. Find out exactly which published number your contract references (national EIA average, PADD-specific regional averages, or a private index), how often it resets, and what the formula actually is. This turns fuel surcharges from a mystery line item into a forecastable cost. If diesel is climbing the way it did in this episode — driven by a widening crack spread rather than crude itself — an operator can look at the EIA's weekly release and calculate their next surcharge before the invoice even arrives, rather than being surprised by it. This is basic cost forecasting and cash-flow planning, not speculation.

The biggest misconception is assuming that a falling crude oil price means falling shipping costs. As the video hammers home, crude and diesel can move in completely opposite directions because the real constraint sits at the refinery, not the wellhead. A fuel surcharge clause tracks diesel (or sometimes a broader fuel index), not crude — so a business owner watching oil futures drop and assuming relief is coming for their freight bill is watching the wrong number entirely. Another subtlety: many surcharge formulas use a lagging average (a weekly or even monthly look-back), which means the surcharge you're paying today may reflect fuel prices from days or weeks ago, creating a mismatch between what's happening at the pump right now and what's showing up on your invoice.

Where this breaks down or creates risk is in contracts with poorly defined or outdated baselines. If a contract's fuel surcharge baseline was set years ago when diesel averaged $3.00 a gallon, and diesel is now sitting at $6.53, the surcharge percentages can become enormous relative to the base rate — sometimes doubling effective freight costs, as the video's farm example shows (diesel cost per acre going from $9 to $18 on corn and soybean operations, or $18,000 versus $9,000 on a 1,000-acre farm). Carriers and shippers who never revisit these baselines can end up either badly overcharging or, more dangerously for a trucking company, undercharging and eating losses if their clause is capped or poorly indexed. In extreme volatility, some contracts also have surcharge caps that stop protecting the carrier once diesel moves far enough, exposing them directly to the spread.

This concept connects directly to the crack spread discussed elsewhere in the same analysis — the actual driver of the diesel price the surcharge is indexed to — and to basic risk management and position sizing for any business with fuel exposure. Just as a trader hedges commodity exposure, a logistics-dependent business effectively has an embedded short position on diesel prices unless its contracts pass that risk through via a surcharge clause. Understanding and actively monitoring this clause is a low-effort, high-value risk management practice: it converts an invisible, reactive cost shock into a visible, forecastable one, and it's one of the clearest real-world links between a macro energy data release and a small business owner's weekly bottom line.
