Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) Export Capacity

Liquefied natural gas (LNG) is natural gas that has been chilled to roughly minus 260 degrees Fahrenheit, where it becomes a liquid that takes up about 1/600th of the space. That shrinkage is the whole point. Gas in its normal form can only travel through pipelines, and pipelines do not cross oceans. Turn it into a liquid and it can be loaded onto a specialized tanker and sold to a buyer on another continent. LNG export capacity is the maximum amount of gas the liquefaction plants, called terminals, can chill and load in a day. It is measured in billion cubic feet per day (Bcf/d). Think of it as the width of a doorway: no matter how many people want through, only so many can pass at once.

This matters because capacity is what links separate regional gas markets together. Without it, the US, Europe, and Asia would each have their own isolated price set by local supply and demand. The video's central claim is that the doorway is narrow enough that a spike overseas does not automatically become a spike at home. Europe's benchmark, the Dutch TTF, closed near €86.8 per megawatt hour, up about 168% from a year earlier, which works out to roughly $28 per million BTUs. Henry Hub, the US benchmark, traded near $3.24. That is a gap of nearly nine to one on the same molecule. Capacity decides how much of that gap can be exploited and how much of the foreign price leaks back into American bills.

Mechanically, the chain runs in steps. Gas is produced from wells and travels by pipeline to a coastal terminal. The terminal pulls out impurities like water and carbon dioxide, then pushes the gas through liquefaction trains, which are massive refrigeration units driven by compressors. The resulting liquid goes into insulated storage tanks, then is pumped onto a tanker. The tanker sails, and a receiving terminal warms the liquid back into gas, called regasification. The bottleneck is the middle step. Each train is engineered around the compressors' power and heat-exchange limits. As the video puts it:

"Each one can only swallow so much gas in a day. And that is a hard mechanical limit. No matter what Europe bids, a liquefaction train cannot process more gas than its compressors were engineered to handle."

The video gives numbers that show the scale. Gas flowing into US LNG terminals, called feed gas, averaged about 17.4 Bcf/d in October, while US production ran about 110.6 Bcf/d. So exports absorbed roughly 16% of domestic output. September had averaged about 18 Bcf/d, so October was actually lower, because Freeport LNG had a delayed restart and Cove Point had planned maintenance. This is the telling detail: Europe's price hit its highest level since 2022 in the same month American export flows fell. If price alone pulled gas out of the US, flows would have risen. Instead, the physical state of the plants set the volume, and price only decided how profitable each cargo was.

If the pipe is full, where does the extra European money go? Into the pockets of whoever owns the cargo. Many US export contracts work like a toll road. The buyer pays about 115% of Henry Hub for the gas, plus a fixed liquefaction fee that historically runs roughly $2.25 to $3.50 per million BTUs. The terminal owner collects the fee regardless of what Europe pays. The buyer then sells the cargo wherever the price is best. A standard tanker carries around 3.7 million million-BTU units, so a $25 gap yields a gross spread near $90 million per ship, with tens of millions left after freight and voyage costs. That spread is trader profit, not a signal that sends more gas out of Texas. This is the "firewall" that keeps US prices tied to US supply.

Traders and investors watch capacity in several practical ways. The first is the spread between TTF (or Asian benchmarks) and Henry Hub. A very wide spread with full terminals means cargo owners earn windfalls, which can help LNG shippers, traders, and owners of uncontracted capacity. The second is weekly feed gas data, which acts like a real-time utilization reading. When Freeport and Cove Point return from outages, feed gas jumps and domestic demand for gas rises, which is mildly bullish for Henry Hub. The third is the EIA's Thursday storage report, compared to the five-year average, which shows whether the US market is loose or tight. Fourth, watch Europe's storage, which the video put near 74%, the lowest for this time of year since 2011.

There are several gotchas. The first is the viral assumption that high foreign prices automatically raise your bill. That only holds if export capacity has room to grow. The second is trusting statistics without sources. The video's host searched for the widely repeated claim that terminals run at "98%" utilization, could not find its origin, and refused to repeat it. Good practice: separate nameplate capacity (what a plant is designed for) from actual feed gas (what it really takes), because outages and maintenance push the second below the first. The third is confusing gas with oil. The real winter shock the video identifies is heating oil, up about 21% for roughly 4.1 million homes, driven by tight distillate stocks, not LNG.

The firewall can break, and the video is clear that it is not permanent. Capacity is growing. Golden Pass in Texas shipped its first cargo on April 22 and is still ramping up, and Corpus Christi finished its stage three expansion on August 28. The EIA expects US LNG exports to average 17.6 Bcf/d this year and 18.6 next year. Each new terminal is, in the video's words, "another straw in the same glass." More straws mean more of Europe's price can reach back into the US market. The defense is production, which the EIA expects to rise from about 112.2 to 116.1 Bcf/d, keeping Henry Hub near $3.16 in 2027. If drillers stop keeping up, the gap narrows from the American side, as US gas becomes more expensive.

Other risks run the opposite way. Terminal outages are a real hazard: a single plant going down strands gas in the domestic market, pushing Henry Hub lower, while cargo prices abroad stay high. Weather can also override the whole structure. The video recalls January 23 of this year, when weekend cash gas at Henry Hub surged above $28, matching Europe's price for a couple of days. That was caused by cold, not exports. Finally, geopolitics can reshape demand. The Strait of Hormuz disruption left Persian Gulf LNG flows more than 75% below pre-war levels, and the EU ban on long-term Russian LNG contracts begins January 1, 2027. If Europe enters January short on storage, the spread could widen further.

LNG capacity connects to several other concepts. It relates to basis and spreads, since the TTF versus Henry Hub gap is a spread that persists because of a physical constraint rather than a lack of arbitrageurs. It ties to storage and seasonality, because the EIA storage numbers, currently about 2% above the five-year average, tell you whether the domestic market has a cushion. It overlaps with energy substitution, since heating oil and natural gas compete for the same household use, yet the video shows they can move in opposite directions. And it illustrates a broader lesson: when a price gap looks like free money, check whether the physical plumbing allows anyone to close it. The takeaway is simple. Cheap American gas is cheap because production outruns the ability to ship it, and every new terminal sells a little more of that discount to someone else.
