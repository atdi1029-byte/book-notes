Stable Value Fund

A stable value fund is an investment option offered almost exclusively inside employer retirement plans such as 401(k)s and 403(b)s. It is built to do one thing: show a share price that only goes up, usually by a small amount each month, while paying more than a money market fund. It holds a portfolio of intermediate-term bonds, commonly with an average maturity of two to five years, and pairs that portfolio with insurance contracts that guarantee participants can withdraw at book value, meaning the amount they put in plus credited interest. The label "capital preservation" on a plan menu often points to one. It is a stable-price product, not a cash product, and that difference is the whole story.

It matters because stable value is one of the largest corners of retirement savings. The video's figure of roughly $900 billion is in the right range, and tens of millions of workers are exposed through default or conservative menu choices. People move money here when stocks and bonds both look scary, which is exactly when they most need to be able to move again. If you think you hold cash but actually hold a bond portfolio with contractual strings attached, you can misjudge your emergency liquidity, your retirement-date flexibility, and how safe the balance really is. The product also shapes how plan sponsors and insurers think about risk, because the guarantee sits on insurance balance sheets.

Mechanically, there are three layers. First, the underlying portfolio: Treasuries, agency securities, high-grade corporates, and asset-backed bonds, whose market prices move with interest rates. Second, the wrap contract, a financial guarantee sold by banks or insurers such as the video's Prudential, MetLife, and Voya. The wrap lets the fund account for the bonds at book value rather than daily market value. Third, the crediting rate, a formula that smooths gains and losses by spreading them across the portfolio's duration. If bonds lose value, the crediting rate drops below what the bonds currently yield until the gap is earned back. Participants see a calm line; the volatility is absorbed in the accounting.

The video gives the real-world stress case. As yields on long Treasuries rose toward the 5.3% to 5.6% range it cites, the market prices of intermediate bonds fell. The video claims the market-to-book ratio of stable value pools dropped to 85-88%, so that $100 on your screen is backed by only about $85 of bonds, with the wrap covering the rest. Treat those exact numbers with caution. The 2022 rate shock did push industry market-to-book ratios below 100%, and many funds fell into the low-to-mid 90s, but that is a smaller gap than the video describes. The mechanism is real, though: a book value of $100 against a market value of $85 would be a 15% shortfall that only the wrap stands behind.

"A stable value fund does not hold cold hard cash, they hold intermediate-term physical bonds sitting on massive unrealized losses."

The key contrast is with a money market fund. A money market fund is regulated by the SEC under Rule 2a-7 to hold very short paper such as Treasury bills, repo, and commercial paper, with a weighted average maturity capped at 60 days. Because the holdings mature so fast, losses from rate moves are tiny. A stable value fund is not a registered money market fund and has no such cap. The video's complaint that advisors treat them as identical is fair: both show a flat price, but one gets it from short maturities and the other gets it from accounting plus an insurance promise. The stable value fund typically pays more, and that extra yield is compensation for taking on duration, credit risk, and the exit restrictions described below.

Investors use stable value as the ballast in a retirement allocation, the place to sit when you want bond-like income without watching daily losses. It tends to beat money market yields when rates are falling, because the crediting rate lags and keeps paying older, higher yields. It tends to trail when rates are rising quickly, because the crediting rate adjusts slowly while money market yields reset immediately. That lag is a practical signal. The video's playbook is to read the annual disclosure for the market-to-book ratio. A ratio near 100% means the wrap is barely needed. The video treats anything below 92% as a warning sign. That cutoff is the presenter's rule of thumb, not an industry standard, but a falling ratio does show the wrap is carrying more weight.

The gotchas are where the video has a point, though some of its terms are loose. Stable value funds usually carry competing fund restrictions, often called equity wash rules: you generally cannot move straight from stable value into a money market fund or short-term bond fund in the same plan. You first have to park the money in a non-competing option, typically for about 90 days. These rules stop participants from chasing higher money market yields while the stable value fund's crediting rate lags. The video describes this as being forced into equities, but plans commonly allow a longer-duration bond or balanced fund as the pass-through, so check yours. Also, the guarantee covers participant-initiated, benefit-responsive withdrawals such as retirement, death, or hardship, not every kind of event.

Failure comes from two places. The first is the employer-initiated event. Wrap contracts exclude things like a plan sponsor terminating the plan, laying off many workers, switching recordkeepers, or moving the fund to a competing option, because those can trigger mass withdrawals the wrap provider never priced. In those cases the insurer may pay out at market value rather than book, or require a delay, commonly up to 12 months. The video presents the 12-month gate as a statutory right exercised against workers. In practice it is a contractual term tied to sponsor decisions, and it is rarely invoked. The second is the wrap provider's credit: a book-value promise is only as good as the institution behind it, and funds usually spread across several wraps to limit that risk.

Stable value connects to several other ideas in fixed income. Duration explains why rising yields hurt the underlying bonds, and mark-to-market versus book value accounting explains why the loss is hidden on your statement. The same logic is why held-to-maturity bonds at banks can hide unrealized losses, as with Silicon Valley Bank in 2023. The video's alternative, Treasury bills held directly or in a government-only money market fund, avoids the wrap, the crediting formula, and the transfer rules. Note the trade-off: T-bill yields reset with short rates and carry reinvestment risk. The video closes on this point: a guarantee is only as strong as the balance sheet and the contract language behind it, so read both before treating a stable price as safety.
