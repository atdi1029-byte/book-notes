Ample Reserves Regime and Reserve Management Purchases

Every bank in the United States keeps an account at the Federal Reserve, and the balance in that account is called bank reserves. Think of reserves as the cash that banks use to settle payments with one another. They are not the paper bills in a vault. They are digital balances on the Fed's ledger. An ample reserves regime is the way the Fed runs interest rates today. It keeps the banking system so well supplied with reserves that no bank has to scramble for them. Because reserves are plentiful, the Fed does not need to adjust their quantity day by day. It steers short-term rates by setting the interest rate it pays on reserves. Reserve management purchases are the Fed's tool for keeping reserves ample as the economy grows: it buys Treasury bills to add reserves to the system.

This matters because nearly every borrowing cost in the economy is anchored to overnight funding rates, and those rates depend on how plentiful reserves are. If reserves run short, banks and dealers bid against each other for overnight cash, and the rate they pay can jump well above the Fed's target. That spike flows into repo markets, money market fund yields, short-term business borrowing and, more loosely, mortgage and loan pricing. If you hold a money market fund, your yield tracks these rates. If you borrow, your cost follows them. The Fed wants one thing from this regime: that its policy rate, not a temporary cash shortage, sets the price of money. Reserve management is the maintenance work that makes that possible.

To see the mechanism, start with the Fed's ledger, which has two sides that move against each other. When the Treasury sells bills, investors pay from their bank accounts. Cash leaves the banks, so reserves fall, and it lands in the Treasury's account at the Fed, so that balance rises. Dollar for dollar, the Treasury's cash goes up and reserves go down. When the Treasury spends, the flow reverses. The Fed can offset the drain by buying bills. It pays by crediting banks' reserve accounts, which creates new reserves. In an ample regime, the Fed's control comes from two administered rates. The interest on reserves sets a floor for what banks will accept. The standing repo facility, the Fed's backstop lending window, sets a ceiling. Overnight rates then trade between the two.

The video gives a concrete picture of how this played out. The Treasury's checking account stood at $891 billion on the day of its November 19 auction of a 9-day cash management bill, and the Treasury said the balance could peak near $1.025 trillion in late November. Meanwhile, bank reserves were roughly $3 trillion on November 18, about $84 billion higher than a year earlier. The video notes that the Fed began buying bills last December to keep reserves topped up, then set those purchases to zero on August 14 because reserves looked ample. The New York Fed's schedule covering November 16 through December 11 also showed zero. Overnight rates sat near 3.9%, inside the Fed's 3.75% to 4% target range and just under the 4% backstop rate.

"Last December, the Fed began buying Treasury bills specifically to keep bank reserves topped up. On August 14th, those purchases went to zero."

The video also quotes the person running the Fed's market operations, Roberto Perli, who said in September that the purchases were paused because reserves looked ample and that they would come back if conditions changed. That is the key point about reserve management purchases. They are conditional and adjustable. They are not a stimulus program and not a bet on the economy. They work like a thermostat. When the system gets chilly and cash grows scarce, the Fed adds reserves. When it is comfortable, the Fed stops. Because the pause is explicit and the restart is promised, the schedule the New York Fed publishes is a clear signal. A move from zero to a positive number would be the Fed saying it now sees what the market sees.

Traders and investors watch a short list of signals. The first is the overnight funding rate relative to the Fed's range. Calm trading near the interest rate on reserves means the system has enough cash. A drift toward the top of the range, or heavy use of the standing repo facility, means reserves are getting scarce. The second is the Treasury's daily cash balance, which is published every business day. A rising balance drains reserves. The third is month-end and quarter-end, when banks and dealers shrink their balance sheets and cash is hardest to find. Finally, there is the New York Fed's purchase schedule. A resumption of buying would be read as a quiet admission that reserves had gotten tight, and traders would position for easier funding.

There are several common misconceptions. The first is that buying bills is the same as quantitative easing. The two differ in purpose and in what they buy. Quantitative easing is meant to push down longer-term rates and ease financial conditions by buying bonds at large scale. Reserve management purchases focus on bills and are meant only to keep the plumbing working, not to change the stance of policy. The video makes this point: the Fed's balance sheet was $186 billion larger than a year earlier, so this is not a quiet tightening. A second misconception is that "ample" means "abundant." Ample is the zone in between: enough that small swings in reserves barely move rates, but not so much that the Fed is flooding the system. The Fed has no precise number for where that zone ends.

Ample reserves can fail, and the failure is usually sudden. The best-known example is September 2019, when reserves had been reduced after years of balance sheet shrinkage. A mid-month tax date and a Treasury settlement drained cash in the same days, and the overnight repo rate spiked to around 10%, far above the Fed's target. The Fed then had to inject cash and later began buying bills. The danger is that the relationship between reserves and rates is nonlinear. Rates are quiet for a long time, then jump once reserves fall below a certain level. That is why the Fed errs on the side of keeping reserves slightly too high. The video flags the same type of risk. A big Treasury cash build lands on month end, with no cushion to absorb it.

This concept connects to several others. The first is the reverse repo facility, where money market funds park cash at the Fed. It once held $2.25 trillion, in May 2023, and had fallen to $331 million by last week. It used to absorb the Treasury's cash swings so that reserves never felt them. With that buffer gone, every dollar the Treasury raises comes out of reserves. The second is the debt ceiling. When the Treasury is constrained, it spends down its cash and reserves rise. When the limit is lifted, it must rebuild its balance quickly by issuing bills, and reserves drop. The video put the room left under the $41.1 trillion limit at $597 billion. Taken together, these pieces explain why the 9-day bill matters as a signal and why the New York Fed's schedule, due around December 11, is on the watch list.
