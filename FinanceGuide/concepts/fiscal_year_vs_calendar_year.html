Fiscal Year vs. Calendar Year

A fiscal year is the 12-month period a company uses for its accounting, budgeting, and financial reporting. A calendar year is the familiar January 1 to December 31. Many companies simply use the calendar year, but plenty do not. A company can start its fiscal year in any month, as long as it keeps the same 12-month window year after year. Microsoft's fiscal year runs July 1 through June 30. The label usually follows the year in which the period ends, so the year that began in July 2026 and ends in June 2027 is "fiscal 2027," or FY27. Think of it as the company's own private calendar, drawn to fit its business rather than the wall calendar.

This matters because nearly every number investors watch is tied to a reporting period: revenue, earnings per share, capital spending, guidance, and growth rates. If you don't know which period a company means, you can misread the figure by months, or even misplace it in the wrong year. Analysts compare companies, build models, and set expectations by period, so a mismatch ripples outward. It also affects how you read the news. A headline saying "2027 spending plan" could describe a stretch that is already underway, or one that hasn't started. Knowing the fiscal calendar tells you how far along a company is in its plan and how much of the year is still ahead.

Mechanically, a fiscal year is divided into four fiscal quarters, each three months long, numbered Q1 through Q4 from the start of the company's own year. For Microsoft, Q1 is July through September, Q2 is October through December, Q3 is January through March, and Q4 is April through June. After each quarter closes, the company has a few weeks to finalize its books and then announces results on an earnings call. Q4 is special: its results are paired with full-year totals, and the company then issues guidance, meaning its forecast, for the new fiscal year. So the late-July report both closes out one fiscal year and opens the next.

Microsoft's guidance of roughly 45% growth for the next quarter refers to the July-through-September period. Same company, same timeline, but labeled by Microsoft's calendar.

"That is a fiscal year, not a calendar year. It's important to know that Microsoft's year runs July through June."

Investors use this knowledge in practical ways. First, they line up companies on a common footing. To compare Microsoft with a calendar-year company, analysts often convert to calendar-year equivalents, regrouping quarters so both cover the same months. Second, they know when to expect news. Microsoft's big annual guidance arrives in late July, and its other reports land in October, January, and April. Third, they track pacing. If $50 billion of a $175 billion plan lands in the first quarter, that is roughly 29% of the year's budget in 25% of the time, suggesting spending that is front-loaded, which tells you something about urgency. Traders watch for those cues and for any revisions to the plan.

There are several gotchas. The biggest is the naming convention: "fiscal 2027" does not begin in 2027 for Microsoft; it began in July 2026. Other companies label differently, so never assume. Another trap is that a "Q1" for one company is not the same months as a "Q1" for another. Some firms use a 52- or 53-week year ending on a particular weekday, so their year-end drifts a few days. Retailers often end their fiscal year in late January or early February, after the holiday rush. And government figures use their own calendars: the U.S. federal fiscal year runs October through September. Always check the company's definition before comparing numbers.

Things go wrong when people mix periods. Someone might compare Microsoft's "full-year" results with a calendar-year competitor's and conclude one is growing faster, when really the two windows captured different slices of an accelerating or slowing business. A fast-growing company looks better over a window that ends later. Mismatches also cause errors when blending data sources: a model that treats the quarter ended June 30 as "Q2 2026," its calendar label, may collide with a company filing that calls it Q4 FY26. A company can even change its fiscal year-end, which forces a short "transition period" and breaks the comparability of historical trends. Leaving these cases unchecked produces confident but wrong conclusions.

Fiscal timing also shapes how to read the video's bigger story. Microsoft's buyout offer, the $175 billion capex plan, and the earnings release all sit in the same fiscal year, but they hit on different dates, so the sequence matters. The presenter notes that Microsoft had 223,000 employees as of June 30, which is the fiscal year-end snapshot. Headcount comparisons, such as 228,000 a year earlier, are year-over-year readings on the same fiscal date. Payroll savings from a buyout accepted by December would show up in the second half of fiscal 2027, helping margins just as the data center depreciation grows. Fiscal timing tells you when the costs and savings actually land on the income statement.

This concept connects to several others. Year-over-year comparisons only work when the periods match, which is why fiscal labels matter. Guidance and earnings season follow the fiscal calendar, not the wall calendar. Capital expenditures and depreciation spread across fiscal periods, shaping reported profit margins, the very margins the presenter says investors refuse to see shrink. And seasonality interacts with it too, since a company's fiscal year-end often sits right after its busiest or slowest stretch. If you can say "Microsoft's fiscal 2027 began in July 2026, and Q4 of fiscal 2026 ended in June," you can follow any earnings conversation on the Street with confidence.
