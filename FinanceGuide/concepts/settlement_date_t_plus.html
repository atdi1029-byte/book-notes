Trade Settlement (T+1)

Trade settlement is the moment a stock trade becomes final: the buyer's cash actually moves to the seller, and the shares actually move to the buyer. When you click "buy," nothing like that happens. You and the seller have only agreed on a price and a quantity, which is the trade date (written "T"). The settlement date comes later, when ownership officially changes hands. T+1 means settlement happens one business day after the trade. Buy on Monday and the deal settles Tuesday. Buy on Friday and it settles the following Monday, because weekends and market holidays don't count. Think of it as the gap between shaking hands on a house and closing day, when the money and the deed are exchanged.

Settlement matters because markets run on trust, and the gap between agreeing and delivering is where that trust is tested. Every trade creates a promise. The buyer promises cash and the seller promises shares. The longer that promise stays open, the more things can go wrong: a firm can fail, a price can swing hard, or one side can simply not deliver. Shorter settlement cycles shrink that exposure. The United States moved from T+2 to T+1 on May 28, 2024, and before that it had moved from T+3 to T+2 in 2017. Faster settlement means less money tied up as collateral, fewer unfinished trades in the system, and quicker access to your proceeds when you sell.

Mechanically, settlement is a chain of steps behind the scenes. First, the trade executes on an exchange such as the NASDAQ, and your broker confirms it to you. That evening and the next morning, the brokers on both sides send details to a clearinghouse, and in the United States that is the National Securities Clearing Corporation, part of the DTCC. The clearinghouse steps into the middle and becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. It also nets the trades, so a firm that bought and sold the same stock all day only moves the difference. On settlement day, shares move through the Depository Trust Company's electronic records and cash moves through banks, in what the industry calls delivery versus payment. Neither side gets one without the other.

The video gives a clean real-world example. Anthropic's IPO priced Wednesday night: 980 million shares at $83, or $81.34 billion in total. Thursday was the first day of trading on the NASDAQ. Friday was settlement day, which the presenter describes as the day the money actually has to show up in the account. That is T+1 working exactly as designed. The trade date was Thursday, so the cash was due Friday. Buying power was never the issue. A fund that was allocated shares at $83 had to have the money ready to wire. The clearing then splits it up: about $1.96 billion went to existing holders who sold in the deal, about $550 million to the 22 underwriters in fees, and the rest to the company.

"Today, Friday, is settlement day, the day the money actually has to show up in the account."

Investors and traders use settlement timing in practical ways. The first is cash management. If you sell a stock on Monday, the cash is officially yours Tuesday. Many brokers let you reuse that money sooner, but you should know when it is truly settled. The second is the ex-dividend date. Since T+1, the ex-dividend date generally falls on the record date itself. To receive a dividend, you must own the shares before the ex-date, and the trade has to settle in time for you to be on the company's books. The third is tax planning. Your trade date, not your settlement date, normally decides which tax year a sale falls into, but the two can land in different years around December 31, so year-end trades need care.

There are a few common misconceptions. The biggest is that the trade date and the settlement date are the same thing. They aren't, and the difference is why a brokerage can show "buying power" that isn't yet cash. A second one is that "T+1" means 24 hours. It means one business day, so a Friday trade settles Monday, and a trade before a holiday weekend can take longer. A third is that settlement is the same for everything. Stocks and ETFs settle T+1 in the US, but options settle T+1 as well, mutual funds can differ by fund, and other countries still run T+2 or longer. The video also shows a subtler point: settlement is when cash is due, not when the price was set. The $83 was fixed Wednesday night. Friday only delivered the money.

When settlement breaks, it is called a settlement fail or "fail to deliver." This happens when a seller doesn't have the shares to hand over, or a buyer can't produce the cash. The clearinghouse then buys the shares in the market at whatever price it takes to complete the trade, and the failing firm bears the cost, so there are penalties and margin requirements. For individual investors, the usual risk is a good faith violation. That happens in a cash account if you buy a stock with unsettled money and then sell it before the original funds settle. Repeat it, and the broker can restrict your account for 90 days. A shorter settlement cycle also leaves less time to fix errors, such as a missing funding wire or a mismatched trade record.

Settlement connects to several ideas in the video. An IPO adds a step, because the deal is priced the night before trading, then the stock starts trading, then it settles one business day later. Each date has a different purpose: price discovery, public trading, and the actual transfer of money. Settlement is also where the video's main theme becomes real. A claim like "$81 billion of orders" is only a promise until settlement turns it into wired cash. This connects to index inclusion too. When funds are forced to buy a new stock for the Nasdaq 100 or the Russell 1000, their trades settle a day later, and they usually have to sell other holdings to raise the cash. That is the mechanism behind the December pressure on your 401(k).
