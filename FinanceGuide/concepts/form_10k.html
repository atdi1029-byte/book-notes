Form 10-K (Annual Report Filing)

A Form 10-K is the annual report that every publicly traded U.S. company must file with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). Think of it as a company's mandatory, audited yearly physical, written down and made public. It contains the full financial statements (income statement, balance sheet, cash flow statement), a plain-language description of what the business does, a section on the risks management believes could hurt it, and a discussion in which executives explain the results in their own words. It is much longer and far less polished than the glossy annual report mailed to shareholders. Anyone can read it for free on the SEC's EDGAR database or on the company's investor relations page.

The reason it matters is that the 10-K is legally binding. When a CEO goes on television and says "our balance sheet is immune to higher rates," nobody can sue over a television appearance. The 10-K is different. Executives sign it, auditors examine the numbers, and knowingly false statements carry civil and even criminal liability under securities law. That makes it the most reliable public record of what a company owes, what it earns, and what could go wrong. Markets run on prices, but prices are only as good as the information behind them, and the 10-K is where the most trustworthy information is stored. It is the dividing line between marketing and accountability.

Mechanically, here is how it works. A company's fiscal year ends, and its accountants close the books. An independent auditor then examines the financial statements and issues an opinion on whether they fairly reflect reality. The company assembles the 10-K, which follows a standard structure: Item 1 (Business), Item 1A (Risk Factors), Item 7 (Management's Discussion and Analysis, or MD&A), Item 8 (Financial Statements and the notes to them), and others. Large companies, called large accelerated filers, must file within 60 days of fiscal year-end. Smaller ones get 75 or 90 days. Between 10-Ks, the company files shorter 10-Qs each quarter and 8-Ks for major events. The 10-K is the only one that is fully audited, which is why it is the anchor document.

Now to the video. The host warns of a $1.8 trillion wall of corporate debt coming due between late 2026 and the end of 2027, much of it borrowed at 2% to 3% in 2020 and 2021 and now facing refinancing at 7.5% to 9%. His step two tells investors to go to the investor relations page of their top three dividend holdings and read the 10-K's debt maturity disclosures. That is exactly where this information lives. Public companies must show a table, usually in the notes to the financial statements and summarized in the MD&A liquidity section, listing how much principal comes due in each of the next five years. It turns a vague worry about "debt walls" into a specific, checkable schedule for a specific company.

"Go to the investor relations page on your top three dividend holdings and examine their form 10-K disclosures under debt maturities by year."

Here is how to use it in practice. Open the 10-K and look for the contractual obligations or debt note, which shows maturities for each year ahead. Compare each year's maturing amount to the cash on the balance sheet and to annual free cash flow. A company with $500 million maturing in 2027 and $2 billion in cash is fine. One with $500 million maturing and $80 million in cash will have to refinance, and at today's rates. Use the video's own math: $500 million at 2.5% costs $12.5 million a year in interest, while the same debt at 8.5% costs $42.5 million. Then check the interest rate on each tranche, and ask whether the added interest would eat into the cash that funds the dividend. Compare the dividends paid, found in the cash flow statement, to free cash flow.

There are gotchas, and a careful reader should know them. First, a debt wall is not a death sentence. Maturing debt is normally refinanced, and healthy companies do this routinely. The signal is the combination of a large maturity, thin cash, weak interest coverage, and a much higher expected new rate. Second, the 10-K is backward-looking. It reports the balance sheet as of fiscal year-end, which could be ten months old by the time you read it. Debt issued or repaid since then appears only in the later 10-Qs, so read the most recent 10-Q too. Third, the document is dense, and key facts hide in footnotes, so a quick skim can mislead you.

Another nuance is that the video's dramatic framing needs checking against the filings themselves. Many large companies deliberately extended maturities in 2020 and 2021 and now have average debt lives of ten years or more, so a headline number like $1.8 trillion does not apply evenly to every company. Likewise, "dividend slashes" are not automatic. A board chooses to cut a dividend. The bondholders-first ordering is real, because interest is a legal obligation and dividends are discretionary, but plenty of companies will cut buybacks, capital spending, or debt paydown before touching a dividend they have raised for decades. Use the 10-K to find which companies have the least room to maneuver.

When it breaks, the failure is usually in the details management would rather not emphasize. Companies in trouble include a going concern warning, in which the auditor states there is "substantial doubt" the company can keep operating for another year. That is among the most serious red flags in any 10-K. Debt notes may also disclose covenants, the financial floors the video mentions, and any waivers or amendments the company obtained from lenders. Filings can also be late: an NT 10-K notice, which signals a delay, often precedes bad news. And on rare occasions, numbers are restated after the fact, which means earlier figures cannot be trusted. None of this is visible in a press release, which is why reading the filing matters.

The 10-K connects to several other ideas in this guide. The interest coverage ratio the host recommends, operating earnings divided by interest expense with a three-to-one floor, is calculated directly from the income statement in the 10-K. So is debt to EBITDA, built from the balance sheet and income statement. Free cash flow, which the host calls king, comes from the cash flow statement. The dividend payout ratio, share buybacks (which the video blames for weakening balance sheets), and the notion of a zombie company all rely on figures the 10-K provides. Learn to read it, and you can check any bold claim on financial television against the company's own sworn numbers.
