Deposit Flight (Deposit Rate Spread Outflows)

Deposit flight is what happens when customers pull money out of a bank because they can earn more somewhere else. It is not a panic in the movie sense, with lines around the block. It is quieter and more rational: someone notices their checking account pays almost nothing while a Treasury bill or money market fund pays over 4%, and they move the cash. The gap between what the bank pays and what the outside world pays is the deposit rate spread. When that spread gets wide enough, deposits start to leave. The video frames this as "catalyst number one" in a three-stage sequence that puts pressure on regional banks.

To see why it matters, you have to understand what a deposit is from the bank's side. Your balance is your asset, but it is the bank's liability, a debt the bank owes you on demand. Banks use that cheap, stable funding to make loans and buy securities, and they earn the difference between what those assets yield and what they pay depositors. That difference is the net interest margin. Deposits are the raw material of the whole business. If they shrink, or if the bank has to pay much more to keep them, the profit engine slows, and the bank may be forced to shrink its lending, which touches households and small businesses.

Mechanically, the process runs in steps. First, market rates rise, so short-term government paper yields, say, 4% or more. Second, the bank does not raise what it pays on checking and savings, because its own assets, such as older bonds and fixed-rate loans, still earn only around 2% and it cannot afford to pass on more. Third, the more rate-sensitive customers notice the gap and move cash to T-bills or money market funds. Fourth, the bank must replace those funds. Each dollar that leaves a 0.3% account and returns as a 4% borrowing turns a nearly free liability into an expensive one, and that hurts the margin directly.

The video gives specific numbers. Regional banks keep paying roughly 0.2% to 0.4% on consumer checking, while T-bills and government money funds yield over 4%. That is a spread of roughly 3.6 to 3.8 percentage points. On a $10 million corporate treasury balance, the difference is about $370,000 a year, which is more than enough to justify a phone call to the treasurer's broker. The narrator argues that "sophisticated" depositors, a group that now includes many people who are not especially wealthy, plus corporate treasurers, will not tolerate that anymore. They sweep their cash out, often with a few clicks.

"You and I wouldn't tolerate that. What they do though is they sweep their liquidity out of regional banks and park it directly into treasury bills or government money market funds yielding over 4%."

The next step in the video's chain shows why the outflow is painful, not merely inconvenient. To meet withdrawals, the bank needs cash. It could sell securities, but the narrator says its bonds sit in the held-to-maturity bucket, carried at full book value even though the market price is far lower. Total unrealized losses across US banks reached $325.1 billion per the FDIC. Selling even a small slice would, per the video, force the bank to mark the whole portfolio to market and expose the losses. So the bank instead borrows from wholesale sources like the Federal Home Loan Banks or the Fed funds market, paying 4% or more to replace money that used to cost almost nothing.

Investors and traders watch this dynamic through several indicators. One is the trend in total deposits from the Fed's weekly H.8 data and the FDIC's quarterly reports, especially the split between insured and uninsured balances, since large uninsured deposits move fastest. Another is deposit beta, the share of a rate increase that a bank passes on to depositors; a low beta looks profitable until customers leave. Others include rising borrowings from Federal Home Loan Banks, growth in money market fund assets, and a bank's net interest margin trend. A falling margin plus rising wholesale borrowing suggests a bank is paying up to keep funding.

There are nuances worth keeping straight. Deposit flight is not the same as a classic bank run. A run is driven by fear that the bank will fail; deposit flight is driven by yield and can happen at a perfectly healthy bank. Second, not all deposits are equally mobile. Small retail checking accounts are sticky because people value convenience, direct deposit, and habit, while large corporate balances are highly mobile. Third, a low-paying bank is not automatically in trouble. Many banks have loyal, low-cost deposits and a well-matched balance sheet. It is the combination of low deposit rates, low-yielding assets, and unrealized losses that creates the vulnerability the video describes. Also treat its claims critically; the video's tone is alarmist, and its stated figures should be checked against primary sources.

When this process goes wrong, it can feed on itself. If the outflow continues, the bank has to keep borrowing at higher cost, so its margin can turn negative: assets yielding 2% funded by liabilities costing 4%. Capital erodes gradually. The larger risk is that the yield-driven exit turns into a confidence-driven one. Once depositors, especially uninsured ones, suspect losses are hidden in the securities book, they may leave faster, and that is what happened at Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023, where a bond portfolio under water met a concentrated base of uninsured, highly connected depositors. The bank had to sell securities, realized the losses, and failed within days.

For ordinary households, the video argues the effect shows up as tighter credit. A bank losing deposits and squeezed on capital protects itself by lending less: fewer approved home equity lines, lower limits on small-business credit lines, trimmed credit card limits, and more friction on large wires. You will not see a press release; you will see stricter terms. Deposit flight also connects to several other ideas in this guide, including interest rate risk, duration mismatch, held-to-maturity accounting, and Fed policy, since higher policy rates are what widen the spread. The practical lesson for savers is simple: your cash is not required to earn 0.3%. Understand deposit insurance limits, compare yields, and recognize that the same spread tempting you to move is the one pressuring your bank.
