Standing Repo Facility

The Standing Repo Facility, or SRF, is a permanent Federal Reserve program that lends cash overnight to eligible banks and dealers. The borrower puts up Treasury securities or agency debt as collateral. A repo, short for repurchase agreement, is a collateralized loan. You hand over a bond, receive cash today, and agree to buy the bond back tomorrow at a slightly higher price. That small price difference is the interest. The Fed launched the SRF in July 2021 so the repo market would always have a lender of last resort. Think of it as a discount window for the Treasury market, open to the firms that move trillions of dollars of bonds every day.

Repo is the plumbing under nearly everything in the bond market. Dealers fund their inventories of Treasuries there, hedge funds finance leveraged trades there, and money market funds park spare cash there. When overnight funding gets scarce, rates spike. Those spikes can force bond sales, widen spreads, and eventually reach the mortgage and corporate borrowing rates ordinary people pay. September 2019 showed the danger. Overnight repo rates briefly jumped to roughly 10% with almost no warning, and the Fed had to improvise emergency lending. The SRF exists so that never has to be improvised again. Its mere presence is meant to cap funding stress, which keeps the Fed's target rate meaningful in the real market and not only on paper.

Here is how it works, step by step. An eligible counterparty, such as a primary dealer or a bank, offers Treasuries, agency debt, or agency mortgage-backed securities as collateral. The Fed lends cash against it and applies a haircut, meaning the collateral is worth a bit more than the loan, so the Fed has a cushion. Operations run each morning and settle for overnight funding. The next day the trade unwinds: the borrower repays the cash with interest and gets the bonds back. The rate is a fixed ceiling set at the top of the Fed's target range for the federal funds rate. If the range tops out at 4.00%, the facility charges 4.00%. Because that price is posted in advance, nobody has to guess what emergency funding will cost.

The ceiling works through simple arbitrage. Why would any dealer pay 4.25% in the private market when the Fed will lend against the same bonds at 4.00%? It wouldn't, so private lenders have to price at or below the Fed's rate or lose the business. In that sense the SRF is a tool whose value comes from being available, not from being used. A fire extinguisher on the wall is doing its job even if it is never discharged. The mirror image is the reverse repo facility, which sits at the bottom of the range and pays cash lenders a floor rate. Together the two facilities form a corridor that boxes in overnight interest rates.

Now the video's example. Around the most recent quarter-end, normally one of the most stressful days for funding, the SRF took in exactly zero. Overnight SOFR, the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, which is the benchmark average of what repo borrowers actually pay, fell two basis points to 3.88%. That was flat with the fed funds rate and 12 basis points below the facility's ceiling. Meanwhile the reverse repo facility, where cash flows the opposite way, jumped to $11.4 billion. The presenter's reading was that money wasn't scrambling to borrow from the Fed, it was flooding into the Fed. That undercut the story of a quarter-end margin crisis.

"Money is not struggling to borrow from the Fed at this point. Cash is flooding into it. That is not a collateral squeeze. That is a cash glut."

Traders read the SRF as a stress gauge. Three numbers matter: SOFR relative to the ceiling, the gap between SOFR and the reverse repo rate at the floor, and the daily SRF take-up. SOFR drifting toward the ceiling means cash is getting scarce. Meaningful SRF usage means private lenders are charging more than the Fed, so someone is paying up for funding. Watch the days when pressure typically peaks: month-end, quarter-end, mid-month Treasury settlement dates, and tax dates. Basis-trade hedge funds care especially, because their financing cost is their profit margin. When the spread they chase is only about five basis points, a few basis points of funding stress can erase the whole trade.

Several misconceptions deserve correction. First, zero usage does not mean the facility is useless. A backstop that stays idle is the design working. Second, low usage does not prove there is no stress anywhere. The facility only sees institutions that are eligible and willing to borrow, and there is a stigma problem: firms fear that borrowing from the Fed signals weakness, so they may pay a bit more privately instead. Third, much of the bilateral repo market, where the video notes 75% of trades carry a zero haircut, stays largely out of public view. Finally, one day proves little. A glut at one quarter-end can coexist with real tightness on a different date.

What happens when it fails or falls short? The classic risk is a facility that sits unused when it should be used. If stigma runs high, stress can build in private markets, rates can blow through the ceiling, and borrowers still hesitate to take the Fed's money. Second, the SRF lends against collateral, so it solves a shortage of cash, not a solvency problem or a collapse in bond prices. Haircuts and valuations still move. Third, only certain firms can borrow directly, so stress at hedge funds has to pass through dealers whose balance sheets are limited. That is why SOFR versus the ceiling over several weeks tells you more than any single day's take-up.

The SRF connects to nearly every idea in the video. It backstops the repo financing behind the basis trade, where hedge funds buy cash Treasuries, sell futures, and borrow against the bonds at thin haircuts. It pairs with the reverse repo facility to form the rate corridor. It interacts with the enhanced supplementary leverage ratio, whose loosening in April lets big dealers hold more Treasuries and therefore lend more in repo. It even touches mortgages, because calm funding means dealers demand a smaller volatility buffer. The takeaway for any market conversation: the SRF is the Fed's promise that overnight money will never become unlimitedly expensive.
