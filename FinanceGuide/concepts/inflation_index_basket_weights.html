Inflation Index Basket Weights (Composition Effect)

Every price index is really a recipe. You take a list of goods and services, assign each one a percentage of the total — called a basket weight — and then track how prices change across that mix. The final inflation number you see on television is not a universal truth about price behavior in the economy. It is a weighted average, and the weights are a set of deliberate choices made by statisticians. The composition effect refers to the way those weighting decisions distort the headline number relative to other indexes tracking the same underlying economy. Two indexes can look at the same price universe and return very different readings simply because they assigned different importance to each category.

This matters enormously because different institutions use different indexes. The Federal Reserve officially targets the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index (PCE), produced by the Bureau of Economic Analysis. Most financial media reports on the Consumer Price Index (CPI), produced by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. These are not interchangeable. When the gap between them widens — which it can do dramatically, as it did in 2025 — monetary policy is being set on a completely different number than the one the public is watching. That divergence moves interest rates, mortgage costs, equity valuations, and bond prices. Misreading it is not an academic mistake; it is a real-money mistake with measurable consequences for households and portfolios.

The mechanical driver of the composition effect is simple arithmetic. If shelter — rent, owners' equivalent rent, lodging costs — makes up 34% of CPI but only 16% of PCE, then a change in shelter prices has roughly twice the impact on CPI than it does on PCE. Run that math in a period where shelter inflation is falling — what economists call disinflation — and CPI gets a powerful drag downward while PCE barely registers the improvement. The opposite is also true: categories that carry heavier weight in PCE than in CPI, such as healthcare delivered through employer plans and government programs, financial services, and technology, pull PCE upward with force that CPI simply cannot feel, because those categories carry a fraction of the weight in CPI's recipe.

The August 2025 data made this abstract principle concrete and expensive. Core CPI came in at 2.4% year-over-year — the slowest reading since the inflation surge began in 2021, and within striking distance of the Fed's 2% target. Headlines declared victory. But core PCE, the last reading available at that point, sat at 3.3% over the year. That is an 80-basis-point gap between the two indexes measuring the same economy in the same month. Historically, PCE runs 30 to 40 basis points below CPI, because its design tends to produce friendlier readings. The relationship had inverted. PIMCO characterized the reversal as one of the largest since 1985. The composition effect, specifically the shelter weight differential, was the primary explanation.

"The CPI number is not telling you inflation is beaten. It's telling you one enormous component is cooling and it happens to own a third of the index."

Shelter had been the one genuinely disinflationary force in the economy — the 2021 and 2022 rent spike was slowly rolling out of the annual comparison data. Because shelter is 34% of CPI, that cooling produced a massive and visible tailwind in the CPI headline. Because shelter is only 16% of PCE, the same cooling produced almost no relief in the index the Fed actually watches. Meanwhile, PCE carries heavier exposure to healthcare spending paid by employers and government — which CPI ignores entirely because CPI only counts what comes directly out of your pocket. PCE also captures portfolio management fees, which rise mechanically when asset prices rise, creating a feedback loop where a rallying stock market literally feeds the inflation gauge that could justify the next rate hike.

Traders use the composition effect as a signal-quality filter. When CPI and PCE diverge significantly, sophisticated market participants look at the source of the CPI move before drawing any conclusions about Fed behavior. In August 2025, the bond market's reaction within minutes of the CPI release told the story: instead of rallying on a soft inflation print, the 10-year Treasury yield climbed toward 4.95% and traders priced an 87% probability of a rate hike at the September 16th FOMC meeting. Bond traders were not looking at the CPI headline; they were mentally adjusting for the basket weight differential, recognizing that the shelter-driven CPI relief had minimal read-through to PCE, which is what actually drives Fed policy. The divergence between the bond market's response and the media's celebration was the composition effect in real time.

The most common misconception is treating CPI and PCE as two names for the same thing. They are not. Beyond the basket weight differences, PCE is chain-weighted, meaning it rebuilds its basket periodically to account for consumer substitution — when beef gets expensive, consumers buy chicken, and PCE reflects that behavioral shift. CPI holds its basket more rigidly. Chain-weighting is precisely why PCE is supposed to print lower than CPI almost all the time: it adjusts for the fact that consumers route around expensive categories. When the flexible index runs hotter than the rigid one, as happened in 2025, it signals that the pressure is broad-based and not confined to categories consumers can dodge. That is a qualitatively more dangerous inflation environment than a simple headline comparison would suggest.

The composition effect can also produce misleading reversals. In March 2017, wireless telephone services fell 7% in a single month — the largest one-month decline ever recorded in that category — and dragged core CPI to negative 0.1% for the whole month. Analysts spent weeks arguing inflation had died. The Fed looked straight through it and kept hiking. In August 2025, the same category moved in the opposite direction: wireless services jumped 5.9% in a single month, the largest one-month increase ever recorded, dragging core CPI upward by a tenth of a percentage point. Anyone building a Fed call on that wireless print — in either direction — was building on a foundation that had nothing to do with broad price pressure. Idiosyncratic category moves are the primary gotcha of index-level inflation analysis.

The composition effect connects directly to the concept of monetary policy transmission and the mortgage-shelter feedback loop. Higher Fed rates push up the 10-year Treasury yield, which sets 30-year mortgage rates. Higher mortgage rates freeze housing affordability, reduce existing home sales, and eventually put a floor under the shelter component of CPI that has been doing the disinflationary work. In other words, the very mechanism by which the Fed fights inflation — rate hikes — actively undermines the one category that had been pulling the CPI headline down. The shelter weight differential means CPI registers that cooling loudly while PCE barely notices; but as rates rise and housing freezes, even that CPI tailwind eventually reverses. Understanding the composition effect is not just about reading the current print correctly — it is about tracing the second-order consequences of policy into the index mechanics that will shape the next print.
