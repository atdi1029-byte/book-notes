Fixed vs. Variable Costs (Operating Leverage in Cost Cutting)

Every business has costs, but they don't all behave the same way. A fixed cost is an expense that stays roughly the same no matter how much the company sells in the short run: rent on a headquarters, the lease on a factory, insurance premiums, the interest owed on a bond. A variable cost rises and falls with activity: raw materials, shipping, sales commissions, hourly labor, credit card processing fees. The distinction is about flexibility. A variable cost is one management can shrink quickly when sales drop, while a fixed cost arrives on schedule regardless. Most real costs sit on a spectrum, and the dividing line is time. Over ten years almost everything can be changed; over ten weeks, very little can.

This matters because the cost structure of a company determines how violently its profits react to bad news. Revenue is only half the story; what survives after costs is what pays shareholders, funds dividends, services debt, and keeps people employed. When a firm's costs are mostly fixed, a small drop in sales can erase a large share of profit, because nothing automatically shrinks to match. That is why economists watch cost structure when judging how an economy will behave in a downturn. It affects whether companies absorb a shock or pass it to workers, shareholders, and lenders, and it explains why earnings swing so much more than sales do in recessions and recoveries.

Here is the mechanism step by step. Revenue comes in. Variable costs are subtracted, leaving the contribution margin, the money each sale leaves behind to cover everything fixed. Fixed costs come out next, and what remains is operating profit. Because fixed costs don't move, every extra dollar of sales drops mostly to profit, and every lost dollar comes mostly out of profit. This amplification is called operating leverage. A company with $100 million in sales, $60 million in variable costs and $30 million in fixed costs earns $10 million. If sales fall 10%, variable costs fall to $54 million, contribution drops to $36 million, and profit falls to $6 million: a 10% sales decline becomes a 40% profit decline.

Now add the twist the video emphasizes: when a new fixed cost lands on the income statement, management must find the money somewhere. Interest on debt is a textbook fixed cost; it is a contractual obligation, owed whether the quarter is good or bad. The presenter's math is the clean illustration. A company carrying $500 million of debt at 2.5% pays $12.5 million a year; refinanced at 8.5%, it pays $42.5 million, a $30 million increase. Sales haven't changed, but a large fixed cost just rose. Since the lender can't be negotiated with, the company looks at what can move, and that means the variable and discretionary lines on the budget.

"I tell you that the employees are variable costs and they're often the easiest variable cost to control... You can't just shut down a factory or sell a factory and assets like that, but unfortunately, you can very easily print out pink slips."

That quote captures the logic of cost cutting in the order of how fast each lever can be pulled. A factory can't be closed in a week, a lease can't be broken without penalties, and debt can't be unilaterally reduced. Payroll, though, is treated as flexible because it's the largest controllable line and it responds quickly. The presenter lists the sequence he expects: requisition (hiring) freezes first, then canceled bonuses, then trimmed benefits such as 401(k) matches, then outright layoffs. Notice this is an escalation ladder from least to most painful. Each step is easier to reverse than the next, which is why companies tend to start at the top of the ladder and descend only as the squeeze demands.

For investors, the practical use is reading a company's flexibility before trouble arrives. Compare fixed obligations to the cash that covers them. The video's screen is the interest coverage ratio (operating earnings divided by interest expense), and its rule of thumb is to be wary below three to one. Then look at the debt maturity schedule in the 10-K to see when cheap debt resets. A company with high operating leverage and a wall of maturities in 2026–2027 is a candidate for cuts: hiring freezes, bonus cancellations, dividend reductions. For workers, the same logic is a warning sign; if your employer's interest bill is about to triple, the budget conversation will include headcount.

Now the gotchas. First, calling employees a "variable cost" is a convenient simplification, not an accounting fact. In the financial statements, salaried payroll is usually treated as a fixed or semi-fixed expense, because you owe wages until you actually lay someone off, and severance makes the first cut cost money. Second, cutting people is not free: lost knowledge, weaker sales, rehiring costs, and lower morale can reduce revenue later, so the savings are partly illusory. Third, not every company is equally exposed. Firms with large cash piles or fixed-rate debt maturing years away can wait. And a cost cut following a rate rise is a plausible pattern, not guaranteed causation; layoffs have many causes, and this video's timeline is a forecast, not a measured result.

What happens when the strategy fails? Cost cutting has a floor. Once the easy discretionary spending is gone, deeper cuts hit the muscle of the business: sales staff, engineers, maintenance, customer service. Revenue then falls, which worsens the very ratio management was trying to fix. This is the death spiral, where cuts shrink the earnings base needed to cover interest. If coverage falls below the floors written into loan covenants, lenders can declare default, demand restructuring, or force a Chapter 11 filing, as the transcript describes. High operating leverage works in both directions: it creates spectacular profits in good times and ruinous losses in bad ones, and the debt on top is a second layer of leverage compounding the first.

This concept connects to several others in the guide. Financial leverage (debt) and operating leverage (fixed costs) multiply each other, a combination analysts call degree of total leverage. Interest coverage and debt-to-EBITDA measure how much cushion the fixed financing cost has. The refinancing wall and maturity schedules determine when a fixed cost resets, while dividend policy explains why shareholders sit behind bondholders in the payment line, making dividends the other flexible line item. Finally, the video's "zombie company" idea is simply a firm whose fixed costs have grown larger than its operating earnings can carry, leaving no flexible expense left to cut.
