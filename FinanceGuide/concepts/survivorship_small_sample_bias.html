Small Sample Size and Averages Hiding Outliers

A small sample size means you are drawing a conclusion from very few observations. An average (the arithmetic mean) squeezes a list of results into one number, and that number can hide how different the individual results were. An outlier is an observation far from the rest. The two problems usually show up together. With only nine data points, one strange result can change the story, and the average blends it in so you never see it. The video's chart does this. It averaged nine NASDAQ corrections into a reassuring 21% one-year gain, and one of those rows was a 24% loss.

This matters because investors make real decisions from tidy summary statistics. "The market always comes back" sounds like a law, but it is only a description of a few past episodes. When someone hands you an average, the money question is what happened in the worst case and how often it happens. If you buy after a drop because the average says it works, you do not receive the average. You receive one specific outcome from the distribution. Ten thousand dollars at +21% becomes $12,100. The same money at -24% becomes $7,600. No investor ever holds a $10,600 "average" outcome. People get hurt when they confuse the summary with the experience.

Mechanically, the mean adds every result and divides by the count, so every row counts equally and none is flagged. With only nine rows, each one carries about 11% of the weight. Two things go wrong. First, the mean can sit far from any typical result, and it says nothing about dispersion, the spread of outcomes. Second, with so few observations you cannot tell a real pattern from luck. A coin that lands heads six times out of nine tells you nothing about the coin. The usual fix is to look at the full list, the median, the worst result, and the reason each result happened. If you cannot explain the cause behind the outlier, you do not understand the average either.

Here is the video's example. A published table listed every time the NASDAQ first closed in correction territory (down 10% from a record) over about a decade, with the return over the next 12 months. The rows included 15% for October 2018, 54% for February 2020, 25% for August 2024 and 24% for March 2025. Averaged together, they gave 21%. The presenter found two problems. The article said there were ten corrections but the table had nine rows. And the March 2025 row showed 19%, but that correction began March 26 and twelve months had not yet passed, so it was not a 12-month return at all.

"The average is not the story, though. The exception really is."

The bigger problem was the single red row. The NASDAQ confirmed a correction on January 19, 2022, and twelve months later it was down another 24% from that day's close. That is a loss measured from the day investors were told to "buy the dip," not from the peak. You can estimate how much it pulled the average. If nine rows average 21%, they sum to 189 points. Remove the -24 and the other eight must sum to 213, an average near 26.6%. The outlier cost about 5.6 points of average, but the average still tells you nothing about the 50-point gap between the best and worst years. Also, seven completed rows rose, one fell, and one was unfinished. That is a thin record for any "always."

Careful traders use the average only as a starting point, then ask a better question: what separated the winners from the loser? The video does this. It does not stop at "2022 was bad" but looks for the variable that differs. In 2022 the Fed's target rate began near zero and the Fed raised it seven times, a path the Fed itself had not predicted. In 2018 the NASDAQ also fell hard, but the Fed paused in January 2019 and was cutting by July. In the other rows the Fed was cutting, at zero, or about to stop. The signal is the regime, not the average. The presenter watched the CPI report and the December 9 projections to see which row today most resembles.

There are several gotchas. First, an outlier is not necessarily noise. Sometimes it is the one case that reveals the mechanism, which is why the presenter said the exception matters more. Second, a sample of one bad year does not prove a rule either. The video itself admits "one bad year in a table is a sample of just one," and the 2018 row shows that rate hikes alone did not stop a recovery. Third, do not swing from "the average is certain" to "the outlier is certain." Both treat thin data as proof. Fourth, watch how the sample was assembled: a different start date, a different correction definition, or a missing tenth event can change everything. And a still-incomplete row should not be counted as a finished result.

The approach breaks when you treat history as a guarantee. Past corrections happened under specific conditions, and if today's conditions differ, the average may not apply. The danger is asymmetric, too. A 24% loss needs a gain of about 31.6% to get back to even, because you are recovering from a smaller base. A concentrated investor, or a homeowner already paying 7.18% on a mortgage, cannot always wait out a bad row. And because the data set is tiny, a single new episode could shift the next published average a lot. The failure is not that averages are wrong. It is that they leave out the tail, and the tail is where the damage happens.

This idea connects to several others. Survivorship bias appears when a table includes only episodes that "worked." Regime dependence means results depend on the surrounding conditions, here the Fed's direction. Discounting explains the mechanism: tech stocks are valued on distant profits, so a higher discount rate cuts their value most. Base rates help too: an average gives a prior, which new evidence like CPI should update. Risk management is the practical response. Size your position so you can survive the worst row, not just collect the average. In conversation, the key point is that the average tells you what usually happened, but the exception tells you how you could lose.
