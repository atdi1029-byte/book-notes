Advance-Decline Ratio

The advance-decline ratio (often shortened to A/D ratio) compares how many stocks rose in a trading session with how many fell. If 1,800 stocks on an exchange closed higher and 1,200 closed lower, the ratio is 1,800 divided by 1,200, or 1.5 to 1. It ignores how big each move was and how large each company is. Every stock gets one vote: up, down, or unchanged. That makes it a measure of market breadth, meaning how widely a move is shared across the market. A headline index like the S&P 500 tells you where the biggest companies went. The advance-decline ratio tells you whether the typical stock came along for the ride.

It matters because index returns can be misleading. The S&P 500 and Nasdaq are market-cap weighted, so a handful of giant companies can drag the whole index up or down while most other stocks do something else. Investors who only watch the index may think the market is healthy when only a few names are carrying it, or think it is weak when most stocks are fine. Breadth separates a rally that lifts the whole market from one that lifts a narrow group. That affects real decisions: whether to trust a rally, whether a pullback is a broad liquidation or a rotation, and how much risk to take while the headlines look strong.

Mechanically, it is simple. After each session, the exchange counts the issues that closed above the prior day's close (advancers), those that closed below (decliners), and those unchanged. The ratio is advancers divided by decliners. A reading of 1.0 means an even split, above 1.0 means more stocks rose than fell, and below 1.0 means the reverse. A closely related figure is the advance-decline line, a running total. Each day you take advancers minus decliners (the net advances) and add it to the previous total. Plot that cumulative number over time and you get a line showing whether participation is building or eroding. The daily ratio is a snapshot. The A/D line is the trend.

The video gives a clean example. On Wednesday, November 11, 2026, the S&P 500 rose 1.31% to 7619 and the Nasdaq gained 1.6%, powered by a beat-and-raise from CoreWeave that revived the AI trade. The host then added a caveat:

"Breadth was actually positive, but it wasn't super powerful... Advancers beat decliners 1.48 to 1 on the New York Stock Exchange."

Here is what that means. For roughly every 148 stocks that rose, 100 fell. That is a clear majority, about 60% of the stocks that moved. It is not the lopsided 3-to-1 or 4-to-1 reading you would expect on a truly broad, high-conviction rally. A 1.3% gain in the S&P is a strong day, yet breadth only modestly agreed. The equal-weight S&P, which gives every company the same weight, rose a smaller 0.8%. Both signals pointed the same way: the leaders did the heavy lifting.

Traders use the ratio in a few practical ways. First is confirmation. When an index rises on a strong ratio, often 2-to-1 or better, the move has broad support and is more likely to last. When an index surges but the ratio is mediocre, the rally is leaning on a few names. Second is divergence. If the index makes a new high while the A/D line fails to, it warns that fewer stocks are participating, which has preceded many market tops. Third is extreme readings. Very lopsided days, like 9-to-1 up or down, can mark capitulation or powerful thrusts. Traders also compare the ratio with equal-weight versus cap-weight index performance to judge whether the move is narrow or broad.

There are several gotchas. The ratio counts stocks, not dollars, so a stock that gains one cent counts the same as one that jumps 20%. It also includes lots of low-impact securities. The NYSE tally has historically included preferred shares, closed-end funds, and other instruments that behave differently from ordinary common stocks, which can distort the signal. Different exchanges also tell different stories. The NYSE is heavy with large, older companies, while the Nasdaq has many small and speculative issues, so a 1.48 on one is not directly comparable to a reading on the other. And a single day is noisy. A 1.48 reading means little alone. What matters is the pattern across days and weeks.

The ratio can fail or mislead in several ways. A market can have a decent ratio and still fall if the decliners are the giants. A day of 1.5-to-1 breadth on a 1% index decline means the large names dragged down a market that was otherwise mixed. Divergences can also persist for months before anything happens, so a weak A/D line is a caution, not a timing tool. Breadth thrusts can be false signals too, especially in a rate-driven market where a bond-market move can reverse everything within hours. The video's own context shows this: the rally came with the bond market closed, and the host warned that the 10-year yield near 5% would get its vote the next day.

The advance-decline ratio connects to several other ideas. It pairs naturally with the comparison of equal-weight versus cap-weighted indexes: when the equal-weight S&P lags, as it did here, breadth is narrower than the headline suggests. It also helps tell a rotation from a liquidation. The host's checkpoint was that if the equal-weight index holds green while the Nasdaq bleeds, it is a rotation, not a liquidation, because money is moving between stocks instead of leaving the market. Related tools include new highs versus new lows, up-volume versus down-volume, and the percentage of stocks above their 50-day moving average. Taken together, these show whether the market is broad and healthy or narrow and fragile.
