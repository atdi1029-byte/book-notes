Price Elasticity of Demand

Price elasticity of demand measures how much the quantity of a good that people buy changes when its price changes. If a small price increase causes people to buy a lot less, demand is elastic — it bends easily. If price can rise a lot and people keep buying roughly the same amount, demand is inelastic — it doesn't bend. This isn't an abstract classroom term. It's the difference between a price spike that consumers absorb by changing their behavior and a price spike that consumers simply have to eat, no matter the cost. Every fuel, food, or good you can name sits somewhere on this spectrum, and where it sits determines who actually pays when supply gets tight.

This concept matters because it explains why the same kind of price shock can hit different corners of the economy in wildly different ways. A 74% year-over-year jump in diesel prices — which is exactly what happened, with the national average hitting a record $6.53 a gallon — does not behave like a 74% jump in the price of, say, a streaming subscription. Elasticity is the hidden variable that decides whether a price spike stays contained to one product or cascades through the entire supply chain into your grocery bill, your Amazon package, and your electricity rate. Without understanding elasticity, price moves in commodities look random. With it, they become predictable.

Mechanically, elasticity comes down to one question: does the buyer have options? When a good has close substitutes, or when consumption can be delayed or reduced without much cost, buyers respond to a price increase by switching away or cutting back — that's elastic demand, and it caps how high the price can realistically go before sellers start losing volume. When a good has no substitute and no ability to delay purchase, buyers are stuck. They pay whatever the price is, because the alternative — not getting the good at all — is worse than paying up. That's inelastic demand, and it means price can spike dramatically with almost no drop in the quantity purchased, because purchasing isn't really optional.

The video's central example lays this out cleanly with gasoline versus diesel. When gasoline gets expensive, a regular driver has real options: combine errands into one trip, skip a discretionary drive, carpool, or wait a few days for prices to ease. That flexibility is what makes gasoline demand relatively elastic. Diesel is the opposite case.

"A class A truck hauling frozen food cannot carpool. A locomotive can't skip the trip. A combine has a narrow window to get the corn out of the field."
There's no substitute fuel for a diesel engine, no way to delay a harvest window, and no way to reroute a freight schedule around a price chart. So when diesel hit $6.53 a gallon in September 2026 — up from about $3.75 a year earlier — the trucks kept running, the trains kept moving, and the combines kept harvesting. Nobody bought less diesel because it got expensive; they just paid more.

This is exactly why the video makes the point that

"diesel spike shows up in your grocery bill and a gasoline spike shows mostly up in your own wallet."
Because diesel demand is inelastic, truckers and freight operators can't absorb a fuel cost increase by simply using less fuel — they pass the added cost straight into the price of shipping, which flows into the price of everything shipped: groceries, retail goods, farm output. Gasoline, being elastic, works differently. When it spikes, some of the pain gets absorbed through reduced driving, so the cost doesn't transmit as cleanly into other prices — it mostly just squeezes the individual driver's budget. Same commodity family, same kind of shock, completely different economic consequence, purely because of elasticity.

Traders and analysts use elasticity as a forecasting tool, not just a description after the fact. If you know a good is inelastic, you know that price spikes in that good will pass through to downstream prices almost dollar-for-dollar, with a lag — which is exactly why the video flags farm diesel costs (Penn State's math showing corn and soybean fuel costs going from $9 to $18 per acre at $6 diesel) as a leading indicator for food inflation, not just an agricultural sector problem. Conversely, when you see an elastic good spike, you expect demand destruction to eventually cap the price — buyers will pull back, inventories will build, and the spike tends to self-correct faster. Watching which category a shock hits — elastic or inelastic — tells you whether to expect contained pain or contagion into other prices.

The biggest misconception is treating "expensive" and "inelastic" as the same thing, or assuming elasticity is a fixed, permanent property of a good. Elasticity is about substitutability and urgency, not price level — a good can be expensive and still elastic if buyers have workarounds, and cheap goods can be inelastic if there's no substitute. Elasticity also isn't binary or eternal; it shifts with circumstances. Diesel demand is inelastic in the short run because trucks and combines can't change overnight, but over a longer horizon, elasticity can creep in — companies can invest in rail instead of trucking, farms can adjust equipment, and behavior can adapt in ways that weren't possible during a single harvest season. Short-run and long-run elasticity for the same good can look very different.

Elasticity breaks down as a clean predictive tool when a shock is severe enough, or persistent enough, to force adaptation even in normally inelastic markets — at some price, even a trucking company will renegotiate routes, consolidate loads, or pass costs downstream in ways that ripple back and eventually reduce total shipping demand. It also gets distorted by policy interference, which is precisely the risk raised around the diesel export ban proposals discussed in the video. An export ban wouldn't change diesel's inelasticity — trucks would still need fuel — but it could scramble supply geographically (the Gulf Coast can't easily reroute diesel to New England because "the pipelines don't run in that direction"), creating regional price distortions layered on top of the underlying inelastic demand curve. This connects directly to the crack spread concept: elasticity tells you demand won't fall when refining bottlenecks push diesel prices up, which is exactly why the crack spread — the refiner's margin — can balloon to four times normal (over $111 a barrel versus a normal $20-30) without collapsing diesel purchases. Inelastic demand is what lets that margin expansion actually stick.
