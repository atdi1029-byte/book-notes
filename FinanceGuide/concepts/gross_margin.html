Gross Margin

Gross margin is the percentage of revenue a company keeps after subtracting the direct costs of producing whatever it sells. Those direct costs — called cost of goods sold (COGS) or cost of revenue — include raw materials, manufacturing labor, component parts, and any expense that goes directly into making the product or delivering the service. Everything else — rent, executive salaries, marketing, R&D, taxes — comes later. Gross margin strips all of that out and answers one simple question: for every dollar of revenue that comes in the front door, how many cents are left before you start paying for anything else? It is the most fundamental measure of a business's core economics.

The formula is straightforward: take total revenue, subtract cost of goods sold, and you have gross profit. Divide that gross profit by total revenue and multiply by 100, and you have the gross margin percentage. A company that earns $100 million in revenue and spends $27 million making its products has a gross profit of $73 million and a gross margin of 73%. That remaining 73 cents per dollar is what the company has available to pay for sales teams, research labs, office buildings, debt interest, and ultimately profit. If gross margin is too thin, none of those downstream expenses can be covered sustainably — the whole financial structure sits on a cracked foundation.

Gross margin matters to investors because it reveals pricing power — the ability to charge more than it costs you to produce. A company with high gross margins can absorb unexpected costs, fund its own growth without constantly raising capital, and reward shareholders. A company with razor-thin margins has almost no buffer. Any cost spike — rising component prices, supply chain disruption, wage inflation — hits the bottom line immediately and hard. This is why gross margin is often the first thing a sophisticated analyst examines when sizing up a business. Revenue growth is exciting, but revenue growth into deteriorating gross margins is a trap. You can be growing fast and quietly going broke at the same time.

The mechanism works like a cascade. Every additional dollar of revenue either expands or contracts gross margin depending on whether costs rise proportionally. When a company achieves operating leverage, its costs of production grow slower than its revenue — so margins expand as scale increases. This is the ideal. Chip designers like Broadcom, for instance, can design a chip once and manufacture it at scale, so each additional unit sold costs less in relative terms, pushing margins higher. The opposite — margin compression — happens when input costs rise faster than prices, when product mix shifts toward lower-margin offerings, or when heavy discounting is needed to maintain volume. Margin compression is the quiet killer that can make a great-looking revenue number into a disappointing story.

The Broadcom example from this week makes the concept vivid. The company reported third-quarter fiscal 2026 revenue of $29.6 billion — up 86% year-over-year. Its AI semiconductor revenue grew 221%. By almost any measure, those are extraordinary numbers. Yet the stock fell 6% the following day. The reason was fourth-quarter gross margin guidance of 73% against Wall Street's expectation of 78%. That five-percentage-point gap matters enormously at Broadcom's scale. On $34.8 billion of guided revenue, the difference between 73% and 78% gross margin is roughly $1.7 billion in gross profit that the market had priced in and suddenly discovered wasn't coming. The market wasn't selling the growth — it was repricing what that growth was actually worth.

"Growing 86% into a shrinking margin is a different investment case than growing 86% into an expanding one."

That line from the video captures precisely how traders and investors think about gross margin in practice. When a company is growing revenue while expanding margins, each new dollar of sales is worth more than the last — the business is becoming more efficient, more profitable, more valuable per share. Analysts build discounted cash flow (DCF) models that project future profits; expanding margins make those projections accelerate. Shrinking margins do the reverse. Investors use gross margin trends — not just the current level — as a forward indicator of whether a business's competitive position is strengthening or eroding. A single quarter of compression can be noise. A guided step-down signals something structural.

A common misconception is that high revenue growth automatically means a healthy business. The Broadcom situation is a near-perfect counterexample. The company grew AI revenue 221% year-over-year, but the market sold it because the quality of that growth was in question. This connects to the broader concept of unit economics: are you making more or less money on each incremental sale? Another misconception is confusing gross margin with net margin — the profit left after all expenses including R&D, taxes, and interest. A company can have 73% gross margin and still lose money if it is spending aggressively on sales and engineering. Gross margin is necessary but not sufficient. It is the ceiling, not the floor.

Gross margin can be manipulated or misread in ways that burn investors. Companies sometimes shift costs between COGS and operating expenses, which artificially inflates or deflates reported gross margin without changing underlying economics. Product mix shifts are another hidden risk: a company might report a blended gross margin that looks stable while its highest-margin products are losing share to lower-margin ones — a slow deterioration invisible in the headline number. Broadcom's margin compression may partly reflect the reality that hyperscaler customers — massive buyers like Google, Meta, and Microsoft — have enormous negotiating leverage and can extract price concessions as the market matures. Rapid growth sometimes comes with the hidden cost of serving powerful customers at thinner economics.

Gross margin connects tightly to several adjacent concepts worth understanding together. Competitive moat is one: businesses with durable pricing power — think software, specialty semiconductors, or branded consumer goods — tend to sustain high gross margins decade after decade. The FICO story from this same week illustrates the flip side: when a competitor or regulator undermines your pricing power (Vantage Score charging $0.99 versus FICO's $10+), gross margin collapses overnight. Inflation and input costs are another link — as the video noted, manufacturing prices remained elevated, and energy costs were surging. Those cost pressures flow directly into COGS. Finally, gross margin feeds into operating margin and ultimately free cash flow, the lifeblood of equity valuation. Understanding gross margin is the entry point to understanding how a business actually generates wealth.
