Repo Haircut

A repo haircut is the gap between what a piece of collateral is worth and how much cash a lender will hand over against it. A repo (repurchase agreement) is a short-term loan secured by an asset. You give a lender a Treasury bond, receive cash, and promise to buy the bond back tomorrow at a slightly higher price. The difference is the interest. If the lender gives you only $98 against $100 of bonds, the haircut is 2%. That $2 is your skin in the game. The word comes from the idea that the lender "trims" the collateral's value before lending against it. A 0% haircut means the lender gives you the full market value, with no trim at all.

The haircut matters because it sets the maximum leverage in the financial system. Leverage is the inverse of the haircut. A 2% haircut means you must fund 2% of each position yourself, so you can hold $50 of bonds for every $1 of your own money. A 1% haircut allows 100 to 1. A 0% haircut, in theory, allows unlimited leverage, because you need no equity at all to borrow against the bond. This one number decides how big a trade can get, how fragile it is, and how much damage can spread if it unwinds. It is the dial that quietly controls how much borrowed money sits under the Treasury market.

Here is the mechanism step by step. A hedge fund buys $100 million of Treasury bonds with its own cash. It immediately hands those bonds to a lender, such as a bank or money fund, as collateral in a repo. With a 2% haircut it receives $98 million overnight. It uses that cash to buy another $98 million of bonds, repos those out too, and repeats. Each round the position grows, and the equity stays the same. The fund pays the repo rate on the borrowing, and it earns the bonds' yield or a convergence profit. Every night the loan is rolled over, so the lender can reprice or refuse at any time.

The video grounds this in a New York Fed finding: 75% of Treasury repo trades in the bilateral market carry a 0% haircut. In the presenter's words:

"Seventy-five percent of Treasury repo trades in the bilateral market carry a 0% haircut. No collateral cushion at all is what that really means."

"Bilateral" means a direct deal between two parties, such as a dealer and a hedge fund, with no clearinghouse in the middle. Those deals are private, so the terms are set by negotiation and relationships, not by a central rulebook. The video then uses the Fed's arithmetic: roughly $553 billion in positions against about $9.8 billion of actual hard equity, which works out to leverage of about 56 to 1 on this financing. That ratio is just assets divided by equity.

Why would hedge funds want this? The video explains the basis trade. Pension and mutual funds buy Treasury futures instead of physical bonds, which pushes futures to a small premium over cash bonds. A hedge fund sells the expensive future, buys the cheaper physical bond, and waits for the two to converge at expiration. The spread averages only about five basis points, or 5/100ths of 1%. Earning 0.05% is worthless on its own. Make the trade 50 times bigger with repo borrowing and it becomes a meaningful return. A near-zero haircut is therefore what makes the whole strategy possible. The thin spread only pays if financing is cheap and the cushion is thin.

A common misconception is that "56 to 1" describes the whole hedge fund. The video corrects this directly. The headline leverage figure applies to the repo financing of one specific trade, not the entire fund. Fund-level leverage for large traders runs closer to 17 to 1, or roughly 20 to 1. A second nuance is that a 0% haircut does not mean the loan is unsecured or free of risk control. Lenders still hold the bond, still mark it to market daily, and can demand more collateral if prices fall. They also tend to extend zero haircuts to counterparties they trust, and often because the trade is hedged: the fund is short futures against the bonds it holds, so price swings largely cancel out.

Haircuts are not fixed forever, and this is where things can break. If bond volatility jumps, a lender may raise the haircut from 0% to 2% or 3%. On a position with $553 billion of assets, even a 1% increase means about $5.5 billion in new equity must appear overnight, which is more than half of the entire $9.8 billion cushion. Funds that can't find the cash must sell bonds, which pushes prices down, which raises volatility, which prompts more haircut increases. This is the spiral of March 2020, when basis trades unwound violently and the Fed had to step in. The video's claim that the same fragility exists today is the core of the "margin call" fear.

The video, though, argues that the fear is overblown and uses market readings to show it. On quarter-end, the Fed's standing repo facility took in exactly zero. SOFR, the benchmark overnight rate, fell two basis points to 3.88%, flat with Fed funds. The reverse repo facility rose to $11.4 billion. That pattern means cash is plentiful, not scarce. Haircuts spike when collateral is doubted or cash disappears, and neither signal was flashing. Traders watch exactly these dials: SOFR against the Fed's range, usage of the standing repo facility, and bond volatility such as the MOVE index. Rising haircuts are a lagging confirmation. These signals are the early warning.

The haircut connects to several other ideas. It is the same logic as margin requirements in stock trading, where a 50% initial margin equals a 2 to 1 limit. It links to repo rates and SOFR, since price and quantity of funding move together. It ties to the basis trade and swap spreads, because the video notes that leverage "never disappears, it only mutates": when the basis trade shrank, funds rotated into swap spread trades, which grew from roughly $100 billion to $300 billion with little public reporting. Finally, it connects to dealer balance sheets and the supplementary leverage ratio, which determine how much repo financing banks can afford to provide at all.
