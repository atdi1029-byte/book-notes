Credit Card Charge-Offs

A credit card charge-off is the moment a lender gives up on collecting a card balance on its normal schedule and writes it off as a loss. It usually happens when a borrower has missed payments for about 180 days, or roughly six consecutive billing cycles. The charge-off is an accounting event, not forgiveness. The borrower still owes the money, and the debt is often sold or handed to a collector. On the bank's books, though, the balance stops counting as an asset expected to be repaid and becomes a realized loss. Banks report this as a net charge-off rate: charge-offs minus later recoveries, divided by average card balances, usually annualized. If a bank has $100 billion in card loans and writes off $4 billion net over a year, its rate is 4%.

Charge-offs matter because credit cards are the most sensitive consumer loan on a bank's books. They are unsecured, so there is no house or car to seize. They are also the first bill a stressed household tends to let slide, since the card is flexible and nobody repossesses a phone plan's worth of groceries. When a household's income gets squeezed, the card balance absorbs the shock first. That makes card losses a real-time readout of household financial health. Because banks lend to millions of people at once, their aggregate numbers show a pattern across the economy long before any single government survey could. And because card issuers are large, publicly traded companies, the data arrives every quarter, with executives on a call to explain it.

The mechanism runs on a timeline. A borrower misses a minimum payment and the account becomes 30 days past due, which is called delinquency. The bank sends reminders, adds late fees, and may raise the interest rate. At 60 and 90 days, the account moves into more serious delinquency buckets and collections calls intensify. By about 180 days, regulatory guidance generally requires the bank to charge the balance off. The bank then books a provision for credit losses, which is money set aside from earnings in anticipation of losses, and removes the balance from its loans. Any later collections or debt-sale proceeds are counted as recoveries. This lag is the key point: a charge-off reflects trouble that began about six months earlier.

Investors use charge-offs in several practical ways. First, they compare the rate to the prior quarter and to the prior year. Direction and speed matter more than the level, because card charge-offs normally run in the mid-single digits and spike during recessions, reaching roughly 10% in 2009-2010 before collapsing in 2021 when stimulus cash flooded households. Second, they read the reserve build: when a bank raises its allowance for future losses, management is telling you it expects worse. Third, they look at segmentation, meaning prime versus subprime borrowers, by credit score band or income. A rise among high-score or high-income cardholders is far more meaningful than a rise among subprime borrowers, who are always the weakest link. The video's focus on "high-tier" borrowers rests on this logic.

There are several gotchas. Charge-offs lag: they confirm stress that began roughly six months before, so they are an early signal relative to government data, but not a leading indicator of the first missed paycheck. Earlier signals include early-stage delinquencies (30 days past due) and payment-rate trends. Second, charge-offs can be distorted by the bank's own choices: tighter underwriting a year earlier lowers today's losses, and fast balance growth can mask the rate because the denominator rises. Third, a charge-off is not a bankruptcy and not debt forgiveness, and it stays on a credit report for about seven years. Finally, a rising rate does not by itself mean white-collar stress; it can come from subprime borrowers, a particular vintage of loans, or a single issuer's strategy. Compare several banks before drawing a conclusion.

When the system breaks, the losses compound. If charge-offs rise sharply, banks raise their loss reserves, which cuts earnings directly. They respond by lowering credit limits, tightening approvals, and closing dormant accounts, which cuts off the borrowing households were using as a shock absorber. That forces more spending cuts, which hurts retailers, airlines, and travel companies, which in turn cut payrolls and create more delinquencies. This feedback loop is how a quiet squeeze can turn into a visible downturn. Investors who hold bank stocks, consumer lenders, or asset-backed securities built from card receivables can suffer from both the losses and the repricing of credit. Card ABS investors, for example, watch charge-off and payment-rate triggers that can shut off cash flow to lower-rated tranches.

Charge-offs connect to several other ideas in this guide. The video's core theme, that the macro averages hide concentrated damage, applies here too: national delinquency data can look calm while a specific income group is under pressure. Charge-offs link to credit spreads, since worsening consumer credit tends to widen the yield gap between risky and safe bonds. They link to the labor data discussed in the video, because job losses and stagnant wages are the cause and charge-offs are the delayed effect. They also connect to the emergency-fund advice: a six-month cash reserve is precisely what keeps a household from becoming one of the accounts that ends up charged off. For an investor, charge-offs turn a vague story about squeezed professionals into a number you can track every quarter.
