EBITDA Leverage Multiple (Debt/EBITDA)

The EBITDA leverage multiple, expressed as Debt/EBITDA, is one of the most important metrics in corporate finance for measuring how indebted a company is relative to its earning power. EBITDA stands for Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization — a rough proxy for operating cash flow that strips out accounting noise and financing costs. When you divide a company's total debt by its EBITDA, you get a number that answers a fundamental question: how many years of current earnings would it take to pay off all the debt? A leverage multiple of 3x means it would take three years of full EBITDA to retire the debt. At 7x, you're looking at seven years — and that's assuming nothing goes wrong.

This metric matters because it sits at the center of every major lending, buyout, and credit-risk decision in modern finance. Banks, private credit funds, and bond investors all use Debt/EBITDA to decide whether a company can safely carry its debt load. Lenders use it to set covenants — contractual tripwires that trigger consequences if leverage climbs too high. Private equity firms use it to structure leveraged buyouts, loading companies with debt while targeting a multiple they believe the business can sustain. Rating agencies use it to assign credit ratings that determine borrowing costs. When leverage multiples rise across an industry, it's a systemic warning sign — the entire sector is becoming more fragile, more sensitive to any earnings decline or interest rate increase.

Mechanically, the ratio works like a seesaw. The numerator is total debt: bonds outstanding, term loans, revolving credit drawn, and any accrued but unpaid interest (more on that last part shortly). The denominator is trailing twelve-month EBITDA, usually the most recently reported four quarters. If a company has $700 million in debt and generates $100 million in EBITDA, its leverage is 7x. Now suppose EBITDA drops to $80 million — maybe customers cancel contracts. Leverage instantly jumps to 8.75x without the company borrowing a single additional dollar. The ratio is dynamic and adversarial: when business deteriorates, both sides of the fraction move in the wrong direction simultaneously, compressing the denominator while the numerator may be growing through accrued interest.

The video provides a painfully concrete example. A middle-market software company — the kind that built its business on per-seat subscription revenue — borrowed from a private credit fund at a leverage multiple of 7x EBITDA with a floating interest rate of 12%. At origination, 7x might have seemed aggressive but manageable given the predictable, annuity-like nature of software subscription revenue. Then AI disruption hit. Enterprise customers realized that five AI agents could replace fifty human seats, so they began canceling licenses in bulk. Revenue per customer collapsed. EBITDA shrank. The 7x leverage multiple, calculated on yesterday's earnings, now reflects a business that is fundamentally weaker — meaning the true forward-looking leverage is far worse than the reported number.

Traders and credit investors watch Debt/EBITDA as a refinancing viability signal. Lenders in the leveraged loan market typically apply informal but widely observed thresholds: deals above 6x are considered aggressive, and anything approaching or exceeding 7x starts triggering serious credit committee scrutiny. When a company needs to refinance — roll over maturing debt into new loans — lenders will underwrite the new deal based on current EBITDA. If earnings have fallen, the company can support less debt at any given multiple. This means a company that borrowed at 7x on $100 million EBITDA (supporting $700 million in debt) but now earns only $60 million cannot refinance the same $700 million — a lender willing to go 6x would only extend $360 million. The company faces a debt maturity wall it cannot climb over.

One of the most important nuances is the interaction between leverage and PIK (Payment in Kind) financing. In a standard loan, the borrower pays cash interest. In a PIK arrangement, unpaid interest is simply added to the loan balance. This means the numerator of the Debt/EBITDA fraction grows automatically every quarter — without any new borrowing decision, without any action by the company. A borrower already at 7x who is PIK-ing their 12% interest sees their debt balance grow by roughly 12% annually. If EBITDA is flat or falling, leverage can spiral from 7x to 8x to 9x within two or three years purely through interest accrual. PIK is often portrayed as financial flexibility; in distressed situations, it is closer to a leverage time bomb with a preset fuse.

The concept breaks down catastrophically when the EBITDA denominator is structurally impaired rather than cyclically depressed. This is the crucial distinction the video identifies. A cyclical EBITDA decline — say, a recession softening demand — tends to recover. Lenders can extend and pretend, waiting for business to bounce back. But a structural impairment, where the underlying business model is made obsolete by technology disruption, means EBITDA will not recover to the levels that originally justified the leverage. The private credit funds that lent against per-seat software models at 7x assumed a seat was a permanent unit of revenue. AI has made that assumption permanently wrong for a significant share of the borrower universe, which is why valuation firms marked down 81% of software loan portfolios — not because of missed payments, but because the future EBITDA that backs the debt has been structurally revised downward.

Debt/EBITDA connects directly to several adjacent concepts that complete the picture. Interest coverage ratio (EBITDA divided by interest expense) measures whether current earnings can even service the debt cost — a 7x leveraged company paying 12% floating rates has $84 million in annual interest on $700 million of debt, which consumes 84% of a $100 million EBITDA, leaving almost nothing for capex, taxes, or principal repayment. Loan-to-value ratios track collateral coverage. Credit spreads price the probability of default implied by these metrics. When all three deteriorate simultaneously — leverage rising, coverage falling, collateral values declining — the credit market faces what the Bank for International Settlements documented: lenders writing down performing loans not because of missed payments, but because the forward math simply no longer works. That is the leverage multiple doing its job as a diagnostic tool, telling anyone willing to read it that the debt cannot be repaid under realistic assumptions.
