Additional Tier 1 (AT1) Contingent Convertible Bonds

An Additional Tier 1 (AT1) bond, usually called a CoCo (contingent convertible), is a piece of debt issued by a bank that behaves like debt when times are good and like equity when times are bad. It pays a fixed coupon, often 7-9%, and it has no real maturity date. Most are "perpetual," though the bank typically has the option to redeem ("call") them after five years or so. The "contingent" part is the catch: if the bank's capital falls below a pre-set trigger, or a regulator decides the bank is failing, the bond can be converted into shares or written down to zero. You are lending money to a bank, but you are agreeing to be hurt before almost everyone else.

Regulators created these securities after the 2008 financial crisis for a plain reason: taxpayers should not have to rescue banks again. Under the Basel III rules, a bank must hold a cushion of capital against its loans. Common equity is the best cushion, but it is expensive for banks to raise. AT1s are a cheaper substitute that still counts as regulatory capital, because they can absorb losses without the bank going bankrupt. So AT1s matter in two ways: they are a major source of funding for European banks, and they are a barometer of how nervous investors feel about the financial system. The video described them as the airbags of the bank's capital structure.

"Think of AT1s as preferred shares... like they are the airbags in your car. They sit directly between senior bondholders and common equity."

To see the mechanics, picture the stack of claims on a bank, ordered by who gets paid first. At the top are depositors and senior bondholders. Below them sit subordinated (Tier 2) debt, then AT1s, and at the bottom is common equity, the shareholders. Losses hit from the bottom up. Shareholders absorb the first losses, then the AT1 holders, and so on upward. The video's point is that AT1s are the layer between the safe senior creditors and the owners. An airbag works because it inflates and takes the impact so the driver doesn't. The AT1 holder is the one who inflates, and the AT1 holder pays for that role.

The conversion trigger works like this. Each AT1 has a written threshold, commonly when the bank's Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) ratio drops below 5.125% or 7%, depending on the terms. The CET1 ratio compares a bank's highest-quality capital to its risk-weighted assets. If it falls below the trigger, the bond either converts into shares at a set price or is written down, in part or in full. There is also a softer second trigger: the issuer can skip coupon payments without defaulting, because AT1 coupons are discretionary. Regulators can force a skip if the bank lacks distributable profits. Skipping a coupon is not a default, which is why these bonds are riskier than they look.

The clearest example is Credit Suisse in March 2023. After a loss of confidence, Swiss authorities forced the bank into a takeover by UBS. As part of the deal, the Swiss regulator ordered roughly $17 billion of Credit Suisse AT1 bonds written down to exactly zero overnight. What shocked the market was the order of losses. Shareholders still received about 3 billion Swiss francs from UBS, so equity holders came out with something while the bondholders who were supposed to rank ahead of them got nothing. Credit Suisse's own AT1 terms allowed this, but investors had assumed that equity would be wiped out first. The video's summary was blunt: when a crisis hits, the airbag gets sacrificed.

Investors treat the AT1 market as a gauge of appetite for risk. The video tracked the Bloomberg global CoCo index, where spreads, meaning the extra yield above a safe benchmark, had fallen below 200 basis points for the first time ever. Wall Street called that "ridiculously tight," because investors were earning very little extra for a lot of risk. Then spreads jumped to 253 basis points in a week, and year-to-date returns fell from 3.6% in August to 1.6%. A wider spread means the price of the bond has dropped and investors want more compensation. Traders watch this as an early warning: when bank capital gets expensive, stress is usually building in the banking system.

The ripple effects reach beyond the people holding the bonds. The video noted that Bank of New York Mellon had to pay a spread 32.4 basis points wider than its post-crisis low to sell dollar preferred shares, which are the U.S. equivalent of AT1s. And Italy's Banco BPM, which tried to sell €400 million of AT1 notes, had seen about €1.5 billion of orders early in marketing, only to face tougher pricing as demand cooled. When a bank has to pay more for this capital, its cost of funding rises. That squeezes net interest margins, can slow lending to businesses, and may limit share buybacks and dividend increases. In the video's words, when the airbag gets expensive, the engine doesn't run as fast.

There are several common misunderstandings. First, a 7-9% yield is not a "high-quality bond" yield; it is payment for a risk that is closer to stock risk than bond risk. Second, the rank in the capital structure is not a guarantee. The Credit Suisse case showed that the contract terms and the regulator's powers can override the usual order. Third, the bond's "maturity" is a convention, not a promise: if the bank decides not to call the bond at the first date, you may be holding a perpetual security. Fourth, AT1s differ by country. European CoCos can be written down, while U.S. banks mostly issue fixed-to-reset preferred stock, which usually lacks automatic conversion triggers.

The failure modes are worth knowing before you buy. In a stressed bank, the sequence can be a skipped coupon, a collapse in price, and finally a write-down or conversion, and each step can happen faster than most investors expect. Prices of AT1s can also fall sharply when no individual bank is in trouble, as other fears spread across the sector, because the market is thin and many holders sell together. Those holders include funds that chased yield during years of tight spreads. Retail investors often own AT1s through bond funds or ETFs without realizing it, and some jurisdictions restrict direct sales to ordinary investors for exactly this reason.

AT1s connect to the video's wider story about the French-German bond dislocation. When the gap between French and German 10-year yields widened by 32 basis points in a week, it signaled doubt about a major European government's finances. Banks hold large amounts of their own government's debt, so sovereign stress feeds directly into bank stress, and bank stress hits the most junior capital first. That is why the CoCo index reacted at the same moment. The lesson for an investor is to read the AT1 market as a stress indicator. Rising spreads suggest that institutions are pulling back from risk, and a falling 10-year yield may reflect a flight to safety rather than a healthy economy.
