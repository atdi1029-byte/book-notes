IPO Book-Building, Oversubscription and Allocation

An initial public offering (IPO) is the first time a company sells its shares to the public. Nobody knows the right price in advance, because no market price exists yet. Book-building is how the banks find one. The underwriters, the investment banks running the deal, publish a price range and then collect orders from institutional investors, recording how many shares each wants and at what price. That list of orders is "the book." The Anthropic offering shows the idea: 825 million shares at $70 to $78 each. The range is a starting question to investors. The book, filled in over days of calls and meetings, is the answer that sets the real price.

This matters because the IPO is where a private company turns its story into cash, and the price decides how much it raises and who profits afterward. Price too low and the company leaves money with the buyers, who get an instant gain. Price too high and the stock falls, which angers investors and damages the company's name in the market. Book-building also reveals how much appetite the market has for a sector. A strong book for one large deal can open the window for other issuers. A weak one can shut it. Since index funds hold most large public companies, every big pricing also changes what ordinary savers are exposed to.

The mechanics run in a sequence. First the company files a prospectus and the banks set an initial range. Then comes the roadshow, a week or two of presentations to large investors. Investors submit indications of interest, and these are non-binding. Bankers watch the book grow and compare total demand to shares offered. That ratio is the oversubscription multiple. If the book is covered several times at the top of the range, the company can raise the range, raise the price, or both. On the final night the company and banks agree on one price. The syndicate desk then decides allocation, meaning who gets how many shares, and the stock begins trading the next morning.

Here are the numbers from the Anthropic deal. Reports put orders at roughly six or seven times the shares offered. That means about six or seven dollars of demand chased every dollar of stock. Bloomberg then reported that the company was guiding investors to a price at the top of the $70 to $78 range or above. At $78, the public offering alone is worth about $64 billion (825 million shares times $78), and the implied company value lands between roughly $916 billion and $1.02 trillion. This is textbook book-building working as designed: strong demand gives the issuer the leverage to push price up before a single share trades.

"Picture pricing night at the banks running a deal like this. The syndicate desk stays lit long after the rest of the floor goes dark. That is the desk that decides who gets shares and how many."

Allocation is where the real power sits, and it is not first-come, first-served. When a book is six times covered, five of every six dollars of demand get cut back or get nothing. The syndicate desk, working with the company, decides who is rewarded. Favored accounts tend to be long-term holders, funds that gave useful feedback during the roadshow, and clients who have steady business with the bank. Quick flippers get less. Investors know this, so many inflate their orders, asking for far more than they want in the hope of receiving a fraction. That makes the headline "6x oversubscribed" a noisy number. Real demand is somewhat smaller than the book suggests, though still strong.

Traders and investors read the book as a signal. A deal covered many times, guided to the top of the range or above, usually prices high and often trades up on day one, which is why individual investors chase "hot" IPOs. A deal that is barely covered, or has to cut its range, tells you the opposite. Professionals also watch the price revision trail: range raised, then priced above it, is the strongest sequence. Cautious investors note that the first-day jump goes to whoever gets an allocation, and most retail buyers do not. Buying at the opening trade, after the pop, means paying a price that the book has already largely absorbed.

There are several nuances here. First, oversubscription does not equal a good investment. It measures demand at the offer price, not value. Second, not every share goes through the book. The Anthropic filing describes a concurrent private placement: seven strategic partners, including Nvidia, Google, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC, agreed to buy $26 billion of stock at the IPO price, outside the public order process. Their allocation was written into the filing before pricing night. That placement is a fixed dollar amount, so a higher final price gives those partners fewer shares for the same money. The shares are non-voting class N stock and are not covered by the underwriters' lockup, though they are restricted securities.

Books can fail in several ways. If demand is thin, the underwriters can cut the price, shrink the deal, or pull it altogether. If the book is inflated by padded orders, the stock can price high and then slide below the offer price once the real buyers, who got only a fraction, stop buying. Banks sometimes defend the price by buying shares in the market. A subtler risk is demand that is not independent. The video notes that the 2000 filing for Divine Interventions had side buyers like Microsoft and Level 3, and part of one payment was a credit for services, not cash. The stock priced at $9 and was near $1.56 by year-end. When buyers are also the company's suppliers, a full book says less about outside conviction.

This concept connects to several others. Circular financing is the video's central worry: suppliers invest cash that returns to them as revenue, so a strong book may partly reflect interlocked interests. Lockups govern when insiders and early holders can sell, which shapes supply after the IPO. The underwriting spread is the fee the banks earn for the whole process. Valuation matters too: the $916 billion to $1.02 trillion range counts shares outstanding after the deal, and the larger $1.4 trillion figure includes options and unissued shares. Finally, the index-fund link: six of the seven buyers are in the S&P 500, so a retirement account can be on the buy side without anyone placing an order.
