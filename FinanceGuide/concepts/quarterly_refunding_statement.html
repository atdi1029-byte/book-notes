Quarterly Refunding Statement

The Quarterly Refunding Statement (QRS) is an official announcement published by the U.S. Department of the Treasury — typically released in February, May, August, and November — that lays out the government's borrowing plans for the coming quarter and beyond. It discloses the sizes of upcoming coupon auctions (the sales of Treasury notes and bonds that pay regular interest), offers forward guidance on whether those sizes will increase or decrease, and explains the Treasury's overall financing strategy. Think of it as the government's earnings guidance, but instead of projected profits, it projects how much debt Washington intends to sell and in what maturities. It is the single most important document for understanding the true supply picture in the Treasury market, and yet it is almost never discussed in mainstream financial media.

Why does this document matter beyond a room full of bond traders? Because Treasury auction sizes set the long end of the yield curve, and the yield curve prices nearly everything in your financial life. When the government announces it needs to borrow more — and must therefore sell more long-dated bonds — it must offer higher yields to attract buyers. Those higher yields ripple outward: your 30-year mortgage rate moves with the 10-year Treasury, your auto loan, your small business credit line, the discount rate applied to every tech stock in your portfolio. A single line in a QRS that says "we expect to increase auction sizes next quarter" can shift hundreds of billions of dollars in asset values before the sun sets on announcement day. Conversely, a statement holding sizes flat can defuse an entire media narrative about a debt flood — if anyone bothers to read it.

Mechanically, the process works like this. Each quarter, the Treasury Department meets with the primary dealers — the roughly two dozen large financial institutions legally obligated to bid at every Treasury auction — and surveys them on financing needs, market conditions, and recommended issuance strategy. Treasury's own Office of Debt Management synthesizes that input alongside its own cash flow projections. Then it publishes the QRS, which specifies exact dollar sizes for each maturity bucket: the 2-year, 3-year, 5-year, 7-year, 10-year, 20-year, and 30-year. It also includes language about the expected trajectory of those sizes, functioning as forward guidance in precisely the same way the Federal Reserve uses language to signal future rate moves. Primary dealers, hedge funds, foreign central banks, and pension funds all update their models within minutes of release.

The August 2025 QRS is the concrete example that makes all of this tangible. Treasury held every coupon auction size completely flat: $58 billion in 3-year notes, $42 billion in 10-year notes, and $25 billion in 30-year bonds — identical to the prior quarter. More importantly, the document included explicit forward guidance stating that Treasury expects to keep nominal coupon issuance steady for "at least the next several quarters." Primary dealers, the most sophisticated readers of this document on the planet, were not even penciling in a size increase until early 2027. This is the detail that obliterated the "flood of supply" narrative being broadcast across financial television that same week, a narrative built entirely on the observation that Treasury was selling bonds — without asking whether more bonds than usual were actually being sold.

"In the August quarterly refunding statement, the Treasury Department kept its coupon auction sizes completely flat. 58 billion in three-year notes, 42 billion in 10-year notes, and 25 billion in 30-year bonds. The exact same numbers as the quarter before. Treasury wrote that its current auction sizes will leave it well positioned and that it expects to keep nominal coupon issuance steady for at least the next several quarters. The primary dealers don't even expect an increase until early 2027. So, when someone tells you this week that a wave of new issuance is hitting the tape, they've not read the announcement."

Sophisticated traders and investors use the QRS in several layered ways. At the most basic level, they use it to size their positions ahead of auction week — knowing the exact supply coming to market lets them calculate clearing yields and bid-to-cover ratios with precision. More strategically, they use the forward guidance language to position on the yield curve before consensus catches up. If the QRS signals a shift toward more long-end issuance (a move toward longer maturities), traders will typically short long-dated Treasuries in anticipation of supply pressure. If it signals stable or reduced long-end supply — as in August 2025 — the rational trade is to fade yield spikes driven by supply-fear narratives. The QRS also directly informs the basis trade, the complex hedge-fund arbitrage between cash Treasuries and futures, because the basis depends heavily on known supply schedules.

The most common misconception about the QRS is conflating the existence of auctions with an increase in supply. Every single week, Treasury sells bills, notes, and bonds. That is not news. The news is whether auction sizes are growing, shrinking, or holding steady. A journalist who reports that "Treasury is auctioning $42 billion in 10-year notes this week" without noting that $42 billion is flat versus the prior quarter is technically accurate and substantively misleading. A second misconception involves confusing net issuance with gross issuance. Treasury auctions include both new money raised and the rollover of maturing debt — old bonds coming due that simply get replaced with new ones. The deficit only drives the net new money portion. Gross auction sizes can look enormous while net new borrowing remains modest.

The QRS framework can break down under stress in ways that matter enormously for markets. When a genuine fiscal emergency forces Treasury to increase issuance outside the normal quarterly cadence — as happened during COVID-19 in 2020 — the market can experience abrupt repricing before the primary dealer system fully absorbs the shock. The more subtle failure mode is when the QRS guidance turns out to be wrong. If Treasury holds sizes flat for several quarters and then is forced to announce a large increase, the surprise amplifies the market reaction significantly — markets had priced in the guidance and must now rapidly reprice. The autumn 2023 bond market tantrum illustrated this: yields surged from under 4% in July to roughly 5% by October 19th, a move the Fed's own researchers placed above the 99th percentile since 1990, driven in part by the perception that Treasury had been underestimating issuance needs.

The QRS connects directly to several other concepts you need to understand together. Term premium — the extra yield investors demand for lending money over a long horizon rather than rolling over short-term debt — responds to QRS signals because term premium partly reflects uncertainty about future supply. A credible flat-issuance commitment compresses the supply-uncertainty component of term premium. The QRS also intersects with quantitative tightening (QT) and its cessation: when the Fed stopped shrinking its balance sheet in December 2025 and began reinvesting maturing principal, the effective net supply of Treasuries to the private market fell even further below what auction headlines suggested. Reading the August 2025 QRS alongside the end of QT and Treasury's simultaneous doubling of its long-bond buyback operations — from $2 billion to $4 billion per operation — reveals an official sector that was leaning decisively into the long end, not away from it. That the 10-year yield hit three-year highs despite all of this tells you that the repricing was driven by demand destruction, not supply excess — a structurally different and more durable problem that no QRS announcement can solve.
