Risk-Weighted Assets and Capital Requirements

Risk-weighted assets (RWA) are a bank's assets, each scaled by how dangerous regulators think it is. Regulators don't treat a dollar of Treasury bills the same as a dollar of unsecured credit card debt. Each asset type gets a risk weight: 0% for U.S. Treasuries, 20% for many agency securities, 50% for a typical first-lien mortgage, 100% for most business loans, and higher for riskier exposures. Multiply each asset by its weight and add them up, and you get RWA. A capital requirement is the minimum cushion of the bank's own money, mostly shareholder equity, that must be held against that total. It is a fraction of RWA, not of raw assets.

This matters because capital is what absorbs losses before depositors or the FDIC do. A bank is mostly borrowed money: deposits and wholesale funding sit on the liability side, and only a thin slice is owners' equity. If loans go bad, losses hit equity first. Capital rules force that slice to scale with the risk being taken, which puts a hard ceiling on how much lending a bank can do with a given amount of equity. That is why capital, not just cash on hand, determines whether credit is easy or tight in the real economy. A bank with plenty of deposits but thin capital cannot safely grow its loan book.

Mechanically, the calculation runs in a few steps. First, the bank sorts every asset into a risk bucket and applies the weight. Second, it adds the results to get RWA. Third, it divides its regulatory capital by RWA to get a capital ratio. The strictest version is Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1), which counts mainly common stock and retained earnings. Basel III sets a 4.5% CET1 minimum, plus a 2.5% capital conservation buffer, so about 7% in practice. Many banks target 9% to 11% to leave headroom. If the ratio falls below the required level, regulators restrict dividends and buybacks, and eventually can force the bank to raise capital or shrink.

Here is the arithmetic behind the video's claim about a new $100,000 home loan. A prudently underwritten first-lien mortgage carries a 50% risk weight, so the loan adds $50,000 of RWA. At a 10% CET1 target, the bank needs about $5,000 of extra common equity to support it. At the 7% regulatory floor, it needs about $3,500. The rest of the $100,000 is funded with deposits or borrowings. That is the true kernel of the video's point: every new loan does consume a slice of capital. But the amount is a few percent of the loan, not a full $100,000 of "fresh reserves."

"Every time they extend a new $100,000 home loan, they must set aside fresh regulatory reserves and they don't have the spare capital reserves to allocate."

That quote blurs two different things, and the difference is one of the most common misconceptions in this area. Reserves are cash a bank holds at the Federal Reserve or in its vault. The Fed cut the reserve requirement to zero in March 2020, so making a loan doesn't require setting aside reserves at all. Capital is the ownership cushion. It isn't "set aside" in a vault; it is the difference between what the bank owns and what it owes, and the loan simply uses up some of it. The video's underlying idea, that lending is limited by capital headroom, is sound. The vocabulary is wrong, and so is the impression that a loan needs dollar-for-dollar backing.

Investors use these ratios as a health check. The number to watch is CET1 relative to the bank's target and to the regulatory floor, along with the trend across quarters. Thin headroom is a signal that a bank will ration credit, tighten HELOC and small-business terms, skip buybacks, or prefer low-weight assets like Treasuries and agency securities over loans. It can also mean a stock offering is coming, which dilutes shareholders. Traders compare CET1 with tangible common equity, which strips out intangibles like goodwill, and with the size of unrealized bond losses. A bank whose loss-adjusted equity looks far weaker than its reported capital ratio deserves scrutiny.

The video argues that $325 billion of unrealized losses on held-to-maturity (HTM) bonds have wiped out tangible equity, and that this is why lending is being squeezed. Real nuances complicate that story. HTM securities are carried at amortized cost, so their paper losses don't reduce reported earnings or equity. For most U.S. banks below the largest tier, unrealized losses are also excluded from regulatory capital by an opt-out, so CET1 doesn't drop when rates rise. The economic weakness is real, though: if a bank has to sell those bonds, the loss becomes real and may taint the rest of the portfolio. That is why the video's "tangible equity near zero" claim is more accurate for economic value than for regulatory ratios, and it varies a lot from one bank to the next.

When the system breaks, it happens through a mix of capital pressure and funding pressure. In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank had large unrealized HTM and available-for-sale losses, and it was exempt from the tougher rules that applied to the biggest banks. When depositors left, it had to sell bonds, the losses became real, and confidence collapsed within days. A bank under this stress faces the same tradeoff the video describes. It can raise capital, which is expensive and dilutive. It can pay 4% or more for wholesale funding, which squeezes net interest margin. Or it can shrink risk-weighted assets by cutting loans, which is the cheapest fix for the bank and the most painful for borrowers.

Capital requirements connect to several other ideas. They tie into net interest margin, because a squeeze on margin cuts retained earnings, and retained earnings are how banks build capital. They tie into deposit insurance, since the $250,000 FDIC limit is what makes depositors less likely to run. They tie into interest-rate risk, because rising rates hurt bond values and mortgage-heavy balance sheets. The takeaway is balanced. The video is right that capital headroom limits credit and that regional banks with weak balance sheets tighten lending. But a tighter HELOC market can also reflect ordinary credit caution, higher rates, and weaker demand. Check a specific bank's CET1, its unrealized losses, and its funding mix before drawing conclusions.
