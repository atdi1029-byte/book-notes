AAII Investor Sentiment Survey

The AAII Investor Sentiment Survey is a weekly poll run by the American Association of Individual Investors, a nonprofit that educates everyday investors. It asks members one question: over the next six months, do you feel the stock market will go up (bullish), stay about the same (neutral), or go down (bearish)? The answers are tallied into three percentages that add up to 100. The survey has run since 1987, which makes it one of the longest-running measures of how ordinary investors feel. It measures opinion, not money. Nobody has to put a dollar behind their answer, and that is what makes it both useful and easy to misread.

Markets move on what people do, and what people do is shaped partly by what they feel. Fear makes investors sell, and greed makes them chase. A sentiment survey tries to measure those emotions directly, before they fully show up in prices. It matters because extreme moods tend to cluster near turning points. When almost everyone is gloomy, most of the selling may already be done, and when almost everyone is giddy, most of the buying may be done. The survey cannot predict crashes or rallies. It gives you a read on crowd psychology, which you can compare with what prices and positioning are doing.

The mechanics are simple. Each week AAII lets members vote through its website. The voting window runs from Thursday to Wednesday, and AAII publishes the results on Thursday. Each member gets one vote, and the three categories are counted as percentages. The useful context is the long-run average, which sits around 37.5% bullish, 31.5% neutral, and 31% bearish. Analysts watch two things: how far a reading sits from those averages, and the "bull-bear spread," which is bullish minus bearish. A spread of +20 points signals excess optimism, and a spread of -20 or worse signals real pessimism. Many people also smooth the numbers with a four-week or eight-week moving average, because any single week can be noisy.

The video gives a clean example. The presenter said the bearish share fell to 42.2% from 51.4% the week before. That is a drop of 9.2 percentage points in a single week, which is large for this survey. The more important point is where the numbers sit. Even at 42.2%, bearishness is about 11 points above the historical average of roughly 31%. At 51.4%, it was extreme. Only a handful of weeks in the survey's history have gone that high, and they have often come near market lows. So the data shows individual investors were deeply pessimistic and had just become somewhat less so. It does not show a relaxed public.

"In the latest survey from the American Association of Individual Investors, the share of Bears actually fell to 42.2% from 51.4% just the week before. The pros are getting phone calls while Main Street is getting more relaxed."

That quote shows both the appeal and the risk of using the survey in a story. The presenter is contrasting two groups: professionals facing margin calls, and ordinary investors who seem calm. The contrast makes a good narrative, but the data does not really support it. A reading of 42.2% bearish is not calm. It is still historically gloomy. The survey also covers only AAII members, who tend to be older, self-directed, and more engaged than the average person. They are not "Main Street" as a whole, and they say nothing about what hedge funds or levered traders are doing. The honest reading is narrower: members were very bearish, and they got a little less bearish.

Traders mostly use the survey as a contrarian indicator. The logic is that when nearly everyone has already turned bearish, there are few sellers left to push prices lower, so the next move is more likely up. Extreme bearishness, such as readings above 50%, has historically come before above-average six-month and twelve-month returns for the S&P 500. Extreme bullishness has tended to come before weaker returns. Practitioners usually wait for a stretch of extreme readings, or for the spread to cross a threshold, before treating it as a signal. They pair it with other gauges, such as the VIX, put/call ratios, and fund flows, to see whether professional behavior agrees with retail mood.

There are several gotchas. First, timing: the survey closes Wednesday, so it can miss the most recent price action. In this episode, the big selloff and the Anthropic IPO day were Thursday, so the 42.2% reading could not have reflected that day. Second, sentiment is a poor timing tool on its own. Markets can stay pessimistic for months while they fall further, as in 2008, and optimism can last a long time in a bull run. Third, the sample is self-selected and relatively small, often a few hundred responses, so week-to-week changes carry sampling noise. Fourth, a drop in bearishness is not automatically a bullish sign. In this case it may just be the usual swings of a fearful sample.

The survey can fail as a signal in a few ways. In a true cash crunch, prices fall because forced sellers need money, not because opinions changed, and a poll of opinions will lag. The video describes that kind of selling: margin calls, leveraged funds dumping their best assets, and gold sold to raise cash. Those sellers do not appear in an AAII poll of individuals, and their selling is not driven by mood at all. Sentiment can show extreme pessimism while forced liquidation keeps going. A contrarian who buys the first extreme reading can be early by weeks. The sentiment signal is most reliable when the forced selling has ended, which the presenter suggests watching for in other places.

The survey fits with the other gauges the video points to. The VIX measures fear through options prices, which reflect what traders are paying for protection, so it is a market-based reading, whereas AAII is a stated opinion. Margin debt, reported monthly by FINRA, shows how much borrowed money is in the system and how vulnerable it is to forced selling, which no sentiment poll can show. Money market fund balances show whether investors are actually moving into cash. The strongest reading comes when these agree: high bearishness in the survey, a high VIX, and cash piling up would suggest fear has run its course. When they disagree, treat the survey as one clue and not a conclusion.
