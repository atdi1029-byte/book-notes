University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment Index

The University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment Index is one of the most widely watched measures of how ordinary Americans feel about their financial situation and the broader economy. Published by the University of Michigan's Survey Research Center, it asks roughly 500 households each month a battery of questions: Are you better off financially than a year ago? Do you expect to be better off a year from now? Is now a good time to buy a major appliance, a car, a house? The responses are aggregated into a single index number, with 1966 set as the baseline of 100. A reading above 100 signals that consumers feel better than the historical average; a reading below signals the reverse. It is one of the oldest continuous sentiment surveys in the United States, running since 1946.

Consumer sentiment matters because consumer spending drives roughly 70 percent of U.S. GDP. When households feel confident, they open their wallets — they buy cars, remodel kitchens, take vacations, and invest in retirement accounts. When they feel anxious, they pull back, save more, and defer big purchases. This feedback loop is self-reinforcing in both directions: confident consumers fuel growth, which validates their confidence; nervous consumers trigger slowdowns, which validate their fear. For markets, sentiment is therefore a leading indicator — it often moves before actual spending data do, giving investors an early read on where consumption is heading. It is also a barometer of whether monetary policy is biting or not.

Mechanically, the survey works in two phases. A preliminary release drops on the second Friday of each month around 10 a.m. Eastern, based on roughly the first 60 percent of responses collected. This preliminary print is the one that moves markets most sharply, because it arrives early and gives the first signal for the month. A final release follows about two weeks later with the full sample. The overall index is composed of two sub-indices: the Index of Current Economic Conditions, which measures how people feel right now, and the Index of Consumer Expectations, which measures how they expect things to look six to twelve months out. The expectations component is particularly important to the Federal Reserve and to stock market analysts because it signals future demand.

In the week described by Mark Malik in the video, the preliminary University of Michigan sentiment reading was scheduled for Friday at 10 a.m. Wall Street time — landing in the same crowded data window as August CPI and August PPI. The context mattered enormously. The August jobs report had already printed 162,000 payrolls against a consensus of 53,000, a massive beat that pushed 2-year Treasury yields to 4.37 percent and lifted the probability of a September rate hike to roughly 62 percent. In that environment, a weak sentiment reading could have been taken as evidence that consumers were already feeling the pinch of higher rates and $89 oil — potentially giving the Fed cover to pause. A strong reading, on the other hand, would have deepened the dilemma: a resilient consumer spending into rising costs means more inflation pressure, which argues for continued tightening.

Traders use the Michigan sentiment index in several concrete ways. First, they compare the actual print to the consensus estimate — the number analysts expected. A beat lifts consumer-facing sectors like retail, restaurants, and travel; a miss pressures them. Second, they watch the inflation expectations sub-question closely. The survey asks respondents what they expect inflation to be over the next year and over the next five to ten years. The Fed pays particular attention to the long-run inflation expectations figure because if households start believing inflation will stay elevated indefinitely, they demand higher wages and accept higher prices, turning a temporary shock into an entrenched one. When that five-year number drifts above 3 percent, it gets the Fed's attention fast.

One of the most important nuances is the gap between sentiment and actual spending behavior. Historically, there have been prolonged periods — particularly during political upheaval — where sentiment collapses but spending holds up, because employment and wages remain solid. Conversely, sentiment can look elevated right up until a recession begins. Analysts therefore use sentiment as a corroborating signal rather than a standalone forecast. The sub-indices also diverge in instructive ways: if current conditions feel fine but expectations are deteriorating, it suggests consumers see trouble coming even if they haven't felt it yet. That divergence is often a more useful signal than the headline number alone. Never read just the top line.

The index can also break down or mislead in specific environments. Highly partisan political climates distort it significantly — studies have shown that supporters of the party in power rate conditions far more favorably than opposition supporters, regardless of the underlying economic data. Sentiment also responds dramatically to gasoline prices, which are visible to consumers every day at the pump, making the index disproportionately sensitive to energy shocks. In Malik's week, diesel hit record highs and West Texas Intermediate closed at $89.38 — exactly the kind of input that hammers sentiment surveys through the wallet channel even when labor markets look healthy. In that sense, energy prices can make sentiment data look more alarming than underlying fundamentals justify.

The Michigan index sits within a broader ecosystem of sentiment and confidence measures. Its closest companion is the Conference Board Consumer Confidence Index, which tends to correlate heavily with labor market conditions — it asks more directly about jobs, hiring, and business conditions. The Michigan index leans more toward financial conditions and purchasing power. When the two diverge, that divergence itself is informative. Both feed into the same big picture the Federal Reserve is assembling from CPI, PPI, payrolls, JOLTS, ISM services, and yield curve signals. As Malik framed it, by the time the September 15–16 FOMC meeting arrived, the Fed would be sitting with a jobs number fighting an ADP estimate, inflation data capturing $89 oil for the first time, and a sentiment print telling it whether the American consumer had started to crack — or was stubbornly, inflationary, still spending.
