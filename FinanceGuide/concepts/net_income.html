Net Income (Net Earnings)

Net income, also called net earnings, is the profit a company has left over after every single expense has been subtracted from revenue. That includes the cost of the product itself, sales and marketing, corporate overhead, interest paid on debt, and taxes. It sits at the very bottom of the income statement, which is exactly why traders call it "the bottom line." Every other number on that statement — revenue, gross margin, operating income — measures profit at an earlier, less complete stage. Net income is the final answer to the question "did this company actually make money this quarter, after absolutely everything was paid?" For a homebuilder like Lennar, that means after the cost of land, lumber, labor, mortgage rate buy-downs, corporate salaries, interest expense, and the IRS all take their cut of the revenue from every house closed.

Net income matters because it's the cleanest single number for judging whether a business is actually healthy, and it's the number that ties most directly to what shareholders own. Earnings per share is just net income divided by shares outstanding, and stock prices are, over the long run, driven heavily by earnings. When net income falls, it signals that the economics of the business are deteriorating even if the top-line story looks fine. That's precisely the tension in the Lennar report: revenue and closings stayed roughly stable while net income collapsed, which tells you the damage is happening inside the business, not in how many homes are moving out the door. A headline about "earnings" dropping is really a headline about how much profit survived the trip from the top of the income statement to the bottom.

Mechanically, net income is built by working down through a stack of subtractions. You start with revenue, subtract the direct cost of building the homes to get gross profit, subtract operating expenses like SG&A to get operating income, subtract interest expense and add or subtract any one-time items to get pre-tax income, and then subtract taxes to arrive at net income. Every layer in that stack can move independently. A company can hold revenue flat while gross margin shrinks because it's spending more to buy down mortgage rates or discount prices — that squeeze shows up first in gross margin, then flows straight down and shows up amplified at net income, because none of the expenses below gross profit shrank to compensate.

Lennar's third-quarter numbers show this mechanism in action with real figures. Net earnings fell to $284 million from $591 million a year earlier — a drop of about 52%. Gross margin, one layer up the stack, fell from 17.5% to 15.8%, and is down sharply from the pandemic peak of 29.5%. That relatively modest-looking margin compression at the gross profit line got magnified into a much larger percentage decline by the time it reached net income, because fixed costs like overhead and interest expense didn't fall in proportion. Meanwhile earnings per share fell "only" about 48%, from $2.29 to $1.19, because Lennar had been buying back stock, which shrinks the share count and cushions the per-share number even when total net income falls harder.

"We are compromising margin in order to maintain volume. Of course, we understand that this is a choice. It is deliberate and it is not something the market is doing to us." — Stuart Miller, Lennar

Traders and analysts use net income and its trend as a core input for valuation, most commonly through the price-to-earnings ratio, and as a gut check on management's public narrative. When a CEO says volume is holding up fine, the net income line is where you check whether that's coming at a cost. A widening gap between stable revenue and falling net income is a classic early-warning signal: it usually means the company is buying growth or market share with margin, a trade-off that can't continue forever. Miller's own admission that this was "a choice" — deliberately sacrificing margin to keep sales and construction volume up — is the kind of statement traders listen for on earnings calls, because it tells you management is aware of the trend and is making an active bet rather than being surprised by market conditions.

The biggest gotcha with net income is that it's an accounting figure, not a cash figure, and it can be moved by things that have nothing to do with the underlying business getting better or worse. Stock buybacks are a good example: they don't change net income at all, but they raise earnings per share by shrinking the denominator, which is why EPS fell less than net income did in Lennar's case even though the actual dollars of profit fell by half. One-time items — a gain on selling an asset, a tax settlement, a write-down — can also swing net income in a given quarter without reflecting the ongoing health of the business. That's why serious analysis usually looks at net income alongside gross margin trends, cash flow, and per-unit economics rather than taking the single bottom-line number at face value.

Net income can also mislead when the components offsetting each other are moving in different directions for structurally different reasons, which is exactly the trap in this Lennar story. The company's headline sales incentive rate actually fell three quarters in a row — from 14.3% a year ago to 12% this quarter — which sounds like builders are pulling back on giveaways. But running the dollar math shows the incentive per house fell by about $10,129 while the average sale price fell by about $11,000, meaning total buyer subsidy per home changed by only $871 on a nearly $400,000 house. Net income fell hard not because incentives disappeared, but because the type of subsidy shifted — from an invisible mortgage rate buy-down toward a permanent, visible price cut that hits every future appraisal and comp on the street. The bottom line captured the pain; it didn't by itself explain where the pain was coming from.

Net income breaks down as a useful signal when it's compared across companies or periods without adjusting for structural differences like buybacks, debt load, or accounting choices, and it can fail as a forward-looking indicator because it's backward-looking by definition — it tells you what already happened last quarter, not what's coming. This connects directly to why the video insists on checking the APR disclosure rather than the advertised teaser rate on a mortgage buy-down: both are cases where a single headline number (net income, or a 3.99% rate) hides a more complete calculation underneath it (the margin math, or the 6.854% APR) that only becomes visible if you're willing to do the arithmetic yourself. Understanding net income properly means always asking what's sitting between it and revenue — gross margin, operating expenses, buybacks, one-time items — because that's where the real story about a company's health actually lives.
