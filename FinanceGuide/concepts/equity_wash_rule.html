Equity Wash Rule (Stable Value Transfer Restriction)

The equity wash rule is a restriction found in most 401(k) and 403(b) plans that offer a stable value fund. It says you can't move money directly from the stable value fund into a "competing" fund, meaning a money market fund, a short-term bond fund, or another low-risk cash-like option. To get there, you first move the money into a non-competing fund, usually a stock fund or a longer-term bond fund. You leave it there for a set period, commonly 90 days, and only then move it on. The money gets "washed" through equities before it can reach cash. The video is right that this exists. It is a plan rule written into the fund's contracts, not a law.

The rule matters because stable value is one of the largest holdings in American retirement plans. The video puts it at roughly $900 billion across more than 20 million accounts. These funds are popular because they pay more than money market funds in most years, and the balance on your statement doesn't bounce around. That smooth ride isn't free. The rule is one of the conditions that makes the smoothness possible, and it limits what you can do with your own savings. A participant who doesn't know about it can be caught off guard by a rate move, or by the need to reposition quickly. It also helps explain why stable value behaves differently from the cash alternatives it is often compared with.

To see why the rule exists, you need the mechanics of the fund. A stable value fund buys intermediate-term bonds, typically three to seven years, and pairs them with wrap contracts sold by insurers. The wrap lets the fund report book value, the dollars you put in plus interest, instead of market value. The fund pays you a crediting rate, which adjusts slowly toward market yields over several years. That slowness is the problem. When market rates jump, the crediting rate lags behind, so cash funds suddenly pay more. If everyone could leave for a money market fund at book value, the high-paying ones would exit first and stick the remaining holders with the lagging, underwater portfolio. The wash rule blocks that exit.

Here is how it plays out with numbers. Say you hold $50,000 in your plan's stable value fund, and it credits 3.0%. The video describes a world where the 10-year Treasury yield pushes toward 5.34% and the 30-year toward 5.60%, so a government money market fund might pay around 5%. That gap, about two points, is roughly $1,000 a year on your balance. You'd like to switch. The plan says you can't go directly to the money market fund. Your choices are to move to an equity or bond fund, wait 90 days, then move to the money market fund. Or you accept the lower crediting rate while it catches up to market yields.

"To prevent consumers from arbitraging rising rates, you're legally forbidden from directly transferring stable value balances into any competing short-term cash vehicle."

Traders and investors use this rule as a planning tool and as a warning signal. First, it tells you that stable value is a different product from a money market fund, and you shouldn't treat the two as interchangeable cash. Second, the size of the gap between the crediting rate and money market yields shows how much pressure the rule is holding back. A wide gap means the rule is doing real work. Third, if you expect to need a lump sum, such as a down payment or a job change, the video's advice is to read your plan documents months ahead and know which transfers are allowed. Many plans let you move into non-competing options, and some let you step out through a self-directed brokerage window. The video also suggests holding Treasury bills directly for money you need as true cash.

The video overstates a few points, and you should separate the true parts from the dramatic ones. The restriction is contractual and set at the plan level. It isn't a federal statute, and rules vary: some plans have no wash period at all, some use 90 days, and some use 120 days or longer. It also doesn't trap your money. It only limits transfers between investment options inside the plan. You can generally still take a distribution, roll over to an IRA, or take a loan at book value if your plan allows it, and the wrap normally covers participant-initiated withdrawals. Many "equity washes" only require you to park the money in any non-competing fund, which may be a stable bond fund rather than stocks.

The video also blends this rule with the much more serious employer-initiated events. When a company terminates the plan, changes record keepers, or triggers a large redemption, wrap contracts may allow a delay of up to 12 months. That is a separate mechanism from the wash rule, and it is rarer. The video's 85-cent market-to-book figure is also far worse than what most funds reported. A market-to-book ratio that low would be a real stress event. Ratios in the 90s during rate spikes are more typical, and the video's own advice is to check yours. The risk of the wash rule is mostly opportunity cost, not loss of principal.

What goes wrong with the wash rule? The first risk is the one the video points at: you are stuck earning a crediting rate below market while rates rise. The second is the detour itself. If you move through an equity fund for 90 days to satisfy the rule, you take on stock market risk for that period. A drop of 8% during the wait on $50,000 costs $4,000, which could erase more than three years of the extra yield you were chasing. That is the real trap, and the video describes it correctly. The third risk is rare: if the wrap provider's finances weaken or the plan hits a trigger event, the book value guarantee gets tested. Then the contract terms matter more than the marketing label.

The equity wash rule connects to several other ideas. Duration risk is why the underlying bonds lose value when yields rise, and book value versus market value accounting is why your statement doesn't show it. The market-to-book ratio measures the gap, and the video suggests worrying below 92%. The wrap contract and the 12-month employer put provisions sit behind the same structure. The comparison to money market funds is the key one, since those hold days-to-weeks paper and reprice almost immediately. Think of the wash rule as the fence that keeps the smooth, slow-moving rate in place. You can talk about it confidently as a plan rule that limits switching between options, not as a freeze on your retirement money.
