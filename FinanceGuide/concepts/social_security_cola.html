Social Security Cost-of-Living Adjustment (COLA)

The Social Security Cost-of-Living Adjustment (COLA) is the yearly increase in Social Security benefits meant to keep a monthly check from losing buying power as prices rise. If groceries, rent and gasoline cost 3% more than last year, the program is supposed to pay beneficiaries roughly 3% more. About 71.5 million Americans collect Social Security, so this one percentage is among the most watched numbers in the country. Each October the Social Security Administration announces it, and the higher payments begin in January. The video's key point is that the COLA is not really a "raise." It is a backward-looking receipt: a refund for price increases retirees have already absorbed.

The COLA matters because for many retirees Social Security is the biggest and most dependable income they have. Roughly half of older households lean on it for a large share of their spending, and it is the one income stream that is legally indexed to inflation. A pension may be frozen for decades and a bond coupon is fixed, but the COLA adjusts. That makes it a quiet force in the real economy. Retirees spend most of what they receive, so a larger or smaller COLA shifts demand for healthcare, groceries, utilities and housing. It also shapes political debate, because every percentage point costs the trust funds tens of billions of dollars over time. For anyone planning retirement, the COLA is the foundation under their inflation assumptions.

Mechanically, the calculation is simple and fully rule-based. The government uses the CPI-W, the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers. It takes the average CPI-W for July, August and September, the third quarter, and compares it with the average for the same three months a year earlier. The percentage increase, rounded to one decimal place, is the COLA. No committee votes and no judgment is involved. The September inflation report arrives in mid-October, at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, and the Social Security Administration announces the COLA the same morning. This year that was Wednesday, October 14th. The new amount is then applied to benefits starting in January.

The video's numbers show how thin the margin can be. By the time the announcement arrived, July and August were already known: CPI-W was up about 3.4% year over year in July and about 3.5% in August. Only September was still unknown. According to the host's math on the government's own index levels, if September rose at the same pace as August, the COLA would round to 3.5%. If prices were flat from August to September, it would round down to 3.4%. Forecasters clustered around that range: The Senior Citizens League at 3.5%, AARP at 3.6%, and some estimates as low as 3.4%. For comparison, last year's COLA was about 2.8%, the year before that was 2.5%, and 2024's was 3.2%. In dollars, the video says a 3.5% COLA on an average retired-worker benefit of about $2,080 adds roughly $73 a month.

"The COLA is not a raise. It's a receipt. It looks backward. It repays you for price increases that you've already been living with all year."

The "receipt" idea explains why the COLA always feels late. The third-quarter average is measured in the summer, announced in October and paid in January, so prices are already well ahead of the check by the time the check catches up. The video's example is gasoline. The AAA national average was about $4.36 a gallon, roughly 41% above a year earlier, and retirees had been paying that at the pump for months. The COLA only starts to offset it in the new year, and it does so on the basis of a broad basket of prices, not on the bills a given household actually pays. In a year when inflation is accelerating, the lag hurts more. In a year when inflation is falling, it can briefly work in retirees' favor.

For investors and planners, the COLA is useful as both a data point and a planning input. The CPI-W readings for July, August and September let you estimate the COLA before it is official, which is what forecasters like The Senior Citizens League do. The announcement also gives a clean read on how much inflation is flowing into household incomes. A bigger COLA means more spending power for retirees, which can support sectors like healthcare and consumer staples. For individuals, the sensible move is the video's first piece of advice: ignore the gross raise and do your own net math. Compare the net deposit after Medicare, then check how the higher benefit changes your taxable income.

The gotchas are where the video spends most of its time, describing "three hands" that reach into the raise. Hand one is Medicare. Part B premiums are usually deducted straight from the Social Security check. The video cites the current standard premium at roughly $202.90 a month (the transcript garbles this figure), up $17.90 from last year, and projects another increase of about $6.60 a month next year. That would take about $79 over a year before any doctor bill, which turns the $73 raise into something closer to $66. The hold harmless rule says a Part B increase cannot exceed your COLA increase in dollars, so your check can't shrink. But it only bites when the COLA is tiny, and it never protects higher-income surcharge payers or new enrollees.

Hand two is the tax code, the part the video calls the "shadow data." Many people assume Social Security is tax-free, but it isn't. The IRS measures provisional income: half your Social Security benefit plus pensions, IRA withdrawals, interest and even tax-exempt municipal bond interest. For a single filer, provisional income above $25,000 makes up to 50% of benefits taxable. Above $34,000, up to 85% is taxable. For couples the thresholds are $32,000 and $44,000. Those lines were set in 1983 and 1993 and have never been indexed to inflation. Each COLA pushes provisional income up while the tripwire stays put. As a result, more retirees cross it every year without Congress ever voting to raise the tax.

Hand three is the index itself. CPI-W tracks what working households buy, but retirees spend a larger share on healthcare and housing. An experimental index for households headed by someone 62 or older, the CPI-E, would have produced a bigger raise in seven of the last ten years, according to The Senior Citizens League. This year it would have given 3.1% instead of 2.8%. The video is fair about the other side: the government treats CPI-E as a research series, and in 2022 and 2023 the official gauge actually paid retirees more. The average gap is only about 0.2 percentage points a year. Still, a small leak compounded over a 20- or 30-year retirement adds up to a real loss in buying power.

The COLA can also fail you through timing and through the income rules around it. Medicare's income-related surcharge, IRMAA, is based on your income from two years earlier. This year it starts above $109,000 for a single person and $218,000 for a couple. Cross it and the standard Part B premium jumps to roughly $284 a month, with a surcharge on drug coverage too. One big year, such as selling a house, a large IRA withdrawal or a Roth conversion, can show up as a higher Medicare bill two years later. The 2025 tax law added a temporary extra deduction for people 65 and older ($6,000 per person, or $12,000 for a qualifying couple, for tax years 2025 through 2028), which helps. But it does not change how much of your benefit is taxable.

The COLA ties into several larger ideas. It is an inflation-indexing mechanism, so it connects to CPI, to inflation-protected assets like TIPS, and to the broader question of whether your income grows as fast as your costs. The 1983 and 1993 thresholds are an example of "bracket creep," where unindexed rules quietly raise taxes as nominal incomes rise. The two-year IRMAA lookback links the COLA to tax planning and to the sequence in which you draw down accounts. Underneath it all sits trust-fund solvency. The 2026 trustees report says the combined funds can pay full benefits until about 2034, after which incoming taxes cover roughly 83% of scheduled benefits. The retirement fund alone runs short in late 2032, with about 78% payable. That is why the video says part of your portfolio needs to grow faster than the prices you pay.

"It is believing that a number with the words 'cost of living' in it actually covers their cost of living."

So how should you talk about the COLA with confidence? Say it plainly: it is an automatic, formula-driven adjustment based on third-quarter CPI-W, announced in October, paid in January, and always looking backward. Then say what shrinks it: Medicare premiums come out first, and tax thresholds frozen since 1983 and 1993 may make more of the benefit taxable. Then say the basket may not match a retiree's actual spending. A $73 headline raise can end up closer to $66 after Medicare, and less after taxes. The practical takeaways are to look at net deposits, watch provisional income and the two-year IRMAA window, shop Medicare plans every open enrollment (October 15th to December 7th), and never build a retirement plan that assumes the COLA keeps you whole.
