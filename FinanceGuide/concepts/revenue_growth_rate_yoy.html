Year-over-Year Revenue Growth Rate

Year-over-year revenue growth rate (often shortened to YoY growth) measures how much a company's sales increased or decreased compared to the same period one year earlier. It's expressed as a percentage: take the current period's revenue, subtract the same period's revenue from a year ago, divide by that prior-year figure, and multiply by 100. If a company made $787 million in Q2 of one year and $11.5 billion in Q2 the following year, the growth isn't just "a lot bigger" — it's a specific, calculable multiple. In this case $11.5 billion divided by $787 million is roughly 14.6, meaning revenue grew by about 1,360%. That's the arithmetic behind the video's claim that Anthropic was "more than 14 times bigger in just 12 months."

This number matters because it's one of the cleanest ways to separate real demand from hype. Anyone can write a press release claiming their product is popular. Revenue is money that already changed hands — customers signed contracts, paid invoices, and kept paying. When a growth rate is this extreme and sustained over a full quarter rather than a single spike, it tells you something structural is happening in the market, not just a marketing push. It's also the number that ultimately has to justify a valuation. A company can't be worth $2 trillion on vibes forever; eventually the growth rate either supports the price tag or it doesn't, and investors watch this metric closely to judge which way that's heading.

Mechanically, calculating YoY growth requires comparing identical, non-overlapping time periods — same quarter, same month, or same year, just twelve months apart. This matters because revenue is often seasonal: a retailer's Q4 (holiday shopping) will always dwarf its Q2, so comparing Q4 to Q2 tells you nothing about underlying growth. Comparing Q2-to-Q2, as the video does with Anthropic's $787 million versus $11.5 billion, strips out seasonality and isolates the actual trend. The formula itself is simple — (Current − Prior) ÷ Prior × 100 — but the discipline is in choosing comparable periods and using consistent revenue definitions (gross vs. net, GAAP vs. adjusted) so you're not comparing apples to oranges.

The Anthropic example from the video is about as dramatic a real-world case as exists in corporate history. Bloomberg reported, based on internal documents, that Anthropic generated approximately $11.5 billion in Q2 revenue against roughly $787 million in the same quarter a year prior. That's not incremental improvement — it's a company that got more than fourteen times bigger while simultaneously posting, reportedly, its first quarter of positive adjusted operating income. The video uses this figure deliberately to counter the narrative that AI valuations are pure speculation:

"This isn't 1999. The revenue actually showed up this time."

The comparison to the 1999 dot-com bubble is precise use of this metric — many dot-com companies had impressive growth rates off tiny or nonexistent revenue bases, or grew revenue while burning cash with no path to profitability. Anthropic's growth rate paired with positive operating income is a fundamentally different signal.

Traders and investors use YoY growth rate as a first-pass filter for whether a company (or an entire sector) deserves its valuation. A high growth rate can justify a high price-to-sales multiple because the market is pricing in future revenue, not just current revenue. Analysts also watch the trend in the growth rate itself — is it accelerating, decelerating, or holding steady? A company growing 1,360% one year but projected to grow only 40% the next is decelerating fast, even though both numbers sound impressive in isolation, and that deceleration curve often matters more to a stock price than the absolute growth number. In the context of the video, the reported jump from a ~$965 billion valuation in May to a rumored $2 trillion valuation by October — a 107% markup in five months — only holds together as rational pricing if revenue growth like the Q2 figures continues or investors believe it will.

The biggest gotcha with YoY growth rate is the base effect. Extreme percentage growth is far easier to achieve off a small starting number than a large one. Going from $787 million to $11.5 billion is mathematically dramatic, but growing an already-massive $100 billion revenue base by even 20% is an enormous absolute dollar increase that would never look as flashy as a percentage. This is why maturing companies naturally see their YoY growth rates decline over time — not because something is wrong, but because the denominator keeps getting bigger. Another nuance: reported figures from unnamed sources, as the video is careful to flag ("the company hasn't confirmed it... treat it as reporting and not as a filing just yet"), aren't the same as audited financial statements. Growth rates calculated from leaked or unofficial numbers deserve more skepticism than those from SEC filings or earnings calls.

YoY growth rate breaks down as a useful signal in a few specific ways. First, if revenue growth comes from one-time events — a bulk prepayment, a single mega-contract, an accounting reclassification — the rate can spike without reflecting a durable trend, and the next year's comparison can look artificially weak or even negative by contrast. Second, extremely high percentage growth rates are mathematically unsustainable forever; a company can't grow 1,360% every year without eventually exceeding the size of the entire global economy, so investors should expect the rate to compress even if the business is healthy. Third, revenue growth alone says nothing about profitability or cash burn — a company can post explosive YoY growth while losing more money than ever if costs (like the massive infrastructure and power contracts discussed elsewhere in the video) are growing even faster. That's why the video specifically notes Anthropic's positive operating income alongside its revenue growth; either number alone would be a less convincing story.

YoY revenue growth connects directly to the video's broader theme of distinguishing genuine demand from speculative excess. It pairs with concepts like valuation multiples (the price investors are willing to pay per dollar of revenue or earnings), capital expenditure commitments (the power contracts and chip purchases that revenue growth is supposed to eventually justify), and operating income (whether growth is translating into actual profit). When a company's revenue growth rate outpaces its spending growth, that's a sign of a business finding real product-market fit. When spending outpaces revenue growth for too long, that's the setup for exactly the kind of "timing mismatch between what you promised to pay for and when the revenue actually shows up" that the video warns is the real risk hiding underneath the AI boom.
