JOLTS (Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey)

The Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey, universally known by its acronym JOLTS, is a monthly report published by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics that measures the health of the labor market from a completely different angle than a standard jobs report. While the famous monthly payroll number tells you how many people got hired, JOLTS tells you how many unfilled positions employers are actively trying to fill, how many workers quit voluntarily, how many were laid off, and how many were hired during the month. Think of it this way: the payroll number is a snapshot of who showed up to work; JOLTS is a look inside the hiring pipeline itself — the demand side of the job market before a single offer is accepted.

JOLTS matters because it gives policymakers, economists, and investors a forward-looking read on labor market tightness. Job openings — the headline figure — represent unfulfilled employer demand for workers. When openings are high relative to the number of unemployed workers, it signals that employers want more labor than is available, which puts upward pressure on wages as companies compete for a limited pool of candidates. Higher wages sound great for workers, but for the Federal Reserve, persistent wage growth is a core driver of services inflation, the stubbornest category to bring down. So JOLTS is not just a labor statistic — it is a direct input into the inflation fight, which makes it one of the most closely watched data points on Wall Street.

Mechanically, the BLS surveys roughly 21,000 business establishments every month, asking them to count open positions as of the last business day of the reference month — positions that exist, are available to start within 30 days, and for which the employer is actively recruiting. Respondents also report hires (people added to payroll), separations (anyone who left, for any reason), quits (voluntary departures), and layoffs and discharges (involuntary exits). Each of these flows tells a different story. Quits, for instance, are a proxy for worker confidence — people only walk off a job without another lined up when they believe the market will absorb them quickly. The quits rate became so closely watched during 2021–2022 that it earned the nickname the "take this job and shove it" index on trading floors.

The report is released with roughly a six-week lag, which means when July's JOLTS data hits the wires, it is already early September. That delay is a feature, not a bug — the BLS needs time to survey, clean, and seasonally adjust the data — but it creates a timing challenge for markets. As Mark Malik highlighted in this week's recap, the July JOLTS report showed job openings flat at 7.3 million, while June's figure was revised down by 177,000. On its face, 7.3 million openings sounds robust, but the downward revision means the prior month was weaker than initially reported, quietly softening the trend line. Revisions like this are why experienced traders never react to a single JOLTS print in isolation — the revision to the previous month often carries as much signal as the new headline number.

Traders and investors use JOLTS as a cross-check and a lead indicator. When openings are running well above the number of unemployed workers — a ratio called the job openings-to-unemployed ratio — the Fed tends to stay hawkish because labor market tightness sustains wage inflation. Conversely, when openings fall sharply toward or below 1:1 with unemployed workers, it signals slack is returning and rate cuts become more plausible. Portfolio managers use JOLTS to position in rate-sensitive sectors: falling openings tend to boost bonds (lower yields), support growth stocks, and pressure financial sector earnings. Rising openings do the opposite. Options traders will also watch JOLTS release dates and price volatility around them, particularly in the weeks immediately before a scheduled Federal Open Market Committee decision.

One of the most important nuances of JOLTS is how it interacts — and sometimes conflicts — with other labor market reports. This is exactly what made the week in the video so volatile. The sequence was: Tuesday's JOLTS showed 7.3 million openings with a downward June revision, implying modest softening; Wednesday's ADP National Employment Report showed only 38,000 private payroll additions in August, a shockingly weak number; then Friday's official Bureau of Labor Statistics payroll report came in at 162,000 — more than four times what ADP suggested. Three major reports, three different stories about the same labor market in the same week. The Fed, sitting inside its blackout period ahead of a September 16th meeting, had to form a policy judgment from data actively fighting with itself.

The gotcha that trips up most newcomers is treating JOLTS as if it were the primary jobs report. It is not. JOLTS measures demand for labor (openings) and flows through the labor market (hires, quits, layoffs). The payroll report measures actual employment outcomes. These can diverge sharply. Employers can post job openings and then never fill them — a phenomenon that spiked post-pandemic when companies posted defensively to signal growth plans without committing to hiring. This means JOLTS can look strong while actual job creation slows, or vice versa. The revisions problem compounds this: because the survey sample is smaller than BLS's payroll methodology, JOLTS data is revised more substantially, and the market sometimes reacts to a number that will look quite different a month later.

When JOLTS data breaks down as a reliable signal — as it did in the summer of 2023 through 2025 — it creates exactly the kind of Fed paralysis on display in the video. Mark Malik captured it precisely:

"The surveys, well, they disagreed. The Fed had to decide on September 16th, coming up fast, using numbers still fighting amongst themselves."
This is the tail risk of over-relying on any single labor market indicator. The Fed's dual mandate requires it to act on the best available data, but when JOLTS says "softening," ADP says "stalling," and the official payroll number says "strong," each rate decision carries more uncertainty and therefore more market volatility. Understanding JOLTS as one instrument in a larger dashboard — alongside payrolls, the unemployment rate, average hourly earnings, and the quits rate — is the difference between reading a single note and reading the whole chord. The concept connects directly to yield curve movements, inflation expectations, and ultimately the discount rate that prices every stock in the market.
