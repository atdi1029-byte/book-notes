Phillips Curve

The Phillips Curve is one of the most famous relationships in macroeconomics: the idea that there is a stable, predictable tradeoff between inflation and unemployment. Named after New Zealand economist A.W. Phillips, who in 1958 documented a consistent inverse relationship between wage inflation and unemployment in the UK from 1861 to 1957, the theory holds that when unemployment falls, wages and prices rise — workers have more bargaining power, employers compete for scarce labor, and money flows more freely. Conversely, when unemployment rises, inflation tends to cool. The implication for policymakers seemed powerful: you could, in theory, "choose" your desired point on the curve, accepting a little more inflation in exchange for lower unemployment, or tolerating more joblessness to tame rising prices.

The reason the Phillips Curve matters is that it became the backbone of post-war economic policymaking. Central banks and governments used it to justify decisions: if inflation ran hot, you could accept some unemployment as the cost of cooling it. If you wanted to stimulate the job market, you could tolerate a bit more price pressure. The Federal Reserve's dual mandate — maximum employment and price stability — is implicitly shaped by this tradeoff logic. Every time the Fed raises interest rates to fight inflation, it is leaning on Phillips Curve reasoning: tighter policy slows demand, demand slowdowns ease the labor market, and a looser labor market reduces wage pressure, which eventually brings prices down. Understanding the curve means understanding the theoretical scaffolding behind every major central bank decision of the last sixty years.

Mechanically, the Phillips Curve works through the labor market transmission channel. When an economy is running hot — unemployment is low, job openings are plentiful — workers can demand higher wages because employers need them more than they need any one employer. Rising wages increase household spending power, pushing up demand for goods and services. Businesses facing higher labor costs and stronger demand pass both onto consumers as higher prices. That is the inflation side of the tradeoff. Run the film backward: the Fed raises interest rates, borrowing becomes expensive, businesses cut back on investment and hiring, unemployment creeps up, workers accept lower wage growth, businesses face weaker demand and restrain price increases, and inflation falls. The curve, in its original form, maps this relationship as a smooth downward-sloping line from high inflation/low unemployment to low inflation/high unemployment.

The most important real-world test of the Phillips Curve came during the 1970s, and it was a spectacular failure. Stagflation — simultaneously high inflation and high unemployment — arrived and simply did not exist on the original curve. Economists Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps had actually predicted this failure in the late 1960s, arguing that the original curve ignored inflation expectations. If workers expect inflation, they demand higher wages preemptively, and businesses raise prices preemptively, which means the government cannot permanently trade a little inflation for lower unemployment — workers adapt, and the "long-run Phillips Curve" is vertical, not downward-sloping. You can fool the labor market temporarily, but once expectations adjust, you just get more inflation without any employment benefit. The 1970s proved them right in the most painful possible way.

The video provides a sharp, contemporary illustration of why the Phillips Curve has lost its grip on actual policy decisions. The video's narrator states plainly:

"Nobody in Washington is demanding lower rates because they read a Phillips curve. They want lower rates because the government is the largest floating rate borrower on the planet Earth, and it has to refinance trillions of dollars of short-dated paper every single year."

By September 2026, with the national debt crossing $40 trillion and federal interest costs hitting $1.25 trillion — 18.5% of all federal revenue — the political pressure on the Fed had nothing to do with inflation-employment tradeoffs. It was about the government's own borrowing costs. A quarter-point hike takes the prime rate from 6.75% to 7%, and every dollar of floating-rate federal debt immediately costs more. The Phillips Curve framework would ask: where is unemployment? What is the output gap? Is there spare capacity? The Treasury asks a simpler question: can we afford the coupon payment?

For traders and investors, the Phillips Curve still functions as a signal-generating framework, even if imperfect. When unemployment falls below what economists call the NAIRU — the Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment — markets tend to price in more aggressive Fed tightening. Bond traders watch nonfarm payrolls and average hourly earnings precisely because wage growth is one of the stickiest inflation inputs; if wages are rising faster than productivity, prices will follow. In the video's scenario, the August inflation data that pushed Fed hike probabilities from 58% to 90% is exactly this mechanism at work — not because anyone drew a Phillips Curve, but because the data signaled that inflation was not cooling despite prior rate increases, forcing a reassessment of where the economy sat on that theoretical tradeoff spectrum.

The most important gotcha of the Phillips Curve is that it is not stable. The relationship shifts depending on inflation expectations anchoring, globalization, productivity growth, and — critically — the credibility of the central bank itself. In the 1990s and 2000s, unemployment fell to historically low levels without triggering significant inflation, a phenomenon economists called the "flat Phillips Curve." Supply chains, foreign labor competition, and Fed credibility all conspired to break the old relationship. Many economists declared the curve dead. Then the post-COVID inflation surge of 2021-2023 suggested it was merely dormant: when supply shocks are large enough and expectations de-anchor, the curve reasserts itself violently. The lesson is not that the curve is wrong — it is that it is contingent on conditions that can change without warning.

The curve breaks down most visibly under supply-side shocks, which is where its policy usefulness collapses entirely. Demand-driven inflation fits the Phillips framework reasonably well: overheat the economy, push unemployment low, and prices rise. But supply shocks — an oil embargo, a pandemic, a war disrupting commodity markets — can cause prices to spike even as demand falls and unemployment rises. This is stagflation, and the Phillips Curve has no answer for it. Tightening policy to fight supply-shock inflation deliberately creates unemployment without solving the underlying supply problem. The Fed in 2022-2023 faced exactly this dilemma: some of the inflation was demand-driven (fiscal stimulus checks), some was supply-driven (logistics disruptions), and applying a blunt rate-hike instrument to both simultaneously meant accepting significant economic pain for a problem that partly required time and supply chain normalization, not just monetary restriction.

The concept connects directly to fiscal dominance — the central theme of the video — and to the credibility architecture that makes modern central banking function at all. The Phillips Curve is usable only if the central bank is trusted to act on it. When a government needs low interest rates to finance its own debt, and that pressure compromises the central bank's willingness to hike, inflation expectations can de-anchor. Once workers and businesses stop trusting that the Fed will keep inflation near 2%, they build higher inflation into wages and contracts preemptively — and the Phillips Curve relationship becomes self-fulfilling in the wrong direction. The 30-year Treasury yield at a 19-year high, as described in the video, is partly the bond market pricing this exact risk: not just current inflation, but the credibility of future inflation control when fiscal pressures are this intense. The curve, in that sense, is not just a theoretical model. It is a trust instrument, and its validity depends entirely on whether people believe policymakers will actually use it.
