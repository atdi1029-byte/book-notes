Retirement Withdrawal Sequencing (Tax-Aware Drawdown Order)

Retirement withdrawal sequencing is the decision about which account you pull money from, in what order and in what mix, to cover your spending. Most retirees hold three kinds of money. Taxable accounts are brokerage accounts and savings. Tax-deferred accounts are traditional IRAs and 401(k)s, where every dollar withdrawn counts as ordinary income. Tax-free accounts are Roth IRAs and Roth 401(k)s, where qualified withdrawals are not taxed. A $50,000 spending need can be met from any of them, and the tax bill for the same $50,000 can differ a lot. The video puts it simply: the order can matter as much as the amount.

It matters because retirement income gets hit by taxes and fees that don't work like a normal paycheck. Withdrawals from a traditional IRA raise your taxable income, and they can also make more of your Social Security taxable. They can push you over the line for Medicare's income surcharge. Different accounts send different signals to the tax system. A dollar from a Roth is invisible to it. A dollar from an IRA is fully visible and has side effects. Over a 25 or 30 year retirement, choosing well can save tens of thousands of dollars without taking any extra investment risk. The savings come from the order of withdrawals, not from picking better stocks. That makes sequencing one of the few retirement levers fully under your control.

The mechanism starts with a number the IRS calls provisional income. The video defines it as half your Social Security benefit plus your other income: pensions, IRA withdrawals, interest, and even tax-exempt municipal bond interest. For a single filer, benefits become up to 50% taxable once provisional income passes $25,000, and up to 85% taxable above $34,000. For married couples filing jointly, the lines are $32,000 and $44,000. Provisional income is the gate, and what counts toward it depends on where the money comes from. IRA withdrawals and interest count. Qualified Roth withdrawals do not. Selling a taxable investment counts only to the extent of the gain, not the return of your original cost. So you can control the gate by choosing which account funds your spending.

Here is a worked example. A single retiree gets $30,000 a year from Social Security and needs another $20,000 to live on. Take the $20,000 from a traditional IRA. Provisional income is $15,000 (half of benefits) plus $20,000, which is $35,000. That is $1,000 over the 85% line, so about $5,350 of the benefit becomes taxable: 85% of the $1,000 excess, plus the $4,500 maximum from the middle tier. Now take the same $20,000 from a Roth. Provisional income is just $15,000, under the $25,000 threshold, so none of the Social Security is taxed. The spending is identical. The difference is thousands of dollars of income the IRS now sees, in the first case and not the second.

"Talk to a tax professional about the order you take money out of your accounts. The actual sequence can matter as much as the amount."

Investors and planners use this in a few ways. The old rule of thumb is taxable first, then tax-deferred, then Roth, which lets the tax-sheltered accounts keep compounding. A more tax-aware approach blends sources every year. You fill up the low tax brackets with IRA withdrawals or Roth conversions, which move money from a traditional IRA to a Roth and are taxed in the year you do them. Then you cover the rest of your spending from Roth or taxable money. The goal is a smooth income line across the decades, avoiding both a tax-free year followed by a huge one and the forced required minimum distributions (RMDs) that begin in your 70s. The practical signals are your bracket this year, your distance from the provisional income lines, and your distance from the Medicare surcharge cliffs.

The biggest gotcha is the tax torpedo. Inside the zone where Social Security is becoming taxable, each extra dollar of IRA income is taxed itself and also drags more benefits into taxable income. In the 85% tier, an extra $1,000 withdrawal adds $1,850 to taxable income. At a 12% bracket the effective rate on that dollar is roughly 22%, nearly double the bracket on paper. The video's point about 1983 explains why this keeps spreading. The $25,000 and $32,000 thresholds were set in 1983, and the $34,000 and $44,000 thresholds in 1993. None of them have ever been adjusted for inflation. Every COLA raises provisional income while the lines stay put, so more retirees cross them without any new law being passed.

The second cliff is IRMAA, the Medicare income-related monthly adjustment amount. It is a surcharge on Part B and drug coverage for higher earners, and it is based on your income from two years earlier. The video puts the starting line above $109,000 for a single person and $218,000 for a couple. Cross it and the standard Part B premium jumps to roughly $284 a month, plus a drug plan surcharge. (The transcript garbles the dollar figures. The standard 2026 Part B premium is about $203, not $22.90, so check the official figures.) IRMAA works in steps, so one dollar over a line can trigger the whole tier. A large IRA withdrawal, a house sale or a Roth conversion in 2026 can raise your Medicare bill in 2028, so plan with the two-year calendar in mind.

Sequencing can also go wrong. Pushing everything into a low-bracket year can backfire if tax law changes, or if a surprise such as a big medical bill, a spouse's death or a forced RMD wipes out the plan. A surviving spouse files as a single person, with narrower brackets and lower thresholds, so income that was comfortable for a couple can land in a high bracket. Selling investments in a down market just to hit a tax target can lock in losses. Roth conversions need cash outside the IRA to pay the tax, or you lose some of the benefit. The 2025 law added a temporary deduction of up to $6,000 per person for ages 65 and older, for 2025 through 2028. It phases out at higher incomes and does not change the Social Security taxation formula. A plan built on a temporary rule can also fail when the rule ends.

Sequencing ties together most of the video's themes. The COLA raises your benefit every year. That pushes provisional income up against thresholds that never move, and it also raises the amount that can be taxed. Medicare premiums come out of the check before you see it, and IRMAA looks back two years at your income. The three hands in the video, Medicare, the tax code and an inflation gauge that doesn't match your spending, all act on the same net number. Withdrawal order is one of the few ways to change the outcome. It also connects to the 2034 trust fund date in the report cited by the video, because if benefits are cut you will lean harder on your own accounts, and the order you draw them down will matter more.
