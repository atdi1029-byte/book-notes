Customer Concentration Risk

Customer concentration risk is the danger that a large share of a company's sales comes from a small number of buyers. If one customer, or a handful, accounts for a big slice of revenue, then that company's fortunes depend on decisions it doesn't control. A bakery that sells to thousands of walk-in shoppers loses almost nothing when one person stops coming. A bakery that sells half its bread to a single grocery chain is in a different situation. When the chain renegotiates, switches suppliers, or goes under, the bakery's revenue can fall by half in a quarter. The business may be excellent, but its income rests on a few relationships. Customer concentration risk measures how fragile that arrangement is.

It matters because a stock price is a bet on future profits, and concentrated revenue makes those profits harder to forecast. Investors pay for earnings they believe will keep arriving. If a single buyer can cut an order, demand a discount, or build the product in-house, the future cash flows are less certain, and the market tends to price that uncertainty into the stock. The effect reaches beyond one company. Suppliers, employees, local economies, and index-fund holders are all tied to the same few buyers. Concentration also changes the balance of power. A seller with a few giant customers has little leverage in negotiations, and its margins can be squeezed. A seller with thousands of small customers can set its terms.

The mechanism is simple arithmetic plus a feedback loop. Start with the share: if one customer is 16% of revenue, then a full loss of that customer cuts sales by 16%. Profit usually falls much more than sales, because many costs are fixed. Factories, engineers, and leases don't shrink when an order disappears. A company with a 40% operating margin that loses 16% of revenue, with costs unchanged, sees profit fall by roughly 27%, not 16%. This is operating leverage. The loop then continues: analysts cut estimates, the stock falls, and the company may have to cut spending on growth, which can weaken its position further. Concentration turns one decision by one buyer into a company-wide event.

Nvidia gives a clear and current example. In the quarter the video discussed, which ended July 26th, Nvidia reported revenue of $96.2 billion, up from $46.7 billion a year earlier. Data center sales were $89 billion of that total, more than 92%. A single customer accounted for 16% of all revenue. Those are really two layers of concentration. One is product concentration, where one business line carries almost everything. The other is customer concentration, where one buyer is a meaningful piece of that line. The host summed it up:

"That's a business with one engine, a wonderful engine, but only one."

Do the math on that 16%. It is about $15.4 billion of quarterly revenue, from one buyer, in one quarter. That buyer is almost certainly a very large technology company building out AI infrastructure. Its spending depends on its own plans, its own investors, and its own view of how much computing power it needs. It might also design its own chips, which would reduce what it buys from Nvidia. Nvidia can't control any of that. The same filing also showed a customer map by headquarters (United States, Taiwan, China, and "other"), and the video pointed out that there was no Singapore line to inspect, even as Washington asked questions about sales routed through Singapore. When the buyer mix can't be fully measured, investors tend to discount the stock until they can measure it.

Investors use concentration as a screening and sizing tool. The first place to look is the annual and quarterly filings. U.S. public companies generally must disclose customers that represent 10% or more of revenue, usually in the notes to the financial statements or the risk factors. Investors scan for those disclosures, then ask practical questions. How long are the contracts? Does the customer have alternatives? Has the share been rising or falling? How healthy is the customer's own business? A 20% customer that has signed a multi-year commitment and has no easy substitute is a different risk from a 20% customer who can switch suppliers next quarter. Many investors also size positions smaller in concentrated companies, or demand a lower price, because the range of outcomes is wider.

There are several nuances worth getting right. First, concentration is not automatically bad. Many excellent businesses, such as defense contractors selling to a government or suppliers tied to a dominant manufacturer, are concentrated and highly profitable for decades. A big customer can also be a sign of quality, since sophisticated buyers don't spend billions on weak products. Second, the headline percentage understates the problem when customers are linked. Five buyers at 8% each look diversified, but if all five are tech giants spending from the same AI budget and responding to the same investor pressure, they behave like one customer. Third, concentration can hide during good times. When orders are booming, profits are huge and the P/E ratio looks tiny, which is the trap the video described:

"When orders are booming, profits are huge, and the PE looks tiny. Then the orders slow, the profits shrink, and that bargain price tag turns out to have been full price all along."

When concentration breaks, it breaks fast and in steps. A customer announces it will use a competitor, or insource production, or simply "digest" the capacity it has already bought. The supplier's next guidance comes in below expectations. Analysts cut forecasts, which raises the P/E on the new, lower earnings, and the stock drops again. If the customer is also in financial trouble, the supplier may be stuck with unpaid invoices on top of lost orders. History has many cases. At the turn of the century, the host described watching telecom equipment makers whose forecasts looked terrific right up until their customers stopped placing orders. The earlier numbers weren't fake. The business simply depended on a few buyers who all pulled back at the same moment. The host was careful to note that this is a lesson about forecasts, not an accusation against any one company.

Concentration connects to several other ideas in this guide. It is a close cousin of position sizing, because the same logic applies to your own portfolio: if one holding dominates, you carry its risk the way a supplier carries its top customer's. The video made the point that Nvidia is roughly 7% of the S&P 500, so about $7 of every $100 in a plain index fund rides on one company, and that company in turn leans on a few buyers. That is concentration stacked on concentration. It also links to the price-to-earnings ratio: a low P/E can reflect the market's doubt about the bottom half of the fraction, the expected earnings, because those earnings depend on a few customers. The practical habit is to read the concentration note in the filing before deciding that a cheap stock is a bargain.
