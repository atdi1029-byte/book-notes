Average Cost Basis and Paper Loss

Your cost basis is what you paid for an investment. If you buy one share for $50, your basis is $50. Most people don't buy everything at one price, though. They buy over months or years as prices move. The average cost basis blends all those purchases into one number: total dollars spent divided by total units owned. A paper loss (or unrealized loss) is the gap that opens when the current market price falls below that average. It is called "paper" because nothing has happened yet except a change in the quoted value of what you hold. You haven't sold, and no cash has left your account. The loss exists on a statement until you make it real by selling.

This matters because investors, journalists, and even professionals constantly confuse a reference number with a trigger. Average cost tells you where you stand relative to your own history. It does not tell you what anyone will force you to do. That confusion drives bad decisions. Retail investors panic-sell the moment they go "underwater." Commentators invent doom stories around a price line that has no legal or contractual power. In the video, a $75,451 average cost became the centerpiece of a "margin trap" narrative, even though the filings contained nothing that tied a forced sale to that number. Knowing what a number can and cannot cause is half of analysis.

Mechanically, the calculation is simple. Suppose you buy 1 coin at $100,000 and later 3 coins at $60,000. You spent $280,000 for 4 coins, so your average cost is $70,000. Now the market price moves. At $77,000, each coin carries an unrealized gain of $7,000, or 10% above basis. At $60,000, you carry an unrealized loss of $10,000 per coin, about 14%. Every new purchase shifts the average toward the price you just paid, and the more you already own, the less one purchase moves it. Crucially, the average changes only when you buy, never when the market drifts. A sale doesn't change the average either; it simply locks in a gain or loss on the units sold.

Here is the video's real-world case. Strategy, formerly MicroStrategy, held 849,327 Bitcoin at an average cost of $75,451 per coin. With Bitcoin around $77,000, the company sat roughly 2% above its basis. The host calculated that thin "cushion" and noted how tempting it was to treat it as a cliff edge. But he stated plainly what happens if Bitcoin dips under that line:

"If Bitcoin dips below 75,451, the pile shows a paper loss against what it cost. That is embarrassing. It's not a liquidation. Those are two different things."

Why is this not a trigger? Because a trigger needs a contract. A margin call happens when you borrow against an asset and the lender requires more collateral as the value falls. The lender sets a threshold, often tied to a loan-to-value ratio, and the borrower must add money or the lender sells. Nothing in that structure refers to what the borrower originally paid. The lender cares about today's value against the loan balance, not your purchase history. In Strategy's case, the host pointed out that the main debt is convertible bonds rather than a brokerage margin loan, and the company's offering documents say no assets are pledged to secure its STRC preferred stock. With no collateral clause, there is no mechanism by which a price below $75,451 forces anyone's hand.

Investors use average cost as a context tool and a discipline tool. It helps you judge how much cushion you have, whether adding more will meaningfully lower your average, and what your tax picture looks like if you sell. Professionals also use it as a sanity check on behavior: a company that sells assets well below its average cost, as Strategy did when it sold coins at averages of $59,663 and $62,780, is revealing something about its cash needs. Those sales were roughly 17% to 21% below its basis. Ordinary long-term holders don't normally do that, so the sales are a signal that dividends, not conviction, were driving the decision.

The gotchas start with the idea that your cost basis is a support level. Markets don't know or care what you paid, so there is no reason price should bounce at your average. Another trap is anchoring: refusing to sell a loser "until I get back to even" while ignoring better uses of the money. The reverse mistake also happens, where a gain above basis feels like safety. Strategy shows this clearly. In the quarter it booked a $19.5 billion profit, driven by a $20.91 billion gain on digital assets under mark-to-market accounting, yet no coin was sold and no cash arrived. That profit could not pay a single dividend. A paper gain is as unspendable as a paper loss is harmless.

When does a paper loss turn real? There are three paths. The first is voluntary or forced selling: you sell below basis and realize the loss. The second is leverage. If you borrowed against the position, a falling price can trigger a margin call, and then the loss becomes real regardless of your preferences. The third is a cash need that forces you to sell at a bad time, which is the video's actual warning. Strategy owes about $1.48 billion a year in dividends and interest, payable in dollars, so a pile of Bitcoin in storage is no help. Selling coins near the lows converted paper exposure into realized outcomes, and the real trap was the recurring bill, not the average cost line.

This concept connects to several others. Mark-to-market accounting turns price swings into reported profit or loss each quarter, which is why Strategy's income statement can swing by billions without any cash moving. Leverage and collateral determine whether a paper loss can become a forced sale, and the video's central lesson is to read the filings to see whether a real covenant exists. Liquidity and cash reserves matter too: Strategy holds a $4.79 billion reserve against a $1.48 billion annual bill, more than three years of cover. Finally, mNAV and dilution explain why the company can't easily raise new money. When you can discuss all of these together, you can tell a scary number from a dangerous one.
