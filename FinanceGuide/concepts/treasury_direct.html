TreasuryDirect

TreasuryDirect is the official website, run by the U.S. Department of the Treasury's Bureau of the Fiscal Service, where ordinary people can buy U.S. government debt directly from the government. There is no broker, no bank, and no fund in between. You open an account, link a bank account for funding, and buy Treasury securities: Treasury bills (maturities of 4 weeks to 52 weeks), notes, bonds, TIPS, floating-rate notes, and Series I savings bonds. When you buy, the debt is recorded in your name in the Treasury's own books. You are lending money to the United States, and the United States owes you the money back with interest.

It matters because of a question most people never ask: when you hold cash, who actually owes you that money? In a bank account, the answer is the bank. Your balance is a claim on an institution that has lent your money out and invested it in securities. FDIC insurance covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, but beyond that, or during a period of doubt about a bank, you are exposed to the institution. TreasuryDirect removes that layer. The video puts it plainly, and this is the whole idea of the chapter:

"When you hold a one-month or a three-month US Treasury bill directly, you have zero bank counterparty risk. You're lending to the United States sovereign government directly, earning over 4% right now instead of serving as unsecured collateral for a trapped regional lender."

Here is the mechanism step by step. First, you register at treasurydirect.gov, providing your Social Security number and a linked checking or savings account, and set up credentials. Second, you choose a security and a purchase amount, in increments of $100, with a minimum of $100. Third, you choose to buy at auction. T-bills are sold at a discount to their face value: you might pay $9,900 for a bill that pays $10,000 at maturity, and the $100 difference is your interest. Fourth, the purchase price is pulled from your linked bank account when the security is issued. Fifth, at maturity, the face value is deposited back into that same bank account, or, if you selected reinvestment, rolled into a new bill automatically.

Pricing works through auctions. The Treasury holds regular auctions for each bill maturity, typically weekly for 4-, 8-, 13-, 17-, 26-, and 52-week bills. As an individual, you place a non-competitive bid, meaning you agree to accept whatever rate the auction sets and are guaranteed to get your full amount filled. Large institutions submit competitive bids naming a specific yield, and their bids determine the rate everyone gets. This is why you cannot be picked off: you receive the same market-clearing yield that professional dealers set. The discount rate is often quoted differently from the investment yield, so compare the "investment rate" or the effective annualized yield when you judge what you earn.

Take the video's context as the worked example. The presenter describes a banking system holding an alleged $325.1 billion in unrealized losses on securities (per the FDIC quarterly banking profile), with regional banks paying 0.2% to 0.4% on consumer checking while Treasury bills yield more than 4% and the 10-year yield sits near 5.27%. Suppose you hold $50,000 of idle cash at a regional bank paying 0.3%. That earns about $150 a year. Put that same $50,000 in 26-week bills at 4%, and you earn roughly $2,000 a year, with the principal owed by Washington instead of by a bank the presenter characterizes as balance-sheet constrained. That gap, about $1,850, is exactly why the video says sophisticated depositors are sweeping out of banks.

Investors use TreasuryDirect in a few practical ways. The most common is as a home for the emergency fund or any cash beyond the FDIC limit, through a ladder: buy bills maturing every four weeks, so that something matures regularly, giving liquidity without needing to sell. Business owners and people who have sold a house or received an inheritance use it to park six-figure sums safely while deciding what to do. Some use rolling 4-week bills as a de facto savings account. Traders also treat bill yields as the "risk-free rate," the baseline against which every other return is judged. If a stock, bond, or bank CD cannot beat a Treasury bill by a meaningful margin, the extra risk is not paying you.

There are nuances and gotchas worth knowing. First, TreasuryDirect is not FDIC insured, and it does not need to be, because the security is the government's own obligation, backed by its taxing power. Second, the video's fear of "zero risk" is really zero credit risk, not zero all risk: if you sell a longer-dated Treasury before maturity, its price can fall when rates rise, which is the same mechanism the video says produced the banks' unrealized losses. Hold a bill to maturity and you get exactly what you paid for. Third, interest is exempt from state and local income tax but is subject to federal income tax. Fourth, the video says "money market funds" and "T-bill funds" are alternatives, and they are, but they add a fund and a custodian back into the chain.

The site itself has practical friction. The interface is dated and can feel clunky. You can only fund purchases from and pay out to one linked bank account, and changing it takes effort. Accounts have been locked for security reasons, and recovering access can take weeks of paperwork, so keep your credentials safe and record who should inherit the account. Bills bought at TreasuryDirect can be sold before maturity only by transferring them out to a broker, which takes time, so it is best for money you plan to hold to maturity. If you want easy resale, a brokerage account (Fidelity, Schwab) is more flexible, and holds bills through the broker's custody, which is safe but slightly different from direct registration.

What happens when things fail? The government paying its debt is the bedrock assumption of the global financial system, and a true default would be a catastrophe well beyond your account. The more realistic risks are political and operational. Debt-ceiling standoffs, like those in 2011 and 2023, have raised fears that certain bills maturing near the "X-date" could be delayed, and bills around those dates have traded at slightly higher yields. Inflation is another risk: a 4% yield with 3.5% inflation leaves you barely ahead. And reinvestment risk means that if rates fall, your next bill will pay less. Finally, you may lose out on returns if the bank crisis the video describes never arrives.

TreasuryDirect connects to several other concepts in this video. It is the practical counter to the held-to-maturity (HTM) accounting issue: banks can carry bonds at full value on the books, while you hold a security whose value is exactly what the government will repay. It relates to deposit flight, the process in which depositors move money from low-paying banks to bills and money market funds, which forces banks to borrow at 4% or more from the Federal Home Loan Banks. It also connects to FDIC insurance limits, reciprocal deposit networks like IntraFi, and the video's three-step defense playbook. The lesson is not that every bank will fail; it is that knowing who owes you your money is a habit worth having.
