SaaS Per-Seat Pricing Model

The SaaS per-seat pricing model (where SaaS stands for Software as a Service) is a revenue structure in which a software company charges customers a recurring fee — monthly or annually — for each individual user, or "seat," who accesses the platform. Instead of selling a product outright, the software vendor essentially rents access to its tool. A company with 500 employees that needs project management software pays for 500 licenses. A company with 5,000 employees pays for 5,000. The more people using the software, the more the vendor earns. This model replaced the old era of one-time software purchases and CD-ROMs, creating something far more valuable to Wall Street: predictable, recurring revenue that grows automatically as the customer's workforce grows.

Per-seat pricing matters enormously to financial markets because it transforms a software company's revenue into something that behaves like a utility bill or a lease payment — steady, contractual, and highly predictable. This predictability is not just a nice feature; it is the entire foundation upon which a decade of software valuations, venture capital bets, leveraged buyouts, and private credit loans were built. When lenders and investors can model a company's future cash flows with near-actuarial confidence, they are willing to extend more credit and at lower interest rates. The software industry's explosive growth from 2015 to 2023 was not purely a product story — it was equally a financial architecture story, built on the assumption that per-seat revenue would grow in lockstep with the white-collar workforce forever.

Mechanically, the model works like this: a vendor signs an enterprise contract with a customer, typically for one to three years. The contract specifies a per-seat rate — say, $50 per user per month — and a minimum seat count. As the customer hires new employees, they provision more seats and the monthly invoice grows automatically. At renewal time, the vendor can negotiate a price increase per seat, expanding revenue even without adding new customers. This creates two growth levers: adding new customers (net new logos) and expanding revenue within existing customers (net revenue retention or NRR). A company with strong NRR — say, 120% — is growing 20% annually from its existing customer base alone, before selling to a single new account. Wall Street learned to prize NRR above almost any other SaaS metric.

To understand how deeply this model was embedded into financial underwriting, consider what private credit lenders were actually doing between 2021 and 2024. They were extending loans — often at 7x EBITDA leverage or higher — to mid-market SaaS companies whose entire loan repayment capacity rested on per-seat subscription contracts. A lender modeling a workflow software company's ability to service a 12% floating-rate loan would build a spreadsheet projecting seat counts multiplying alongside enterprise headcount. The underlying assumption, rarely made explicit but always present, was that human workers and software seats were permanently coupled. One employee, one seat, forever. This is what the video's narrator means when he says lenders "sold these deals as bulletproof annuities" — the seat count felt as reliable as a government bond coupon.

The real-world rupture of this assumption is best illustrated by HubSpot's second-quarter 2024 earnings, which the video dissects as a case study. HubSpot reported revenue of $911.7 million — up 20% year over year. Customer count jumped 14%, reaching 306,000 accounts. Those are genuinely strong numbers. But the signal hiding inside them is alarming: average subscription revenue per customer rose only 4%, to approximately $11,800. HubSpot was acquiring customers 3.5 times faster than it was growing revenue per customer. Management explicitly acknowledged that enterprise clients were demanding outcome-based pricing rather than per-head billing. This is not a struggling company — HubSpot is a healthy market leader. When even the winners are being forced to dismantle per-seat pricing, the model itself is under structural siege.

"If a healthy market leader is voluntarily dismantling per-seat pricing, what happens to a middle market software vendor leveraged at 7 times EBITDA that borrowed from a private credit person at 12% floating interest?"

For investors and traders, the per-seat model generates a set of specific signals worth tracking. The most important is the divergence between customer count growth and revenue per customer growth — exactly the gap HubSpot revealed. When those two metrics decouple, it signals that seat compression is occurring: existing customers are renewing but shedding seats, while new customers are signing smaller initial contracts. Investors should also watch dollar-based net revenue retention rates. An NRR falling below 100% means a company is losing revenue from existing customers even as it wins new ones — a treadmill problem. For credit investors specifically, the ratio of software payment-in-kind (PIK) loans rising within a portfolio is a forward indicator of cash flow stress, because PIK means borrowers cannot actually service their debt in cash.

The most dangerous misconception about per-seat SaaS is conflating revenue stability with business model durability. These are not the same thing. Revenue can look stable for several quarters even as the underlying driver of that revenue — seat counts — quietly erodes, because enterprise contracts have long durations and customers don't cancel immediately. They reduce seats at renewal. This lag creates a valuation trap: a company's trailing revenue looks healthy, its forward guidance sounds reasonable, and its loan covenants appear intact — right up until the renewal cycle rolls over and the seat reductions hit the income statement simultaneously. This is precisely why the Bank for International Settlements found that less than 1% of private credit software borrowers were technically behind on contracted payments, even as lenders were marking down 81% of their software portfolios. The cash hasn't stopped yet. The math has.

When the per-seat model breaks down at scale, the damage propagates far beyond individual software vendors. The companies that borrowed against per-seat revenue streams cannot simply pivot their pricing model mid-loan. Switching from per-seat to outcome-based or consumption pricing requires repricing every enterprise contract, retraining the sales force, rebuilding financial models, and often accepting a near-term revenue reduction as the transition occurs. A company carrying 7x EBITDA leverage at a 12% floating rate has no margin for a revenue dip of any size. It cannot refinance because lenders are no longer willing to underwrite per-seat assumptions. It cannot raise equity easily because public markets are repricing the entire category. This is how a pricing model disruption becomes a credit event — not through outright fraud or mismanagement, but through the quiet mechanical failure of an assumption that was never tested because it had never needed to be.

The per-seat model connects directly to several broader financial concepts worth understanding. Multiple expansion and compression in public software equities followed per-seat reliability — when the model was trusted, SaaS companies traded at 15-20x revenue; as AI disrupts seat counts, those multiples compress. Private credit underwriting standards, which were supposed to price risk into loan spreads, failed to include even a single basis point of AI disruption premium, according to BIS research — a structural failure in credit pricing. And the Business Development Company (BDC) structure, which pools these private loans and sells shares to retail investors seeking high dividend yields, is the ultimate transmission mechanism through which a per-seat pricing breakdown in enterprise software becomes a risk sitting inside an ordinary American's retirement account.
