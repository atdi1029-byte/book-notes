Comparable Store Sales (Same-Store Sales)

Comparable store sales — also called same-store sales or simply comp sales — measure the revenue growth (or decline) of a retail company's existing store locations over a given period, compared to the same period a year earlier. The key word is existing. Only stores that were open during both time periods count. A brand-new store that opened six months ago doesn't make the cut. This filter is what gives the metric its power: it strips away the flattering noise of expansion and asks a harder, more honest question — are the customers who already know you actually coming back and spending more? That distinction separates real demand growth from geographic growth, and it's one of the most important questions you can ask about any retailer.

The reason comp sales matter so much is that they function as a health gauge for underlying demand. A company can always inflate its total revenue by opening more locations — it just needs capital and a willing landlord. But if those new stores are cannibalizing existing ones, or if the brand has simply lost its pull with customers, total revenue growth becomes a mirage. Investors and analysts focus on comp sales because they reveal whether a retailer's relationship with its customers is strengthening or eroding. Rising comps signal that the same customers are buying more, or that foot traffic is increasing at existing locations — both signs of genuine brand strength. Falling comps, even alongside rising total revenue, are a serious warning that growth is borrowed, not earned.

Mechanically, the calculation is straightforward. A company identifies every store that was open for the full comparable period — typically a fiscal quarter or full year — in both the current and prior year. It then totals the revenue from that fixed pool of stores for each period and calculates the percentage change. If that pool of stores brought in $1 billion last year and $910 million this year, comp sales are down 9%. New store openings, store closures, and relocations are excluded from the calculation. Some companies also adjust for calendar differences or currency fluctuations in international markets. The resulting number is a clean, apples-to-apples read on whether the existing business is accelerating or decelerating, independent of the expansion strategy.

The Lululemon example from the video makes this concrete. In their second quarter of fiscal 2026, Lululemon reported total revenue of $2.44 billion — down 4% year-over-year. But the comp sales figure told a sharper story: down 9% overall, and down a punishing 12% in the Americas. That 12% figure means Lululemon's existing stores in its home market were generating dramatically less revenue than those same stores did the prior year. The company wasn't just growing slowly — its core customer base was pulling back. And this happened even as Lululemon continued opening new locations globally. Without the comp sales lens, an investor looking only at total revenue might underestimate the severity of the problem. With it, the picture is unmistakable.

"Comparable sales compares the same amount of stores as you had last year. So you can't just grow by adding stores. And that's why we watch that carefully, especially with bricks and mortar retailers."

For traders and investors, comp sales are an early-warning system that often precedes broader financial deterioration. Experienced analysts watch for comp sales deceleration — when the year-over-year growth rate starts slowing quarter after quarter — as a signal to scrutinize the investment thesis. Negative comps at a major retailer often trigger sharp stock selloffs because the market re-rates the business: instead of pricing in stable or growing earnings, it begins pricing in the cost of fixing the brand, running promotions, and potentially closing underperforming stores. Conversely, consistently positive comp sales — especially accelerating ones — can sustain premium valuations because they signal the company is taking market share or deepening customer loyalty without needing to spend aggressively on expansion.

The most important nuance — and the most common misconception — is conflating adjusted earnings with operational health. The Lululemon quarter illustrated this perfectly. Adjusted earnings per share came in at $2.92, which appeared to crush the $1.82 consensus estimate. Impressive on the surface. But $0.86 of that beat came from a tariff refund, a one-time government credit that had nothing to do with selling yoga pants. Strip that out and EPS was roughly $2.06 — barely ahead of estimates. The lesson: headline earnings figures can be dressed up with accounting adjustments, tax credits, or one-time items. Comp sales, by contrast, are grounded in physical reality — how many people walked into how many stores and spent how much money. It is much harder to manufacture that number.

Comp sales can break down as a signal in a few specific edge cases worth knowing. First, when a company undergoes significant store remodels or relocations, those stores may be temporarily excluded from the comp base, which can inflate the reported figure. Second, in periods of heavy promotional activity, comps can look healthy in volume terms while gross margins quietly collapse — customers are buying, but only because of deep discounts. Third, the shift to e-commerce complicates the picture for omnichannel retailers: if a brand closes physical stores but online revenue surges, the comp calculation may not capture the full consumer relationship. Companies handle this differently, which is why reading the footnotes on how a company defines its comp base is essential before drawing conclusions.

Comp sales connect directly to several other core concepts in retail and value investing. They feed into operating leverage analysis: when comps are positive, fixed store costs are spread over more revenue, expanding margins. When comps are negative, those same fixed costs crush margins on a smaller revenue base — which is why a 9% comp decline can translate into a much larger earnings decline. Comp sales also tie to the concept of brand moat. A company with a durable moat — genuine pricing power, customer loyalty, product differentiation — will tend to sustain positive comps across economic cycles. When comps turn negative, it often signals the moat is narrowing. As the video argued of Lululemon, a company that misses on comp sales has a brand problem, not an accounting one — and you cannot refund your way out of a brand problem.
