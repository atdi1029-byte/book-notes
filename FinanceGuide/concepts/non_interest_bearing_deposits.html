Non-Interest-Bearing Deposits (Core Deposits)

A non-interest-bearing deposit is money a customer keeps at a bank that earns no interest. The usual examples are business checking accounts, payroll accounts, escrow balances, and the everyday checking balances of households. Banks call the stable part of their deposit base core deposits. These are balances that tend to stay put through good times and bad, because customers use them to run their lives and companies rather than to chase yield. From the customer's side, the account is a convenience. From the bank's side, it is a loan the customer makes for free. The video frames it that way: "Can you imagine if somebody gave you interest-free loans? That's what that really was."

These deposits matter because they are the raw material of bank profit. A bank earns money on the gap between what it earns on assets (loans and securities) and what it pays for funding (mostly deposits). That gap, divided by earning assets, is the net interest margin, or NIM. Every dollar funded at 0% instead of 4% widens it. This is why the video says experienced bank investors ignore headline earnings per share and look at two things: net interest margin and the cost of deposits. Cheap deposits also support the real economy. Funding that costs little lets banks offer loans, credit lines, and equipment leases to small businesses and households at prices they can afford.

The mechanism is simple. A company deposits $10 million in operating cash. The bank pays nothing on it and puts the money to work. Some goes into a 30-year mortgage at 6%, some into a commercial real estate loan, some into Treasury securities, and some stays in reserve. The bank keeps the whole spread, not just a slice of it. The video calls the bank a "leverage spread machine" that borrows "at as close to zero percent as possible" and lends "at four, five, six, or even 7%." The key feature is a mismatch in timing. The deposit can leave tomorrow, but the loans it funded are locked in for years. As long as the deposits stay, the mismatch is invisible and very profitable.

Deposits do not leave a bank at random. They leave when the customer's alternatives are good enough to justify the effort. When the Federal Reserve held rates near zero, a checking account paying 0% cost depositors nothing, since a Treasury bill paid almost the same. Once short-term rates rose, the cost of holding idle cash became real. The video points to Treasury bills and institutional money market funds paying over 4.3%, with SOFR (the Secured Overnight Financing Rate) at 3.87%. Its examples of who moves are "affluent households and commercial corporate treasuries." A chief financial officer leaving $10 million idle at 0% has a hard time justifying it. The usual remedy is an automated sweep account, which moves surplus cash every afternoon into a money market fund, repo facility, or Treasury bills.

"Well, no chief financial officer in America can legally or fiduciarily justify leaving $10 million sitting idle in a checking account."

Here is how the video applies this to Bank of America's third-quarter report. The bank posted $6.9 billion in net income, down 12% from a year earlier. A $1.2 billion regulatory and compliance charge was one cause, but the video argues credit desks cared more about the deposit runoff, and the shares fell nearly 5% in a single session. The walk-through goes like this. Suppose a bank loses $50 million of core deposits. It cannot call in a 30-year mortgage or a 10-year commercial real estate note on 24 hours' notice. So it replaces the money with wholesale funding: brokered certificates of deposit, promotional CD rates, or Federal Home Loan Bank advances, at "4 and even 5%." Old loans yielding 3-4% are now funded at costs well above that, and the margin compresses.

Investors turn this into signals. The first is the mix of deposits: what share of total deposits is non-interest-bearing, and whether that share is rising or falling. The second is the deposit beta, which measures how much of each rate increase the bank has to pass on to depositors. A higher beta means less of the cheap-funding advantage survives. The third is reliance on brokered CDs and FHLB advances. The video suggests checking "deposit stability ratios" at regional banks and watching SOFR, wholesale CD yields, and bank credit default swaps. A bank that leans heavily on expensive wholesale money has a thinner cushion for its dividend. Individuals can use the same logic in reverse, since sitting in a sub-1% account while the bank borrows at 4.5% means subsidizing its margin.

There are nuances the video skips over. Deposits are not literally free. The bank pays for branches, staff, technology, FDIC insurance assessments, and the payment services that business customers often receive in place of interest. Many commercial accounts earn "earnings credits" that offset fees. Not every non-interest-bearing dollar is equally sticky either. Operating cash that a business needs every day is much stickier than excess balances the treasurer keeps out of inertia. The video's description of decades of "4-7%" lending is also a simplification, because loan yields were far lower for much of the 2010s. It also contrasts SOFR at 3.87% with Treasury bills "over 4%," and the exact rates change constantly. The takeaway is the direction of the gap, not those particular figures.

When core deposits drain fast, the damage spreads in several ways. The most direct is margin compression: funding costs jump while the yield on existing fixed-rate loans and bonds is stuck. The video describes a cost of funds rising about 200 basis points against portfolios locked in at 3-4%. Management then protects itself by tightening credit, which means fewer revolving lines for mid-size firms, higher deposit-balance requirements for equipment leases, stricter credit-score cutoffs, and extra cash collateral on refinancings. The video says banks also stop buying Treasuries when deposits leave. The worst case is a run, as in March 2023 when Silicon Valley Bank lost deposits in days and had to sell bonds at a loss. Losses on long-dated holdings, a heavy share of uninsured deposits, and a nervous customer base can turn runoff into failure.

This concept connects to several others in the video. Net interest margin is the profit measure it feeds. Wholesale funding, brokered CDs, and FHLB advances are what banks turn to when it fails. Money market funds and T-bills are where the money goes, and the sweep accounts are the pipeline between the two. SOFR and the overnight-to-10-year Treasury spread describe the yield environment that makes leaving cash idle costly. It also links to credit conditions in the wider economy: when funding gets pricier, lending standards tighten, and small and mid-size firms feel it first. And it ties to Treasury demand, since banks that lose deposits have less cash to park in government paper at auctions.

The practical conclusion is that a bank's deposit mix says more about its future earnings than a single quarter's EPS. If you own bank stocks or an ETF like XLF, ask where the bank gets its funding, how fast cheap balances are shrinking, and what it pays to replace them. If you are a depositor, understand that your idle checking balance is the "interest-free loan" the whole business model depends on. Keeping only what you need for bills in checking, and moving the rest to Treasury bills or a government money market fund, earns you the going rate. The video's own summary fits: headline profits are a glamour metric, but the deposit plumbing is the real engine.
