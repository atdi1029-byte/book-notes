Swap Spread

A swap spread is the gap between two interest rates that are supposed to describe the same thing: the cost of borrowing money for a fixed period. One is the fixed rate on an interest rate swap, a contract where two parties trade a fixed payment for a floating one. The other is the yield on a US Treasury bond of the same maturity. If the 10-year swap rate is 4.20% and the 10-year Treasury yields 4.40%, the swap spread is minus 20 basis points. A basis point is one hundredth of a percent. The number tells you how the market prices bank-style, derivative-based funding against the government's own borrowing.

To read a swap spread you need to know what each side represents. A Treasury yield is what the US government pays to borrow. The market treats it as the benchmark "risk-free" rate, backed by taxing power and a printing press. The swap rate is the fixed rate you would accept to receive in exchange for paying a floating overnight rate, today the SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate). Historically, swap rates sat above Treasury yields, so spreads were positive. That made intuitive sense, since swaps carried bank credit risk. For roughly a decade now, long-maturity spreads have been negative, which looks backwards and is the root of much of the trading activity.

Why should anyone outside a trading desk care? Because the swap spread is a pressure gauge on the plumbing of the bond market. Treasuries are the collateral and pricing anchor for mortgages, corporate bonds, bank loans, and retirement portfolios. When the spread moves sharply, it signals that something is straining: dealers' balance sheets, the supply of Treasuries, or demand from leveraged funds. In the video, the argument is that hedge fund leverage did not vanish when the Treasury basis trade shrank. It moved into swap spread trades, which the BIS said tripled from about $100 billion to $300 billion in 12 months. A crowded trade in a pipe that carries your mortgage rate is worth understanding.

Here is the mechanism step by step. In a swap, Party A pays a fixed rate and receives floating SOFR. Party B does the opposite. No principal changes hands, only the net difference in interest payments, which is why swaps are called off-balance-sheet derivatives. The fixed rate is set so the contract has zero value on day one. That rate is the swap rate. Now compare it with the Treasury yield of the same maturity. The difference is the spread. The swap rate reflects expectations for overnight rates over the life of the contract, plus a small premium. The Treasury yield reflects those same expectations, plus supply and demand for the bonds themselves. When Treasuries are plentiful or dealers cannot hold them cheaply, Treasury yields rise relative to swap rates and the spread falls more negative.

Why would spreads turn negative at all? The key is balance sheet cost. To hold a Treasury, a dealer must fund it, usually in the repo market, and the bond sits on the dealer's balance sheet, where regulators require capital against it. A swap, by contrast, is a derivative that consumes far less balance sheet space. So the Treasury is "expensive to hold" and the swap is "cheap to hold," and the market demands extra yield on the bond to compensate. Add the enormous volume of Treasury issuance the government has needed to fund deficits, and dealers are asked to absorb more bonds than they have room for. The Treasury yield cheapens relative to the swap rate, and the spread goes negative.

Now the trade itself. A swap spread trade bets on how that gap will move. The most common version, often called "receiving the spread" or being long swap spreads, wagers that negative spreads will narrow back toward zero. The trader buys a Treasury, finances it in repo, and pays fixed on a swap of the same maturity. The Treasury pays a high yield. The swap costs a lower fixed rate. If the spread is minus 40 and the trader collects that gap as carry, they earn a small spread on a very large position. Because the margin per dollar is tiny, funds lever the position many times over to make it worthwhile. That is the leverage the video describes crossing from one trade to another.

The video's central claim is about rotation. It says the Treasury basis trade, where funds buy Treasuries and sell futures against them, was being unwound because it stopped paying. The Fed's financial stability report, as quoted, said that unwind was largely offset by hedge funds rotating into swap spread trades. The BIS then reported those positions went from $100 billion to $300 billion in a year. The speaker's line was:

"Leverage never disappears. It only mutates."

The speaker also pointed to calm funding markets as evidence that this was a quiet rotation and not a crisis. The Fed's standing repo facility took in zero. SOFR was 3.88%, flat with Fed funds. The reverse repo facility rose to $11.4 billion. His reading was a cash glut, not a collateral squeeze. He tied the new risk to the 10-year yield at 5.26% and a MOVE index above 100.

Traders use swap spreads in several practical ways. Hedge funds trade them for carry, as above. Banks and insurers use them to hedge: a pension fund that owns long Treasuries but has liabilities tied to swap rates can use swaps to match them. Macro investors read the spread as a signal. A spread that gets sharply more negative suggests Treasury supply is overwhelming dealer capacity. A spread that snaps toward zero can mean a regulatory easing or a big wave of forced buying. The video notes the enhanced supplementary leverage ratio loosened in April, which frees dealer balance sheets and, in theory, helps spreads narrow. That is exactly the move a long-spread trader is betting on.

Several nuances get lost in headlines. First, a swap spread is not a measure of bank credit risk any more than it once was. Since swaps moved to central clearing and SOFR replaced LIBOR, the credit component is mostly gone, so today's spread is almost entirely about balance sheet cost and Treasury supply. Second, a negative spread does not mean Treasuries are "riskier than banks." It means they are costly to hold and finance. Third, the BIS number is a snapshot of notional or estimated exposure, not a measure of losses at risk. Fourth, the video's claim of "zero visibility" is a stretch: dealers report, clearinghouses collect margin, and the BIS and Fed are clearly watching. The truthful version is that there is no public, regularly published positioning report like the CFTC's futures data.

What goes wrong when a swap spread trade breaks? The trade is a small-margin, high-leverage bet, so a modest adverse move creates large losses on paper. If Treasury yields jump faster than swap rates, the spread widens against the position, and the lenders financing the Treasury leg demand more collateral through higher haircuts or margin calls. The fund must sell to raise cash. Selling Treasuries pushes yields up further and spreads even wider, which triggers more margin calls. This is the same spiral seen in March 2020, when basis and swap spread positions unwound together and the Fed had to buy trillions in Treasuries. Because many funds hold the same position, exits become crowded, and the unwind can run well beyond what fundamentals justify.

The video draws a line from this plumbing to ordinary households. Dealers who warehouse mortgage-backed securities demand a wider cushion when volatility is high and swap spreads are unpredictable. The video said the gap between the 10-year Treasury and the 30-year mortgage stayed above 260 basis points, pinning mortgage rates near 7.4%. Treat that as the speaker's causal claim, not settled fact. Mortgage spreads reflect prepayment risk and supply too. Also treat the claim about bank credit as an argument: if a bank commits balance sheet to hedge fund swap trades, that capacity is not available elsewhere. That is plausible in principle, but the size of the real-world effect on credit card limits or small business loans is not proven by the transcript.

Swap spreads connect to a family of ideas in this guide. The Treasury basis trade uses the same ingredients, leveraged Treasury holdings financed in repo, with futures in place of swaps. Repo haircuts and margin determine how much leverage a fund can use and how quickly it is forced to sell. The supplementary leverage ratio explains why dealers ration balance sheet. The MOVE index measures bond market volatility, which raises the cost of financing every one of these trades. Finally, SOFR is the floating rate on the other side of the swap. If you can explain how those pieces link, you can talk about swap spreads confidently and separate real risks from alarm.

To sum up the practical takeaway: a swap spread is a simple subtraction, swap rate minus Treasury yield, that reveals how costly it is for the financial system to hold government debt. When it is deeply negative, it means balance sheets are tight and leveraged funds are being paid to provide liquidity. When many of them pile into the same position with borrowed money, a small shock can force simultaneous selling. The video's claim that $300 billion of leverage moved here is worth tracking, but the useful posture is measured. Watch the spread, watch funding markets, and remember that a quiet market today does not guarantee orderly exits tomorrow.
