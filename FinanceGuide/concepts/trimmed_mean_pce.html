Trimmed Mean PCE

Trimmed mean PCE is an inflation gauge published by the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas. It starts with the same raw material as the Fed's preferred inflation measure, the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index, which tracks what Americans pay for everything from groceries and rent to medical care and airfare. The Dallas Fed then does something the headline number doesn't: it lines up every category's price change for the month, throws away the most extreme movers at both ends, and averages whatever is left. The "trim" is the discarding, and the "mean" is the average of the survivors. The goal is to strip out the noise from wild one-off swings so that you see the underlying trend in prices rather than a few dramatic outliers.

It matters because the Fed sets interest rates based on where it believes inflation is truly heading, not on a single scary month. Interest rates, in turn, drive your mortgage rate, your credit card APR, your car loan, and the valuation of nearly every asset in your portfolio. If a spike in one category, like crude oil, makes headline inflation look worse than the underlying economy really is, the Fed can be tempted to overreact. If a temporary plunge makes things look tamer than they are, it might relax too soon. Trimmed mean PCE tries to answer a simple question: what is inflation doing across the broad middle of the economy, once we ignore the extremes?

Mechanically, the process has a few steps. First, the Dallas Fed takes the monthly price change for each of the roughly 180 or so detailed spending categories inside the PCE index. Second, it ranks them from the biggest price decline to the biggest price increase. Third, it weights each category by how much consumers actually spend on it, so housing counts for far more than, say, pet supplies. Fourth, it chops off a fixed share of that weighted distribution from each tail, historically around 24 percent of the weight from the low end and about 31 percent from the high end. Finally, it averages what remains and annualizes the result. The asymmetric trim exists because the Dallas Fed's research found that cutting more from the high side tracks underlying inflation better.

The video offers a vivid snapshot. Mark Moss lists a whole dashboard of alternative inflation trackers (Truflation, the Cleveland Fed's nowcast, the Atlanta Fed's sticky-price CPI, breakevens) and then points to the Dallas Fed's version:

"Through June, that measure sat at just 2.2% over the trailing 12 months. Guys, is that or is that not the tamest read of the bunch?"

Compare that 2.2% to the other numbers in the same discussion. Headline CPI was running around 3.5% (with July expected near 3.4%), the Atlanta Fed's sticky-price CPI sat at 2.8% year over year, and the 10-year TIPS breakeven was 2.29%. Trimmed mean PCE came in below all of them except, roughly, the bond market's forward-looking expectation. The gap between 3.5% headline and 2.2% trimmed tells you something specific: a meaningful chunk of the headline reading was coming from outliers, most plausibly energy, rather than from broad-based price pressure.

Traders and investors use it as a cross-check on whether the headline number is telling the truth about the trend. If headline CPI is hot but trimmed mean PCE is calm, the market narrative becomes "this is a supply shock, not an overheating economy," and the case for rate hikes weakens. If both are rising together, the inflation is broad, and the odds of a hawkish Fed go up. Bond traders watch the spread between trimmed mean and headline to judge whether the Fed will "look through" a shock. Equity investors use it to gauge the risk of multiple compression: persistent broad inflation means higher discount rates and pressure on growth stocks, while narrow inflation is far more forgivable.

Now the gotchas. First, trimming is not the same as core inflation. Core PCE removes food and energy every time, by rule, whether or not they are misbehaving. Trimmed mean removes whatever is extreme this month, which could be used cars, airfares, or egg prices, and it can change from month to month. Second, a low reading is not proof that inflation is gone. If prices rise sharply and persistently in a few big categories, trimming hides real pain that households feel. Third, the 2.2% in the video is a trailing 12-month figure, so it is backward-looking by construction. Ironically, that is the video's whole complaint about Fed data: this number describes the world through June, before the July oil surge.

That leads to how it can fail. A trimmed mean assumes extreme moves are noise. But sometimes extremes are the signal. If an oil shock like the one described in the video, with crude jumping nearly 24% in July, feeds into transportation, shipping, plastics, and food over the following months, the outlier gradually turns into broad inflation, and the "noise" you trimmed becomes the trend. The measure will catch up only after the pass-through has already spread into the middle of the distribution. Also, trimming reduces volatility but can add a lag in turning points, and it is subject to revisions when the underlying PCE data are revised. Treat a single month's reading with caution.

Finally, see where it fits in the family of inflation measures. It sits alongside core PCE, median CPI (the Cleveland Fed's cousin, which keeps only the single middle price change), and the Atlanta Fed's sticky-price CPI, which isolates items like rent and insurance that rarely reverse once they rise. Real-time tools such as Truflation and the Cleveland Fed's nowcast tell you where inflation might be heading, while the 10-year TIPS breakeven shows what bond traders expect. Trimmed mean PCE is best read as one instrument on a panel: a good gauge of the broad trend, but not a forecast. The sharpest takeaway is to compare it against headline and sticky measures, then ask whether the gaps are likely to close.
