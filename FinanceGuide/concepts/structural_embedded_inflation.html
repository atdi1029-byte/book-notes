Structural (Embedded) Inflation

Most people think of inflation as a single number — the Consumer Price Index ticks up, prices rise, and eventually they come back down. But economists and central bankers distinguish between two very different kinds of inflation, and mixing them up can cost you real money. Transitory inflation is temporary price pressure caused by a specific, identifiable shock — a port closure, a crop failure, a pandemic disrupting supply chains. It burns hot, then fades once the disruption resolves. Structural inflation, sometimes called embedded inflation, is something far more dangerous: it's price pressure that has woven itself into the cost fabric of the economy, no longer driven by any single shock but by the self-reinforcing expectations and behaviors of businesses, workers, and consumers. When inflation becomes structural, it doesn't need a trigger to keep going. It feeds itself.

The reason structural inflation matters so profoundly is that it changes the rules of the game for everything connected to money. Interest rates, bond yields, stock valuations, mortgage rates, wage negotiations, corporate pricing decisions — all of them are calibrated against assumptions about what inflation will do over the next one, five, and ten years. When those assumptions are anchored low, the entire financial system operates smoothly, with cheap borrowing lubricating growth. When inflation embeds itself structurally, those assumptions shatter. Central banks are forced to keep rates higher for longer, borrowing costs stay elevated across the entire economy, and assets priced on the expectation of cheap future money — growth stocks, real estate, long-duration bonds — reprice downward. In short, structural inflation doesn't just hurt your grocery bill. It reprices the future.

The mechanical process by which inflation becomes structural is sometimes called the wage-price spiral, but that's only one channel. Here's how it actually works: First, an initial shock pushes prices up across several sectors simultaneously. Workers, seeing their purchasing power erode, demand higher wages. Businesses, facing higher labor costs, raise prices to protect margins. Landlords, seeing construction and maintenance costs rise, raise rents. Businesses then factor these higher input costs into their long-term pricing models rather than treating them as temporary. Crucially, consumers and businesses begin to expect higher prices in the future, so they pull forward purchases and lock in higher wages preemptively. Those expectations themselves create the very inflation they anticipated. The original supply-chain shock is long gone, but the behavior it triggered has become self-sustaining. This is the embedded phase — and it is extraordinarily difficult to reverse without significant economic pain.

The clearest real-world signal that inflation has become structural is breadth — how many different categories of the economy are inflating simultaneously, not just one or two. This is exactly the data point Kevin Warsh highlighted at the Jackson Hole symposium in 2025. He pointed to the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) basket — the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge — and noted that approximately 54% of that basket was still running above 3% inflation on a 12-month basis. Before the pandemic, that figure was roughly 32%. That 22-percentage-point gap is not noise. It means that more than half of what American households actually spend money on — shelter, services, insurance, healthcare, food away from home — is still inflating at rates well above the Fed's 2% target, years after the original pandemic supply disruptions resolved.

For traders and investors, structural inflation fundamentally changes how you read market signals. Softer monthly prints — a single CPI or PCE report that comes in below expectations — are not the same as a structural downtrend in inflation. Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks were a direct rebuke of the narrative that had been dominating financial media all summer: that a few good months of data meant the Fed was ready to cut rates aggressively. Sophisticated investors track the breadth of inflation across PCE sub-components, not just the headline number. They watch supercore inflation (services excluding housing and energy), which is the most persistent and hardest to cure category. They also watch inflation expectations surveys — from the University of Michigan and the New York Fed — because if consumers start expecting 4% inflation for the next five years, that expectation alone can embed the problem further. When these signals stay elevated, the correct bet is that rates stay higher for longer, regardless of what headline CPI does month to month.

One of the most common misconceptions about structural inflation is conflating disinflation with deflation or victory. Disinflation means the rate of price increase is slowing — inflation is still happening, just at a lower pace. Prices are still going up, just less fast. The media coverage Warsh was implicitly criticizing made exactly this error: treating disinflation as equivalent to inflation being "conquered." A second major misconception is that interest rate hikes work quickly and evenly. In reality, monetary policy operates with long and variable lags — a phrase directly from Milton Friedman — meaning the full impact of rate hikes can take 12 to 24 months to work through the economy. This lag makes it genuinely difficult for the Fed to know, in real time, whether its policy is tight enough. Warsh's statement that he'd struggle to call current financial conditions "restrictive" suggests he believed the lags weren't biting hard enough to finish the job.

The edge case — and it's a dangerous one — is when a central bank declares premature victory over structural inflation and begins cutting rates before the embedding has truly reversed. This is what happened in the 1970s under Fed Chair Arthur Burns: rates were cut in 1974-75 as inflation seemed to moderate, but because it had already embedded itself in wage contracts and business pricing models, inflation roared back even higher, ultimately requiring the brutal Volcker shock of 1980-82 (rates above 20%) to finally break it. The modern version of this risk is precisely what Warsh was warning against — the market was pricing in a rapid cutting cycle based on a few soft monthly prints, while more than half the PCE basket was still running hot. If the Fed capitulates to market pressure and cuts prematurely, embedded inflation can re-accelerate, forcing an even more painful tightening cycle later.

Structurally embedded inflation connects directly to several other critical concepts. It is the primary driver of what economists call the neutral rate of interest — the theoretical rate at which monetary policy is neither stimulating nor restricting growth. If inflation is embedded at 4%, the neutral rate is significantly higher than if inflation is at 2%, meaning the Fed must maintain higher rates just to be in neutral, let alone restrictive. It also connects to real yields: what matters for economic activity is not the nominal interest rate but the rate minus inflation. If rates are 5% but inflation is 4%, real rates are only 1% — far less restrictive than they appear. Finally, embedded inflation shapes the entire yield curve: long-term bondholders demand higher yields as compensation for the erosion of purchasing power over time. Understanding structural inflation is, in essence, understanding why the simple story of "rates go up, inflation comes down, rates come back down" can fail — and what the much harder, more expensive alternative looks like.
