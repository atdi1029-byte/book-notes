Credit Default Swap (CDS)

A credit default swap, or CDS, is a contract that works like insurance on a loan or bond. One side, the protection buyer, pays a regular fee. The other side, the protection seller, promises to cover the loss if the borrower fails to pay its debt. Suppose you lend a company $100 million by buying its bonds and worry it might not repay. You can buy a CDS on that company. If it defaults, the seller pays you the loss. If it never defaults, you have paid fees for protection you didn't need, the same way you pay car insurance and never crash. The key difference from ordinary insurance is that anyone can buy a CDS, even someone who owns none of the debt.

CDS matters because it puts a market price on something that is otherwise hard to see: the odds that a company will not pay its bills. Stock prices mix together growth hopes, sentiment, and hype. A CDS price is narrower. It answers one question, which is how much it costs to protect against this borrower failing. That makes it a favorite warning gauge for lenders, regulators, and traders. When CDS prices on a company or a whole sector climb, borrowing gets harder and more expensive, bondholders get nervous, and stocks often follow. CDS also helped turn the 2008 crisis from a housing problem into a global financial one, which is why it still gets so much scrutiny.

Here is how it works, step by step. A buyer and a seller agree on a reference entity (the borrower), a notional amount (say $10 million of protection), and a term (often five years). The buyer pays a yearly fee called the spread, quoted in basis points, where one basis point is 0.01%. A spread of 500 basis points means 5% a year, or $500,000 on $10 million, usually paid quarterly. If nothing bad happens, payments continue until the contract ends. If a defined credit event occurs, such as missed payments, bankruptcy, or a restructuring, the contract settles. The seller covers the difference between the debt's face value and what it is still worth after the default. Because that recovery value is not known until afterward, settlement usually runs through an auction.

The video gives a live example. CoreWeave is an AI cloud company that builds data centers and buys Nvidia chips faster than it generates cash, and it fills the gap with borrowing. The presenter says it carries roughly $52 billion in loans and lease obligations. During the week described, its bonds sold off and yields on some debt pushed toward 15%. On Wednesday the stock fell 15.46% to $65.78. Thursday, Nvidia's CEO Jensen Huang said CoreWeave does not need a backstop, and the stock bounced briefly. Then on Friday it fell another 11.91% to $61.39 as the cost of insuring its debt against default hit a record. For the week the stock lost 25.2%.

"The bond market doesn't care about your backlog. It cares whether you can make the next payment."

Why does a record CDS price matter more than the CEO's reassurance? Because the CDS is what people are willing to pay, in real money, for protection. A reassuring comment costs nothing. Paying a higher and higher fee to insure CoreWeave's debt means someone is putting cash behind the fear. When Huang said no backstop was needed, he was talking, and the CDS market kept pricing the risk anyway. The stock bounced and the relief faded, while the CDS spread kept widening. That is why the presenter calls CoreWeave a canary in the coal mine. The problem was never weak demand. The problem was the price of borrowed money when rates were rising: the 10-year Treasury yield briefly topped 5.32% that week.

Traders use CDS in several practical ways. A bondholder hedges: owning the bonds while buying CDS removes most of the default risk. A speculator bets on trouble by buying protection with no bonds at all, hoping the spread widens so the contract can be sold at a profit before any default happens. A seller collects the fee as income, betting the borrower stays healthy. Equity investors and analysts treat the spread as a signal. A rising spread often warns of trouble before a stock reflects it, and comparing spreads across companies shows who the market thinks is safest. Wide, rising spreads on heavy borrowers, next to tight spreads on cash-rich firms like Apple, are the "AI winners versus AI borrowers" split in action.

There are several gotchas. First, a high CDS price signals risk, not certainty. Spreads can overshoot, and many companies with wide spreads never default. Second, the CDS market is often thin, with few trades, so a "record" can reflect a handful of nervous buyers rather than broad judgment. Third, a rising spread does not mean the buyer is right, only that the price moved. Fourth, a CDS covers the company's debt, not its stock. A stock can fall hard while the CDS barely moves, or the reverse. Fifth, a CDS is not a prediction by an expert panel. It is a price set by supply, demand, and hedging needs, which can include banks hedging lending exposure rather than expressing a view on the company.

CDS can also fail in ways that matter. The seller must actually have the money to pay. In 2008, AIG had sold enormous amounts of protection and could not cover the claims when mortgage debt collapsed, which required a government rescue. That is counterparty risk: your insurance is only as good as the insurer. Disputes over definitions are another risk. Whether a restructuring or a missed payment counts as a credit event can be argued, and the answer decides who gets paid. Because the buyer needs no ownership of the debt, bets can pile up far above the real amount of debt, so one default can trigger losses much larger than the loan itself. Today most CDS trades are cleared through central clearinghouses, which reduces but does not remove these risks.

CDS connects to many other ideas in the video. It is tied closely to bond yields: the extra yield a risky borrower pays over Treasuries is the same risk viewed from the bond side, and CDS spreads and bond spreads usually move together. It links to the cost of capital, since when the 10-year yield sits above 5%, borrowers like CoreWeave face both a higher base rate and a higher risk premium on top. It also connects to leverage and distressed debt, which the presenter mentions when bonds trade in distressed territory. And it connects to inflation and Fed policy: hot CPI and PPI readings raised rate-hike fears, which pushed yields up, which exposed the most indebted companies first. A record CDS price is where that chain of cause and effect shows up as a single number.

To talk about this confidently, remember the core idea: a CDS is a price on the chance that a borrower fails to repay. The buyer pays a spread, the seller promises to pay out after a credit event, and the spread rises as the market's fear rises. In the CoreWeave story, the stock fell, the bonds sold off, and the CDS hit a record, all pointing to the same thing: a company that depends on borrowed money was being re-priced by a bond market where money suddenly cost more. Treat a CDS as one useful signal rather than a verdict. Read it alongside bond yields, the company's cash flow, and its debt maturities, and you will understand credit stress better than most people who only watch the stock chart.
