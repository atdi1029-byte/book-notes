Geographic Arbitrage and Price Spread

Geographic arbitrage means buying a good where it is cheap and selling it where it is expensive, and keeping the difference. The difference between the two prices is the price spread. The idea is older than finance: a merchant who bought spices in one port and sold them in another was doing it. The video gives a modern version. A million BTUs of natural gas (BTU is the standard unit of heat energy) trades for about $3.24 at the US benchmark, Henry Hub. In Europe, the benchmark, the Dutch TTF, implies roughly $28 for the same amount. It is the same molecule with the same chemistry, priced about nine to one apart. A spread like that is an opportunity for whoever can physically move the product from one market to the other.

The spread matters because it shows whether two markets are actually connected. In a perfectly linked world, a price gap would pull supply toward the expensive market until the gap closed. When a large gap persists, something is blocking that process, and finding the blocker tells you where the real constraint is. The spread also decides who gets paid. The video's central point is that the $25 gap between Europe and the US does not go to American gas producers, and it does not raise Henry Hub. It goes to a specific party in the chain. This is why a headline like "Europe pays nine times more" tells you very little about what American households will pay. You have to ask who captures the spread and whether the connecting infrastructure can carry more.

The mechanism has four steps. First, someone buys gas in the cheap market at the local price. Second, the gas is moved to the expensive market. Natural gas can't just be loaded onto a ship, so it has to be chilled into a liquid. That is LNG, liquefied natural gas, and it requires an export terminal, which is a plant with liquefaction trains. Third, the liquid travels on a tanker, which costs freight and takes time. Fourth, it is sold at the foreign price. Profit is the destination price, minus the purchase price, minus the costs of liquefying, shipping and regasifying. The spread is what you start with, and the net margin is what remains after those costs. Each of these links can be a bottleneck.

Now the video's numbers. On a Tuesday, front-month TTF closed at €86.8 per megawatt hour, its highest since December 2022 and up about 168% from a year earlier. One megawatt hour equals about 3.412 million BTUs, and a euro was worth about $1.12, so Europe was paying roughly $28 per million BTU. November Henry Hub traded near $3.24. That leaves a gap of about $25. A standard LNG tanker carries roughly 3.7 million million-BTU units, so the gross spread on one cargo is about $90 million. The presenter calls this "back of the envelope" math, which is accurate: it is a gross figure before freight, fees and the voyage. After those costs it is still, in his words, "tens of millions of dollars a ship."

Who gets that money depends on the contract. The video describes a typical terminal agreement as working like a toll road. The buyer pays about 115% of Henry Hub for the gas, plus a fixed liquefaction fee that has historically run from $2.25 to $3.50. Whatever Europe pays above that total belongs to whoever owns the cargo. The terminal gets its fixed fee whether Europe pays $10 or $28, and the American gas seller gets Henry Hub-linked pricing. The cargo owner, often a trading firm or portfolio player, holds the exposure to the spread and keeps it.

"Whatever Europe pays above that goes to whoever owns the cargo. The trader keeps the spread. The American price stays anchored to American supply."

Traders and investors use spreads as both a signal and a trade. A wide spread tells you the destination market is short of supply and the origin market is not. Here that means low European storage, about 74% full and the lowest for this time of year since 2011 in the video's figures, while US storage sits about 2% above its five-year average. Traders who hold terminal capacity or cargo contracts get paid. They also watch the costs: freight rates, the liquefaction fee, and how long the voyage takes. If the spread falls below those costs, they stop shipping and the arbitrage shuts off. Investors can also look at who owns the capacity. The toll-road owners earn steady fees. The cargo owners earn volatile spread profits.

The biggest misconception is that a big spread automatically pulls prices together. In theory arbitrage closes gaps, because buying in the cheap market pushes its price up and selling in the expensive market pushes its price down. That only works if you can move unlimited quantity. The video's key point is that you can't. Terminals can only process so much gas per day, set by the compressors they were built with. US LNG feed gas averaged about 17.4 billion cubic feet a day in October, out of about 110.6 billion produced, roughly 16%. Once the pipe is full, a higher European price "cannot pull one more molecule out of Texas." It only makes each cargo more profitable. October flows were actually below September's 18 bcf/d because of a delayed Freeport restart and Cove Point maintenance. A second gotcha is that the headline spread is gross. Real profit is the spread minus costs, and the video itself says it could not verify the popular "98% utilization" claim, so check the sources behind a viral number.

Arbitrage links can break or change in several ways. Pricing can differ for weeks when capacity is fixed, as the 9-to-1 gap shows. Outages widen the gap: a terminal going offline cuts the flow of cheap gas while the destination price stays high. Weather can erase the firewall: on January 23 this year, cash gas at Henry Hub for weekend delivery rose above $28, so for one weekend Americans paid the European price, and the video says that was weather, not exports. The long-run risk is the one the video flags. New capacity, including Golden Pass (first cargo April 22), Corpus Christi's expansion (finished August 28) and Plaquemines, adds what the presenter calls more straws in the same glass. As capacity grows, more of the European price can reach back into the US market. The EIA expects US production to rise from about 112.2 to 116.1 bcf/d next year, which is why it still forecasts Henry Hub near $3.16 in 2027. The gap would then start to shrink from the American side.

Geographic arbitrage connects to several other ideas. It is a case of the law of one price, which says identical goods should sell for the same price once you account for transport and costs. This chapter is about the reasons that law fails in practice, and the main one is capacity constraints. It also relates to benchmark pricing, since Henry Hub and TTF are separate reference prices for separate regions. It connects to storage and seasonality, because inventory relative to the five-year average often explains the spread. And it relates to substitution between fuels. The video notes that the real winter shock is in heating oil, about 21% higher at an average of $2,115 for roughly 4.1 million homes, and it comes from tight distillate stocks, not from European gas. Someone who understands arbitrage can explain why a price spike abroad doesn't automatically reach home, and can also say when it might.
