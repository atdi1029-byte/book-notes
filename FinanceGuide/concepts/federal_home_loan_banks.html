Federal Home Loan Bank (FHLB) Advances

The Federal Home Loan Bank (FHLB) system is a network of eleven government-sponsored cooperative banks, created by Congress in 1932 during the Depression, that lend to other financial institutions. Its customers are its owners: commercial banks, thrifts, credit unions, insurance companies, and community lenders that buy stock in their regional FHLB. A loan from one of these banks to a member is called an advance. Think of it as a wholesale credit line for banks. You can't walk into an FHLB and open an account, but your bank can call it on a Tuesday afternoon and have cash by Wednesday, provided it pledges good collateral.

Advances matter because they are the shock absorber sitting behind the banking system. The FHLB system routinely has hundreds of billions of dollars outstanding. It funds itself cheaply by issuing debt with an implicit government backing, and it passes that cheap funding to members, who use it to make mortgages, cover seasonal swings, and plug funding gaps. When advances are quiet, nothing seems wrong. When they surge, it usually means deposits are leaving the banking system faster than banks can replace them. That makes the FHLB balance sheet one of the best places to read stress in real time, well before it appears in headlines.

Mechanically, an advance works like a secured loan. A bank chooses a term, from overnight to ten years or more, and a rate type, fixed or floating. It then pledges collateral: residential mortgages, commercial real estate loans, Treasuries, or agency securities. The FHLB applies a haircut, lending only a fraction of the collateral's value. A bank pledging $100 million of mortgages might borrow $80 million. The FHLB prices the advance off its own cost of funds plus a small markup, so it usually costs less than an unsecured borrowing. Members are also required to hold FHLB stock in proportion to their borrowing, which locks them into the system.

Here is how the video uses the concept. A regional bank loaded up on 10- and 30-year Treasuries and mortgage bonds yielding roughly 1.5% to 2.2% back in 2020 and 2021. Those bonds now sit in the held-to-maturity bucket, and the FDIC reports $325.1 billion in unrealized losses across the industry. Then depositors leave. Sophisticated savers and corporate treasurers sweep cash into T-bills and money funds yielding over 4%, while the bank pays 0.2% to 0.4% on checking. The bank needs cash to honor withdrawals but can't sell its bonds without tainting the whole portfolio. So it calls the FHLB.

"Instead of selling those bonds, the bank goes to wholesale borrowing markets. They borrow from the Federal Home Loan Bank system. They borrow in the overnight Fed funds window. They pay 4% plus."

The arithmetic shows why this is painful. Suppose a bank holds $10 billion of bonds yielding 2%, which is $200 million a year. If $2 billion of deposits leave, and the bank replaces them with advances at 4.2%, it is paying $84 million a year for money that used to cost perhaps $6 million at a 0.3% deposit rate. Nothing about the bonds changed, but the funding cost of part of the balance sheet rose roughly fourteen-fold. Repeat that across many billions and the net interest margin, the gap between what a bank earns and what it pays, shrinks toward zero and can turn negative. That is the video's "catalyst three," where the bank slowly burns its own capital to keep operating.

Investors and traders watch advances as a signal. The FHLB Office of Finance and each regional bank publish quarterly figures, and banks disclose their advance balances in filings. A rising ratio of FHLB borrowings plus brokered deposits to total funding suggests a bank is relying on wholesale money because its core deposit base is eroding. Analysts compare that ratio with the bank's unrealized securities losses, its tangible common equity, and its uninsured-deposit share. A bank with heavy advances, large HTM losses, and a high share of uninsured deposits is the profile that fails when confidence turns. Short sellers screen for exactly that combination, and long-only holders should too.

There are nuances people miss. First, borrowing from the FHLB is not itself a sign of weakness. Healthy banks use advances for asset-liability matching, such as funding a fixed-rate mortgage with a fixed-rate advance of similar length. The signal is the reason and the trend: is the bank borrowing to grow, or to replace fleeing deposits? Second, advances are not free money from the Fed. They are secured loans that sit ahead of depositors and the FDIC in the pecking order. Third, the video says the bank is "slowly liquidating its internal capital," which is fair for margin compression, but the pain shows up gradually in earnings rather than as a sudden cliff.

Advances can also break in ways that matter. Because the FHLB lends against collateral and takes a haircut, a bank in trouble can find its collateral marked down or its borrowing capacity cut. Every dollar pledged to the FHLB is a dollar that is unavailable to depositors and other creditors if the bank fails. This is the super-lien problem: the FHLB is paid first, which leaves the FDIC's insurance fund to absorb larger losses. In March 2023, advances surged to a record above $1 trillion as Silicon Valley Bank and others failed and banks scrambled for liquidity. FHLB borrowing rose because it worked as a backstop, but it also showed how quickly a funding problem becomes a solvency question once the bank runs out of pledgeable collateral.

FHLB advances connect to nearly every other idea in the video. They are the escape hatch that keeps banks from selling held-to-maturity bonds, which is why the accounting padlock and the borrowing choice are two halves of one story. They tie to the Fed funds and SOFR rates that set the cost of overnight money, to the discount window, which is the Fed's own lender of last resort, and to the credit squeeze consumers feel. A bank paying 4% for funding while earning 2% on old bonds has little room to lend, so it tightens HELOC and mortgage standards. Advances postpone the day of reckoning for the bank, but the household often pays the bill through scarcer, pricier credit.

The practical takeaway is to read advances as a symptom, not a verdict. Rising FHLB borrowing tells you a bank is paying up for money that used to come free from depositors. If the bank also holds large unrealized losses, funds itself with uninsured deposits, and shows a shrinking net interest margin, the risk is real. If it borrows to match longer-term loans while its deposits stay stable, it is ordinary banking. Ask three questions: how fast are advances growing, what is the rate spread versus the yield on the assets, and could the bank survive if it had to sell its bonds tomorrow? Those answers separate a stressed balance sheet from a routine one.
