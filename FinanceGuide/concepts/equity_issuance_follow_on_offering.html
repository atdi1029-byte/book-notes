Equity Issuance (Follow-On Stock Offering)

A follow-on offering (also called a secondary offering or seasoned equity offering) happens when a company that is already publicly traded sells brand-new shares of its own stock to raise cash. It differs from an IPO, which is the first time a company sells shares to the public. The company creates shares that did not exist before, hands them to investors, and receives the money. Think of the company as a pie. After the offering the pie is bigger, but every existing slice is a smaller percentage of it. That is why the move is so closely watched. The company gets funding, and everyone who already owns the stock now owns a thinner share of the business.

It matters because stock is one of only a few ways a company can fund itself, and the choice reveals a lot. A company can spend the cash it earns, borrow through bonds or loans, or sell ownership. Equity is the most expensive of the three in the long run because shareholders expect high returns for taking the most risk. When a large company reaches for it, the market asks why it is not paying with its own cash flow. Offerings also move prices directly: new supply of shares, sold at a discount, pulls the stock down. They affect index funds, retirement accounts, and anyone holding the name. The effects spread to credit markets and to the suppliers and customers of whatever the money is funding.

Mechanically, the process runs fast. First the board authorizes the sale, and the company files with the SEC, often using a shelf registration that lets it sell on short notice. Then it hires investment banks as underwriters. After the market closes, usually with a short announcement, the banks call large institutions to gauge demand, a step called bookbuilding. Overnight they set a price, typically below the last close, and the buyers commit. The banks buy the shares from the company and resell them, keeping a fee, the underwriting spread, commonly around 1% to 4%. Shares settle a day or two later. An overnight or bought deal means the banks take the price risk themselves, which is why they demand a discount.

Oracle's deal in the video shows every piece. On October 28th, at its investor day, Oracle said it would limit new capital-markets funding to $20 billion through fiscal 2030, about $10 billion of stock and $10 billion of debt. Five days later, on Monday night before the midterm elections, it priced 86.2 million new shares at $116 each, which is roughly $10 billion (86.2 million × $116 ≈ $10.0 billion). The same night it sold $10 billion of bonds. The presenter points out that Oracle did not break its promise, since it raised right up to the ceiling. But it spent a four-year allowance in one evening, which invites an obvious question about year two.

"$116 a share was about 11% below where Oracle closed the Friday before... So existing shareholders got diluted and marked down in the same breath. Nobody ordered that combo."

The video's phrase "diluted and marked down in the same breath" names two separate harms, and it helps to pull them apart. Dilution is the ownership effect. If a company had about 2.8 billion shares and adds 86.2 million, existing holders go from owning 100% to owning about 97% of the company. That is a roughly 3% cut in their claim on future profits unless the new money earns a good return. The markdown is the price effect. The stock was already down 8.8% on Monday, closing at $119.22, and the deal priced at $116, below even that. Oracle sold ownership at a discount to what the market had just said it was worth. Share counts here are illustrative. The video gives only the offering size and price, so treat the 3% as an order-of-magnitude estimate.

Investors read offerings as signals. The standard finding is that stock prices tend to fall on the announcement of a follow-on, because managers are assumed to sell equity when they think it is richly priced, or when other funding is too costly. Traders watch three things. First is the discount to the last close. A wide one signals weak demand or urgency. Second is the use of proceeds. Money for a profitable expansion is seen differently from money to plug a funding gap. Third is the pairing with other financing. Here Oracle's stock sale came alongside bonds yielding up to 8.65%, with the 2037 bond at 8%, about 3.1 percentage points over the roughly 4.91% Treasury yield. Stock sold cheaply and debt sold expensively together say cash flow is not covering the bills. One issuer is a headline. Five is a trend.

There are several gotchas. First, dilution is not automatically bad. If a company raises $10 billion and invests it at returns above what shareholders require, the value per share can rise even though the share count did. Second, the discount is not always a loss to existing holders. Part of it is a price paid for certainty, since the banks guarantee the money. Third, the price drop often begins before the deal is announced, because rumors leak and traders anticipate it, so the "dilution drop" may already be in the price by the time you read about it. Fourth, an offering is different from a buyback, which does the reverse. Fifth, raising equity can actually protect bondholders and ratings, because it lowers the need for debt. Oracle was rated BBB- by S&P, one step above junk, so adding equity helped its credit even while it hurt shareholders.

Offerings can go wrong in several ways. If demand is weak, the underwriters may be stuck holding unsold shares and sell them into the market at lower prices, which pushes the stock down further. A deal can be cut in size or pulled. A company that raises equity at a depressed price has to issue more shares to get the same dollars, so dilution grows as the price falls, a vicious cycle sometimes called a death spiral when it repeats. Marvell's all-stock purchase of Lumentum shows a related failure. Marvell stock fell 12.7% on Monday and Lumentum fell 18.6%, because Lumentum holders are paid in Marvell shares at a fixed ratio of 4.5. When your stock is your currency, a falling price shrinks what you can buy, and the premium can disappear before the deal even closes.

"Once your stock is your currency, your share price stops being a scorecard, it starts being the business plan."

Equity issuance connects to many other ideas. It sits beside debt issuance, and comparing the two shows the cost of capital: stock costs more over time, but unlike bonds it carries no required payments. It ties to credit spreads and ratings, as Oracle's BBB- rating and 3.1-point spread over Treasuries show. It links to dilution and earnings per share, since more shares spread the same profit thinner. Stock-funded acquisitions, like Marvell's, use the same mechanism with the target's holders receiving the new shares. The lesson from Lucent in 1999 is that paper money works until the price falls. Before buying any AI-linked company, ask whether it funds growth from cash it generates or from shares it prints, and who absorbs the dilution. If you own the stock, the answer may be you.
