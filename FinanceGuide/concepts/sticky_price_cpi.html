Sticky-Price CPI

The sticky-price CPI is a version of the Consumer Price Index, published by the Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, that looks only at the goods and services whose prices change slowly. Economists split the CPI basket into two groups. Flexible-price items, such as gasoline, airfares, fresh produce and hotel rooms, get repriced constantly, sometimes daily. Sticky-price items, such as rent, insurance premiums, medical services, cable and phone bills, and tuition, get repriced rarely. A landlord may change your rent once a year, and an insurer may change your premium once every six or twelve months. The sticky measure strips out the jumpy part of inflation and keeps the slow-moving part. In the video, the Atlanta Fed's reading was 2.8% year-over-year in June.

It matters because the Fed does not really fear a one-month spike in gas prices. It fears inflation that becomes a habit. When oil jumps 24% in a month, as it did in July in the video, headline CPI lurches, but the price of crude can fall just as quickly as it rose. Sticky prices behave differently. Once rent, insurance and service contracts reset higher, they rarely reset lower, so they carry the inflation forward for years. That is why the video calls it the inflation that keeps a Fed chair awake. It shapes mortgage rates, credit card APRs, the pace of rate cuts or hikes, and the Social Security cost-of-living adjustment that retirees depend on.

Mechanically, the Atlanta Fed starts with the individual components of the CPI, around 45 categories, each with its own history of price changes. It measures how often and how much each category's price moves, and it uses that history to classify each one as sticky or flexible. Researchers drew on work by economists Bryan and Meyer, who used the frequency of price adjustment as the dividing line. The Atlanta Fed then rebuilds two separate indexes, one for each group, weighting each category by its share of consumer spending. The sticky index is updated each month when the Bureau of Labor Statistics releases the CPI, and it is usually reported as a 12-month percentage change.

Why do some prices stick? There are real reasons. Changing prices costs money, which economists call menu costs, and for a landlord or insurer that cost includes renegotiating contracts, filing rate changes with state regulators, and risking tenants or customers leaving. Many prices are fixed by multi-year agreements. Wages are the biggest reason of all. A service business, such as a dental office or an insurance company, sets its prices mostly by what it pays its workers, and wages are themselves sticky and rarely cut. There is also a psychological factor. Customers resent price increases, so firms avoid changing prices often and instead make larger, less frequent adjustments. Those adjustments only go one way in a healthy economy, and that is up.

"Things like rent… insurance, same, upward only. Cable bills, upward only. And as of June, that measure was running at 2.8% year-over-year. That's the inflation that actually keeps a Fed chair awake at night."

Look at the video's numbers side by side. Headline CPI was expected at roughly 3.4% year-over-year for July, down from 3.5% in June. The Dallas Fed's trimmed mean PCE, which discards the most extreme price moves at both ends, stood at 2.2% through June. Sticky-price CPI sat at 2.8%. The ten-year TIPS breakeven, the bond market's inflation forecast, was 2.29%. Headline inflation at 3.4% is being pushed up by energy, which is flexible and could reverse. The 2.8% sticky reading says that the stubborn part of the economy is running well above the Fed's 2% target, and it is the harder number to bring down. The gap between 2.8% and 2.2% is the kind of detail that makes a Fed official hesitate before cutting rates.

Traders and investors use sticky-price CPI as a filter for what is noise and what is signal. When headline CPI surprises to the upside but the sticky component is flat or falling, the market tends to treat the surprise as temporary, often an energy blip, and rate expectations barely move. When the sticky component accelerates, markets price in a higher chance of Fed hikes or a slower path of cuts, which pushes up Treasury yields and typically weighs on growth stocks and rate-sensitive sectors like housing and utilities. Bond investors also compare it with the TIPS breakeven. If sticky inflation runs at 2.8% while breakevens say 2.29%, either bond traders are too relaxed or the sticky measure is about to cool. Either way, the gap is worth a closer look.

There are several gotchas. First, sticky does not mean permanent or unimportant in the other direction. The classification is based on past behavior, and it can drift. Second, the sticky index is not the same as core CPI. Core removes food and energy by category, while the sticky index sorts by price-change frequency, so a flexible category like used cars is inside core but outside sticky. Third, it is still a government-survey number, so it carries the same lag the video complains about. The July CPI is released in August, and the June reading was the latest available. Finally, a high sticky reading does not mean inflation is accelerating. It may only mean that the slow part is catching up to earlier price increases. Rent, for example, lags market rents by months because most leases roll over only once a year.

What happens when the concept breaks down? The sticky-flexible divide assumes that the sticky group moves slowly, but a severe shock can make sticky prices move quickly. In the high-inflation years after 2021, insurers and landlords repriced aggressively, and categories that had barely moved for a decade suddenly rose double digits. When a supply shock lasts long enough, firms stop treating it as temporary and build it into their contracts and wages. This is the feedback loop central bankers dread, and the sticky measure is the early warning sign that it has begun. The risk of misreading it runs in both directions. Ignore it, and the Fed may cut too early and let inflation entrench. Over-rely on it, and the Fed may hold rates high while the lagging data is already softening, which is the rearview-mirror problem the video describes.

Sticky-price CPI connects to most of the other tools on the video's instrument panel. It sits beside the Dallas Fed's trimmed mean PCE, which attacks the same noise problem by a different method, discarding extremes rather than sorting by price flexibility. It complements the Cleveland Fed's inflation nowcast, which tries to guess the next headline number, and the ISM prices paid index of 71.1, which shows cost pressure building at factories before it reaches shelves. It also ties to the TIPS breakeven, the market's own forecast. Together they tell the story the video wants you to see. Energy shocks are loud and fast, wages, rents and contracts are slow, and the Fed's job is to work out which one it is looking at. When the loud and slow signals agree, that is when it acts.
