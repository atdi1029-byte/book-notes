Non-Traded Perpetual Vehicles for Retail Investors

A non-traded perpetual vehicle is a pooled fund that holds hard-to-sell assets, usually private loans or real estate. It has no end date and does not trade on an exchange. The two common forms are the non-traded BDC (business development company), which lends to mid-sized companies, and the non-traded REIT, which owns property. "Perpetual" means the fund keeps accepting new money and never has to liquidate on a schedule. "Non-traded" means you cannot sell your shares to another investor on a screen. Your only exit is to ask the fund itself to buy your shares back, on the fund's terms, at the fund's calculated net asset value. That exit is the product's central feature.

This matters because these vehicles have moved from a niche into mainstream advice. After 2008, banks faced tighter capital rules, and private equity sponsors built direct lending funds to fill the gap. The video puts the sector near $1.7 trillion. Wealth platforms then packaged that lending for retail investors and 401(k) rollovers, promising yields of 10% to 12% with none of the daily price swings of stocks or bonds. For a household, the decision is whether that money is truly available when needed. It is not like a money market fund or an ETF. The same product can look safe for years and then trap capital at exactly the moment you want it back.

Here is the mechanism. The fund raises cash from investors and lends it to private companies. These loans are typically floating-rate: they pay a benchmark such as SOFR plus a spread of 600 to 700 basis points. The loans mature in three to four years and cannot be sold quickly. Investors, though, are offered periodic liquidity, usually through a quarterly tender offer in which the fund repurchases up to about 5% of net asset value. That is roughly 20% a year. Because the cash going out is capped, the fund only has to hold a small cash buffer, and new investor money coming in can fund the redemptions. This is a maturity mismatch: short-term promises backed by long-term assets.

The video gives concrete numbers. It cites $7.1 billion of redemption requests hitting the largest private credit funds in the first half of the year. It then walks through the borrower's side. When SOFR was near zero, a borrower paying a 650 basis point spread owed about 6.5% in total. With SOFR at 3.88%, the same borrower owes roughly 10.4%. The debt service cost rose about 60% while operating cash flow, in the video's telling, stayed flat. If that is right, the same floating-rate loans that pay investors more also put the borrowers under more strain. Higher payments can mean more late payments and more restructurings. At the same time, investors may notice that Treasuries now pay a meaningful yield with no lockup.

"The volatility was never eliminated. It was simply stored up behind a locked door."

Investors use these vehicles for a specific job: capturing an illiquidity premium, the extra yield paid for accepting that you cannot get out quickly. Used sensibly, that means a modest slice of a portfolio, sized so that a frozen position would not force you to sell something else at a bad time. The video offers a three-part audit. First, check how much reported income is PIK (payment-in-kind), where the borrower pays interest in more IOUs instead of cash. The video treats more than 10% to 15% as a warning sign. Second, read the latest 10-Q and see whether tenders were honored in full or prorated. Third, check the asset coverage ratio, which must stay at or above 150% for BDCs.

There are several nuances. The 5% cap is usually described as "statutory" in the video, but it is mostly a limit the fund sets in its own documents, and the board decides each quarter whether to run a tender at all. PIK is also not automatically a red flag. Some loans are structured with PIK from day one, so what matters is whether PIK is rising because borrowers are struggling. Reported low volatility is partly a pricing artifact. Private loans are valued by models and managers rather than a market, so a smooth line does not mean low risk. Finally, a high yield is not the same as a high return. If principal is impaired, the distribution can be partly a return of your own money.

Here is how things break. Suppose redemption requests exceed the cap. The board can prorate, paying everyone the same fraction. If you request $100,000 and demand is double the cap, you might receive about half. If demand is ten times the cap, you receive $10,000 and the other $90,000 stays inside. The fund can also sell assets at a discount, which lowers NAV for everyone who remains, or it can borrow against credit lines to meet requests. That leverage adds risk. In the extreme, the board can suspend tenders entirely. Remaining holders may then see their NAV marked down, and there is no market to sell into. Early redeemers get out whole while the rest bear the losses, which is what turns a slow leak into a run.

These vehicles connect to several core ideas. Liquidity risk and maturity transformation are the same mechanism that causes bank runs, except here the "bank" has written a gate into its contract. Interest rate risk works in an unusual direction: floating-rate loans protect the lender's income, but they transfer risk to the borrower's credit quality. Opportunity cost matters too. When Treasuries or money market funds pay close to what private credit pays, the illiquidity premium shrinks. And asset allocation logic applies: a fund that cannot be rebalanced or sold in a crisis is not diversifying your portfolio, it is concentrating your risk in time.

Before buying any non-traded vehicle, read the redemption section the way you would read a loan agreement. Find the quarterly cap, who decides whether to honor it, what happens when requests exceed it, and whether the board can suspend repurchases. Ask how the NAV is calculated, how much of the income is PIK, and how much fund-level debt sits above you. Then ask the question that decides everything: if I could not touch this money for three years, would I still be fine? If the answer is no, the position is too large. The yield is real compensation, but what you give up for it is the ability to leave.
