Core PCE (Personal Consumption Expenditures)

Core PCE, or Core Personal Consumption Expenditures, is the Federal Reserve's preferred measure of inflation in the United States. Unlike the more famous Consumer Price Index (CPI), which tracks what consumers pay for a fixed basket of goods, PCE measures what businesses actually charge across the entire economy — covering everything households spend money on, from groceries to haircuts to hospital bills. The "core" part means it strips out food and energy prices, which swing wildly month to month based on weather, geopolitics, and commodity cycles. What remains is a cleaner signal of the underlying, persistent inflation that the Fed actually tries to control. When the Fed says its inflation target is 2%, it means core PCE specifically — not CPI, not headline PCE.

Why does this one number carry so much weight? Because it directly drives Federal Reserve interest rate decisions, and interest rates are the price of money itself. When core PCE runs above the 2% target, the Fed feels pressure to raise the federal funds rate — the benchmark rate that cascades through every corner of the financial system. Higher rates mean more expensive mortgages, costlier business loans, tighter credit card terms, and lower valuations for stocks. When core PCE cools toward 2%, the Fed gains room to cut rates, loosening financial conditions and stimulating growth. Every asset class — equities, bonds, real estate, commodities — prices itself relative to where the Fed is expected to move next, and core PCE is the single most important input into that expectation.

The mechanical difference between PCE and CPI matters more than most people realize. CPI uses a fixed basket: the Bureau of Labor Statistics decides what an average household buys and tracks those same items over time. PCE uses a chain-weighted formula that adjusts for substitution — if beef gets expensive and consumers switch to chicken, PCE captures that behavioral shift automatically. PCE also covers a broader scope, including healthcare costs paid by employers and the government on consumers' behalf, which CPI ignores. The result is that PCE typically runs about 0.3 to 0.5 percentage points lower than CPI. The Fed chose PCE precisely because it's considered a more realistic picture of actual spending behavior rather than a theoretical basket frozen in time.

To see why this matters in practice, consider the situation Kevin Warsh described at the Jackson Hole Economic Symposium in 2026. The mainstream narrative all summer had been that inflation was essentially defeated — monthly prints had softened, and markets were pricing in imminent rate cuts. But Warsh pointed to the annual numbers: headline PCE at 3.7% and core PCE at 3.3%, both dramatically above the 2% target. A single soft monthly reading might drop the annualized rate briefly, but 3.3% core PCE means that over the past year, the prices of everything except food and energy rose by 3.3% — nearly 65% faster than the Fed's stated goal. Warsh's point was that monthly noise was being used to obscure an annual reality that hadn't changed.

"This summer's better than expected inflation prints do not mean underlying inflation trends have meaningfully improved." — Kevin Warsh, Jackson Hole, 2026

Traders and portfolio managers treat core PCE releases as among the most market-moving data events on the calendar. The report is released by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) monthly, typically near the end of each month. A reading that comes in hotter than expected tends to push Treasury yields higher (as markets price in fewer rate cuts or more hikes), which drags down bond prices and often pressures equities — especially growth stocks and REITs that are highly sensitive to discount rates. A cooler-than-expected reading does the opposite. Sophisticated traders also watch the month-over-month change versus the year-over-year figure: a string of soft monthly prints can eventually pull the annual number down, but only if those soft prints are sustained and not revised upward later.

One of the most common misconceptions is confusing a declining rate of inflation with actual price decreases. When core PCE falls from 3.3% to 2.8%, prices are still rising — just slightly slower. This is called disinflation, not deflation. Actual price decreases require negative PCE readings, which are rare and often signal dangerous economic contraction. Another gotcha: the Fed targets 2% inflation on average over time, which is why a few months of good data don't automatically trigger rate cuts. After the post-COVID inflation surge, the Fed was acutely aware that declaring victory prematurely — as it arguably did in the 1970s under Arthur Burns — could allow inflation to re-accelerate, requiring even more painful tightening later.

The scenario where core PCE "breaks" as a signal is when it diverges sharply from other inflation measures, creating confusion about what's real. During the 2021–2023 inflation surge, critics pointed out that shelter costs (rent and owners' equivalent rent) were lagging in PCE because the methodology uses smoothed, backward-looking lease data rather than current market rents. This meant PCE was understating the real cost of housing for months. Similarly, if supply chain disruptions cause a temporary spike in goods prices that then reverses, a single month's core PCE can swing dramatically and mislead policy. The Fed explicitly says it looks at supercore PCE — services inflation excluding housing — to cut through these distortions, adding yet another layer of complexity to the interpretation.

Core PCE connects directly to the broader concept of financial conditions, which is why Warsh's Jackson Hole statement was so significant. When he said he would be "hard pressed to describe broad financial conditions as restrictive," he was making a precise claim: despite elevated interest rates on paper, credit was still flowing freely, stock markets were near record highs, and spreads were tight. This matters because the Fed's rate hikes only work to cool inflation if they actually tighten conditions — if markets price in imminent cuts, long-term rates don't rise much and the tightening has little bite. Core PCE sitting at 3.3% while financial conditions remain loose means the Fed's inflation-fighting medicine hasn't been absorbed. That's the chain: core PCE elevated → Fed signals longer-for-higher → financial conditions tighten → borrowing slows → demand cools → inflation falls. Break any link, and the whole mechanism fails.
