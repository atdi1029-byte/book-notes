Inflation Nowcasting

Inflation nowcasting is the practice of estimating what inflation is right now, or what the next official report will say, using data that arrives faster than the official statistics. "Nowcast" blends "now" and "forecast." A forecast asks what inflation will be next year. A nowcast asks what it is this month, a question that sounds silly until you learn that the answer is not known for weeks. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the Consumer Price Index (CPI) about two weeks after a month ends. Core PCE, the Fed's preferred gauge, arrives later still. A nowcast fills that gap with a running estimate built from oil prices, gasoline, past inflation, and market signals, updated daily instead of monthly.

It matters because nearly every decision tied to inflation is made on stale information. The video's central complaint is that the Fed's three big inputs (unemployment, core PCE, and GDP) all describe the past. GDP covered the second quarter, July's jobless rate described July, and the latest core PCE reflected June prices, before oil jumped nearly 24% in July. Mortgage rates, credit card APRs, bond yields, and the stock market all respond to expected Fed policy, which responds to inflation. Even Social Security cost-of-living adjustments are tied to CPI. If you can see inflation turning a few weeks before everyone else, you see rate expectations shifting before they shift in prices.

"That's not data dependence, that is data lag."

Mechanically, a nowcast starts from a simple fact: inflation is not random from month to month. Part of it is predictable from things we already observe. The best-known version is the Cleveland Fed Inflation Nowcast, which is refreshed every business day. In rough terms, it takes the daily price of oil, weekly retail gasoline prices, and the most recent actual CPI and PCE readings. A statistical model, trained on decades of history, learns how much gasoline and oil moves typically feed through to headline inflation and how persistent core inflation tends to be. It then outputs estimates of month-over-month and year-over-year CPI and PCE, for headline and core, for the current month and quarter. Each new day's energy prices nudge the estimate, and the official release eventually replaces it.

Take the video's own setup. June's inflation data looked tame because easing US-Iran tensions pushed energy costs down. Then talks collapsed and crude surged nearly 24% in July. An investor looking only at official data saw a calm June print and a Fed leaning on it. An investor watching a nowcast would have seen the July estimate climbing as gasoline and oil climbed. By the time the July CPI was due, with the whisper number at roughly 0.1% for the month and about 3.4% year-over-year, the nowcast had already been telling the story for weeks. The official number mostly confirmed what daily data had signaled, and that confirmation is the whole value of the tool.

Traders use nowcasts in three practical ways. First, as a baseline for the data release: if the nowcast says 3.4% and the consensus says 3.4%, a print near that is a non-event, while a large miss in either direction is the real surprise that moves markets. Second, as a direction signal for rate expectations. With September odds described as a coin flip between holding and hiking, a rising nowcast would nudge a trader toward positioning for a hike through Treasury futures, rate-sensitive stocks, or the dollar. Third, as a cross-check against official narratives. When the nowcast and the headline disagree, that gap is where the opportunity, or the risk, lives.

The video presents nowcasting as one part of a wider dashboard, and the other instruments are worth knowing because each measures something different. Truflation scrapes online prices daily and ran cooler than official CPI for most of the year. The Atlanta Fed's sticky-price CPI, covering items like rent and insurance that rarely fall, ran at 2.8% in June. The Dallas Fed's trimmed mean PCE throws out the most extreme price moves in both directions and read 2.2% over twelve months. The ISM manufacturing prices-paid index hit 71.1 in July, down from 73.0, still signaling factory cost pressure. The 10-year TIPS breakeven, the gap between regular Treasury yields and inflation-protected ones, sat at 2.29%. Each is a different lens; none is "the" answer.

Now the gotchas. A nowcast is an estimate, not a measurement, and it is only as good as its inputs. The Cleveland Fed model leans heavily on energy prices, so it does well when oil and gasoline drive the month and worse when the surprise comes from rent, insurance, used cars, or services. Also, the video's framing that the Fed is "choosing not to use" real-time data goes further than the evidence. Fed staff run their own nowcasts and the Cleveland model is a Fed product, but central banks avoid citing unofficial numbers because they cannot defend them if challenged. Declining to quote a tool is not the same as ignoring it. And note that the alternative gauges often disagree with each other, which is a feature of what they measure, not proof that one is lying.

Nowcasts fail in predictable ways. A sudden shock outside the model's training history, such as a pandemic reopening, a supply-chain break, or a tariff change, can make past relationships useless. Seasonal quirks, survey-collection problems, or methodology changes at the statistics agency can produce official prints that diverge from any reasonable estimate. Online-scraped indexes like Truflation weight things differently from CPI, so a persistent gap may reflect construction rather than insight. The biggest risk is behavioral: treating a smooth daily number as certainty and sizing a bet accordingly. A nowcast that misses by two or three tenths of a point on a month can still flip a market that was positioned for a different outcome.

Nowcasting connects to several ideas you will meet elsewhere. It is the inflation cousin of the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow, which does the same job for economic growth. It leans on data dependence, the Fed's stated practice of waiting for numbers before moving, because the more lagged the numbers, the more a nowcast is worth. It feeds directly into rate-hike and rate-cut probabilities in futures markets, into breakeven rates in the Treasury market, and into the real-yield calculations that drive mortgage and credit card costs. For retirees, it offers an early read on the next Social Security COLA. Used well, it won't replace the official data. It is an early, imperfect read on a number that everyone will see anyway, and the edge comes from knowing how imperfect it is.
