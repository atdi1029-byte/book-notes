IRMAA (Medicare Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount)

IRMAA, pronounced "ER-mah," stands for Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount. It is a surcharge that higher-income Medicare beneficiaries pay on top of the standard premiums for Medicare Part B (doctor visits and outpatient care) and Medicare Part D (prescription drug coverage). Most people picture Medicare as a flat-rate program: everyone enrolled pays the same monthly premium. That is true only up to a point. Once your income crosses certain thresholds, the government asks you to pay more for exactly the same coverage. You get no extra benefits, no better doctors and no faster appointments. It works like a means test attached to a health insurance bill, and it is easy to miss until the letter arrives.

IRMAA matters because it can be a large, sudden and surprising cost. The video puts the numbers in plain terms: the standard Part B premium this year is about $22.90 a month, but cross the income line and it jumps to roughly $284 a month. That is more than twelve times the base amount. On top of that comes a separate surcharge on your drug plan. For a retired couple, one unlucky tax year can add thousands of dollars to the following years' healthcare costs. It matters in the wider retirement picture too, because it quietly eats into the money you planned to live on. A retiree who budgets around the standard premium and then gets hit with IRMAA has a hole in their plan they never saw coming.

Here is the mechanism, step by step. First, Social Security looks at your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI), which is your adjusted gross income plus any tax-exempt interest, such as municipal bond interest. Second, it does not use this year's income. It uses your tax return from two years earlier, which is the heart of the "two-year trap." For 2027 premiums, for example, the agency would look at your 2025 return. Third, it compares that figure to a set of income brackets. Fourth, if you are above the first bracket, it assigns you a higher premium tier. Fifth, it sends a notice, and the extra amount is usually deducted from your Social Security check along with the base premium. The surcharge rises in steps as income climbs through higher tiers.

The video gives the key lines: IRMAA starts above about $109,000 for a single filer and $218,000 for a married couple filing jointly. Now consider how a one-time event plays out. Say a single retiree sells a rental property or a longtime family home and books a big taxable gain. Or they pull a large lump sum from a traditional IRA, or do a big Roth conversion to reduce future taxes. Their income for that one year might spike to $150,000. Two years later, their Medicare premium jumps from $22.90 to around $284 a month, plus the drug-plan surcharge. Their income has long since returned to normal, yet the bill still arrives. As the video puts it:

"One big year selling a house, a large IRA withdrawal, a Roth conversion can show up as a higher Medicare bill 2 years later. Plan those moves with that calendar in mind."

Investors and retirees use IRMAA as a planning constraint, not a market signal. The core skill is managing taxable income across years so you stay below a bracket line. That might mean spreading a Roth conversion over several years instead of doing it all at once, so no single year breaks the threshold. It might mean timing a home sale or the realization of capital gains in a year when other income is low. It might mean drawing from a Roth account, which does not add to MAGI, rather than a traditional IRA in a year that is already high. The video's broader advice applies here: the order and timing of withdrawals can matter as much as the amount. A tax professional can model the tradeoff between paying tax now and paying IRMAA later.

There are several gotchas worth knowing. The first is that the brackets are cliffs, not slopes. Being a single dollar over a threshold can push you into the next tier, so a small amount of income can cost you thousands of dollars in premiums. The second is that the look-back period confuses people, since your premium reflects what you earned long ago, not what you earn now. The third is the common misconception that IRMAA applies only to the wealthy. A single large transaction can lift an otherwise modest retiree across the line. Fourth, the surcharge also hits Part D, not just Part B. And fifth, the hold harmless rule discussed in the video does not shield you here. The video notes it "never protects people who pay the higher income surcharge."

What happens when the system gets it wrong or your life changes? The look-back means IRMAA can be based on income that no longer reflects reality. If you retire, stop working, lose income or go through a major life event such as a death of a spouse or divorce, your current income may be far below the figure Social Security used. In those cases you can file a request to have the premium recalculated, using a form that documents the "life-changing event." Success is not guaranteed, and a one-time gain like selling a house is not a qualifying event on its own. The risk is on the other side too: people who never check their notice may overpay for years. You can also appeal if the underlying tax data was wrong or the income was misreported.

IRMAA connects to several other ideas in the video and in retirement planning. It is a close cousin of the provisional income trap, where the 1983 and 1993 thresholds make more of your Social Security taxable because they were never indexed for inflation. Both are cliff-style rules triggered by income, and a Roth conversion or IRA withdrawal can set off both at once. It also interacts with the COLA. The premium comes out of the check before you see it, so a higher Part B bill shrinks your raise. Finally, it ties into Roth conversions, required minimum distributions and asset location, since all of these shape the taxable income that IRMAA reads two years later. A confident retiree plans all of them as one system.

The practical takeaway is to treat the calendar as part of the plan. Before any large income event, ask three questions. What will this do to my MAGI this year? Which bracket will I land in, and how close will I be to the line? And what will I owe in Medicare premiums two years from now because of it? Check your notice from Social Security every year, compare it to your actual income, and use Medicare open enrollment, which the video notes runs from October 15 through December 7, to review your drug and health coverage. IRMAA is not something you can eliminate, but it is something you can see coming. Seeing it coming two years ahead is the difference between a planned expense and an unpleasant surprise.
