Trading Volume

Trading volume is the number of shares (or contracts, or coins) that change hands during a given period, usually one trading day. If a stock's volume is 748 million shares, that many shares were bought and sold between investors that day. Every trade has two sides: a buyer and a seller. Volume counts the trade once, not twice. Volume is the "small print" at the bottom of every quote screen, while price gets the big bright numbers. The video puts it simply: the price tells you what happened, and the volume tells you how big the crowd was that made it happen. Price alone is a result. Volume adds the context for judging whether that result is believable.

"The price tells you what happened. The volume tells you how big the crowd was that made it happen."

Why does this matter? Because a price is only as meaningful as the amount of trading behind it. Suppose a stock rises 4% because a handful of small orders hit a market with almost no sellers. That tells you very little about what investors broadly think. Suppose the same 4% happens while hundreds of millions of shares trade and institutions are fighting over the price. Then you are looking at a real contest of opinion and capital. Volume affects how easily you can get in and out of a position (liquidity), how much your own order moves the price, and how much weight to give a headline move. It is the closest thing markets offer to a measure of conviction and participation, and it affects everyone from day traders to 401(k) holders.

Mechanically, volume accumulates one trade at a time. A buyer submits an order to buy, say, 500 shares. It is matched against a seller's order to sell 500 shares at an agreed price, and 500 is added to the day's running total. Prices move because the next buyer is willing to pay more than the last trade, or the next seller will accept less. Volume is how many of those matches it took to get there. Large institutions rarely trade in one block, because that would move the price against them. They slice orders into thousands of small pieces across the day, often through algorithms, and market makers trade constantly on both sides. This is why volume tends to be heavy at the open and close and light mid-day, and why it spikes sharply when news arrives.

The video's Nvidia example shows the idea in numbers. After the CEO's resignation announcement, Nvidia opened near $151 (down from a $173.73 close), touched about $148.80, and recovered to close at $166.19, down 4.34%. The headline said small investors saved the day: Vanda Research recorded a record net $111 million of retail buying in the first 30 minutes. But the presenter read the small print. 748 million shares traded Thursday, against 339 million the day before and just 92 million on a quiet day earlier in the month. That is more than double the prior day and roughly eight times a quiet day. This is the signature of a genuine event, and volume made it visible before any commentary did.

Then comes the arithmetic that makes volume useful as a reality check. Price all 748 million shares at the day's absolute low of $148.80, the cheapest any share could possibly have traded, and the day's dollar volume is still roughly $111 billion. Set that against the $111 million of record retail buying, and you get at least $1,000 of Nvidia changing hands for every $1 of that retail buying. The video drives it home with a tug-of-war image: a thousand people on the rope, and the headline handed the trophy to one person because he was the loudest. Even if the retail figure for the full day were 20 times bigger, about $2.2 billion, it would still be around 2% of the day's trading. The other 98% belonged to someone else.

"For every $1 of that record retail buying, at least $1,000 of Nvidia changed hands that day."

Traders use volume in several practical ways. The most common is confirmation: a breakout above resistance on heavy volume is more trustworthy than one on thin volume, because many participants agreed at that price. The reverse, a big price move on weak volume, is often suspect and more likely to fade. Traders compare today's volume to a baseline, such as the 30-day or 50-day average, often called relative volume. Thursday's 748 million is a big number only against a normal day of 92 to 339 million. Volume also marks key price levels. In the video, $148.80 matters because it is where buyers "with real size" stepped in, and whether it holds after earnings tells you whether those buyers still feel the same way.

The presenter lists volume as one of four things to watch around earnings, and the logic is straightforward. After the report, the stock will move, and the question is what kind of move it was. His rule: a big move on a huge crowd means something very different from a big move on a thin one. If Nvidia jumps 8% on volume near the Thursday level of 748 million, pension funds, mutual funds and trading firms are repositioning, and the move has weight. If it moves 8% on a fraction of that, it may simply reflect a lack of sellers at that moment. Earnings reports also land after the close, when volume is thin, so the first reaction can look dramatic and then change once the full market opens. Compare the after-hours reaction with the next regular session.

There are several gotchas. First, volume does not show direction. Heavy volume means a lot of buying and a lot of selling, since every share bought was sold by someone else. The video says it directly: "748 million bought, 748 million sold." Second, headlines about "net buying" by a group are not the same as volume. Net means buys minus sells, so retail's $111 million net purchase implies that larger players on the other side were net sellers to them in that window. Third, a lopsided order count, such as Fidelity's roughly 395,000 buys to 80,000 sells, shows sentiment among those customers but not dollars, since a retail order might be $500 and an institutional order $500 million. Finally, a "stock goes up because buyers outnumber sellers" is a myth: the two are always equal. Prices rise because buyers will pay more.

Volume can also mislead or fail. A high number can come from mechanical events that carry no opinion at all: index rebalancings, options expiration days, and quarterly "triple witching," when funds trade huge blocks to match an index. Dividend capture and tax-driven trades can also inflate it. Thin volume has its own risks. In low-volume stocks, a few orders can move the price a lot and bid-ask spreads widen, so you pay more to get in and receive less to get out. After hours, thin volume means headline moves can reverse by morning. Volume figures can also differ by data source, since some count only trades on certain exchanges, and crypto "wash trading" can fake it. And a stock that splits has its share counts change, so you should compare dollar volume (price times shares) across time.

Volume connects to several other ideas in this guide. It underlies liquidity and market depth, which determine how much it costs to trade. It tells you about market capitalization context: the video computed that about $419 billion of market value came back in one session, and retail's record buying was about 3.03% of that. It is the other half of price action in technical analysis, alongside support and resistance levels. And it helps explain the 2x leveraged funds in the video: the $42.5 billion that flowed into such funds shows where small-investor money was concentrated going into earnings, but the volume numbers show where the real price-setting power sat. The takeaway is to read the price, then read the small print, and ask who was on the other side.

To discuss this confidently in a conversation about markets, hold on to four points. Volume counts shares traded, once per trade, with a buyer and a seller on every one. It gives context: the same price move means different things on a crowded day and a quiet day. It should always be compared to a baseline, and ideally converted to dollars so it can be compared against claims like "retail bought $111 million." And it tells you how many people took part in a move, not which direction they were pushing. When you see a dramatic headline about a stock, ask how many shares traded, how that compares to normal, and who could actually afford to be on the other side of those 748 million shares. The answer is usually the institutions, not the crowd named in the story.
