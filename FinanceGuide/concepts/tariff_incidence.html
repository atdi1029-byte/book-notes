Tariff Incidence (Who Bears the Tariff)

Tariff incidence is the economist's term for who actually ends up paying a tax, as opposed to who is legally required to send the check. A tariff is a tax on imported goods, collected by customs when the goods cross the border. The legal payer is the importer of record, the company whose name is on the customs paperwork. But the legal payer and the economic payer are often different parties. The importer can absorb the cost and earn less, push it back onto the overseas factory by negotiating a lower price, or build it into the shelf price and let shoppers pay. Incidence asks a simple question: after all the adjusting is finished, whose money is actually gone?

This matters because the answer decides who wins and loses from trade policy, and it is rarely the answer a headline implies. When a politician says a foreign country is "paying" the tariff, or a critic says consumers are paying it, each is making a claim about incidence, and each is usually only partly right. For investors, incidence determines whether a tariff shows up in a company's profit margins, in consumer prices and inflation data, or in the finances of foreign suppliers. It also determines who has a claim when a tariff is later struck down. In late 2026, that last point turned into a $126 billion question.

Mechanically, it works like this. A container of sneakers arrives at a U.S. port. Before customs releases it, the importer pays the tariff, say 10% of the declared value. Now the importer has a cost it did not have before and three levers. First, it can absorb the tax, accept a thinner margin, and keep the shelf price unchanged. Second, it can push back on the supplier, demanding a discount on the next order so the factory effectively covers some of the tax. Third, it can pass through the cost by raising the price. Which lever wins depends on bargaining power, competition, and how sensitive shoppers are to price.

What decides the split is elasticity, meaning how easily each side can walk away. If shoppers have close substitutes and will stop buying at a higher price, the retailer cannot raise prices much, so the cost falls back on the retailer and the factory. If the factory has no other buyers and its plant sits idle without U.S. orders, it will cut its price to keep the business. If instead the product is a necessity, or every competitor faces the same tariff, shoppers have nowhere to go and the tax flows through to the register. The side with fewer options pays more. That is why the split differs by product, by store, and even by month.

The video gives a concrete case. The administration imposed tariffs on nearly everything the U.S. imports under an emergency law. On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court ruled 6 to 3 that the law did not give a president the power to set tariffs. Roughly $166 billion had been collected from about 330,000 importers. Customs opened a refund portal in April, and by early October about $126 billion, including interest, had been sent for payment. Every dollar went to the importer of record. Shoppers who may have paid part of that tax in higher prices had no form to file and no standing to receive anything.

"A tariff is a tax paid by the company that brings the goods into the country. That company has three choices. Eat it and earn less, push it back on the factory overseas, or build it into the price. Most do some of each and no receipt ever shows you the split."

That last sentence is the core problem. Nothing at the register says "$1.20 of this price is tariff." The tariff is blended into a price that also reflects wages, freight, rent, and the retailer's profit target. So a refund can legitimately land with the importer even when shoppers carried part of the burden, because nobody can prove how much. Retailers, meanwhile, had a choice about what to do with the money. The video shows several outcomes. Abercrombie and Fitch booked about $120 million of refund benefit in operating income, and its stock jumped almost 20%. Dollar Tree reported $372.4 million of refunds plus $14.4 million of interest, and spent about $103 million on reinvestment such as lower price points and marketing, plus $40 million on donations. Best Buy booked about $41 million and said not to expect it again.

For investors, incidence is a lens for reading earnings. The practical skill is separating one-time items from the underlying business. A refund is found money, and it inflates profit for the quarter without saying anything about next year. The video points to the line that matters: profit without the refund. Dollar Tree's raised forecast includes about 65 cents a share from refunds, and Abercrombie's about $2.15. Next year that money is gone, but the tariff is not. An investor should ask who absorbed the tariff in the first place, whether the company has pricing power to pass it on now, and what the margin looks like once the windfall disappears. Companies with strong brands can pass costs through. Companies with thin margins and price-sensitive customers get squeezed.

There are several common misconceptions. The first is that "importers pay, so importers bear the cost." Legal liability is not economic burden, and the importer may have passed most of it along. The opposite error is just as common: "shoppers paid, so shoppers are owed." Plenty of importers say they never raised prices and simply ate the tariff, in which case the refund is their own money coming home. Costco faces four class action lawsuits over exactly this question, and they are still being fought. A third mistake is assuming one answer fits everyone. Incidence varies by company, product, and time. A fourth is assuming a tariff ended just because the law behind it did. The tariff itself was rebuilt, as the next section explains.

Incidence also breaks down in ways that create risk. If a retailer passes costs to shoppers and later keeps the refund, it is paid twice, once at the register and again by the government, which is the claim made in court. If the retailer ate the tax and then cuts prices to share the refund, it is giving away money it never took from shoppers. Measuring any of it is nearly impossible because prices move for many reasons at once. There is also policy risk. The refund came from an emergency law the court struck down, but the replacements use different authority: a temporary 150-day surcharge, then Section 301 tariffs of 10% or 12.5% on 60 trading partners, and now proposed rates of up to 15% to 35% on 14 economies. Section 301 requires investigations and hearings, and the court case never touched it, so a second refund is unlikely. The video puts it plainly: the refund was paid once, the tariff was rebuilt three times.

Tariff incidence connects to several other ideas. It is a case of pricing power: the firm that can pass costs along protects its margins, and the firm that cannot loses profit. It feeds into inflation, since pass-through to shelf prices shows up in consumer price data. It connects to earnings quality, because refunds are non-recurring and flatter the numbers. It relates to supply chains and currency moves, since a foreign factory can offset a tariff by cutting prices, and a weaker foreign currency can do some of the same work. The dates to watch in the video are comments due January 5 and hearings starting January 25, where retailers say aloud what the tariffs do to prices, and the first Costco ruling on whether a shopper has any claim at all.
