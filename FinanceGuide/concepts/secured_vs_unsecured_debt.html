Secured vs. Unsecured Debt

Every loan answers one question before it answers any other: if the borrower can't pay, what can the lender take? Secured debt is borrowing backed by collateral, a specific asset the lender has a legal claim on. A mortgage is secured by the house, and a car loan by the car. If you stop paying, the lender can seize and sell that asset to recover its money. Unsecured debt has no such claim. Credit cards, personal loans, and most corporate bonds rest on one thing, the borrower's promise to pay, plus whatever general claim the lender has on the borrower's remaining assets. The difference sounds technical, but it sets the price of nearly every loan in the economy, because it decides how much a lender stands to lose.

This matters because the interest rate on a loan is mostly a price for risk, and collateral cuts the risk. A lender holding a claim on something valuable can be repaid even when the borrower fails, so it accepts a lower rate. A lender with only a promise needs more yield to compensate for the chance of losing a large part of its money. That is why mortgage rates sit far below credit card rates, and why the same company can pay very different rates on different pieces of its debt. For investors, the distinction reaches beyond bond portfolios. It shapes how much a company's financing costs, how much of its cash flow goes to interest, and how much cushion is left for stockholders if something goes wrong.

Mechanically, secured lending works through a legal claim called a lien. The borrower pledges specific assets, such as equipment, property, receivables, or a contract, and the loan documents give the lender first right to them if the borrower defaults. Lenders also set how much they will advance against the collateral's value, and they do not lend a full dollar against a dollar of assets. Unsecured lenders get no lien. In a bankruptcy they join the pool of general creditors and share whatever remains after secured claims are satisfied. Their only protection is the borrower's creditworthiness, plus contract terms called covenants that limit what the borrower can do, such as taking on too much new secured debt ahead of them.

The order in which claims get paid in a bankruptcy is the capital structure or "waterfall." Secured lenders come first, up to the value of their collateral. Unsecured creditors, including bondholders and trade suppliers, come next and split what's left in proportion to their claims. Equity holders, the stockholders, come last, and they are paid only if every creditor has been paid in full. The video put it in restaurant terms: the secured lenders are the regulars with a table, the unsecured bondholders are the walk-ins standing in the rain, and the stockholders are "still looking for parking." The further back you stand, the more you lose when the pie shrinks, and the more yield you demand for standing there.

CoreWeave, the AI cloud company, is a clean example because it sat in both places in the same year. In March it closed an $8.5 billion loan that Moody's rated A3, which is investment grade. It priced at roughly 5.9% fixed and was secured by the chips themselves and by a long-term contract with a big-name customer, reportedly Meta. In June it sold $1.25 billion of unsecured bonds due 2032 with a 9⅝% coupon. By the Wednesday the stock fell about 15.5%, those bonds were yielding about 14.8%. Oracle's bond maturing the same year yielded about 7.24%. That is nearly nine percentage points between two kinds of CoreWeave debt, with the same company, the same chips, and the same year.

"Cheap lenders were never really lending to the neo cloud, they were lending to its customers' signatures."

That quote carries the main lesson. The 5.9% lender was not betting on CoreWeave's long-term success. It was relying on a contract signed by a far richer company, and on chips it could take if payments stopped. The secured lender's repayment came from the customer's creditworthiness and from hard assets, so CoreWeave's own weakness mattered much less. The unsecured bondholder had none of that. The bond's price fell as the yield rose to 14.8%, and that was the market saying it knows exactly where it stands in line and wants to be paid for the wait. The video made the same point, calling the yield "not the market saying AI is a fad."

Investors use this in several practical ways. First, compare yields on a company's secured and unsecured debt, because the gap measures how worried the market is about the company itself. A narrow gap suggests comfort and a wide one suggests that lenders expect losses if things go wrong. Second, watch bond yields as an early warning for the stock. The video's point was that stockholders "caught up to what the lenders had been saying all along." Bond investors read covenants and balance sheets for a living, and their repricing often leads equity. Third, ask where you sit. A stockholder is behind every creditor, so a company paying 15% to stay funded is a company whose shareholders carry the most exposed claim.

There are gotchas. "Secured" does not mean "safe." In August, CoreWeave arranged a $2.6 billion secured loan rated below investment grade. It was first marketed at about 4.25 percentage points over the benchmark rate, demand was soft, the price rose to 5.5 points over, and it still sold at a discount. Collateral helps only if it holds its value. Computer chips lose value as new generations arrive, so a lender with a claim on aging hardware may recover far less than the loan balance. Also, the cheap loan was an exception. Of $35.1 billion in total debt, about $31.4 billion was recourse debt, meaning the whole company is on the hook. Only about $3.7 billion was non-recourse, where lenders can go after only the specific pledged assets. The $8.5 billion facility was also draw-as-you-go, so the money arrives only as data centers get built.

When things break, the order of the waterfall becomes real. A company that cannot pay its interest or refinance its maturing debt defaults, and the creditors fight over what remains. Pledged collateral goes to the secured lenders first. If it is worth less than their claims, they take the loss and become unsecured creditors for the shortfall. Anything left flows to unsecured creditors, usually at cents on the dollar, and stockholders are typically wiped out. The video recalled the telecom bust: ICG Communications filed for bankruptcy in November 2000 with about $2.81 billion of liabilities against $2.79 billion of assets, and almost half of the publicly traded upstart phone companies failed between 2000 and fall 2002. Refinancing risk also bites. CoreWeave reported about $7.5 billion of debt due within 12 months of June 30, and when rates rise, replacing that debt costs more every time.

This concept connects to several others. Credit spreads, the extra yield a borrower pays over a safe benchmark like Treasuries, are the market's price for position in line and default risk. Investment grade versus high yield is partly a label for how safe the lenders think they are, and a secured or contract-backed loan can earn an investment-grade rating even when the borrower's unsecured bonds would not. Interest coverage matters too. CoreWeave's second-quarter interest expense was $640 million on about $2.6 billion of revenue, roughly a quarter of every revenue dollar. Finally, leverage ties it together, since a company that finances growth with debt carries fixed bills that arrive every quarter whether or not the data center is finished. A backlog of $104 billion is a promise of future revenue, but interest is due on schedule. Knowing the waterfall lets you read a company's financing as clearly as its revenue.
