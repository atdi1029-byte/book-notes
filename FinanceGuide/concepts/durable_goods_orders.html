Durable Goods Orders

Durable Goods Orders is a monthly U.S. government report, released by the Census Bureau, that tracks new orders placed with domestic manufacturers for goods expected to last three years or longer. Think airplanes, cars, refrigerators, industrial machinery, semiconductors, and military equipment — big-ticket items businesses and consumers don't buy on a whim. Because these are expensive, planned purchases, the report is treated as a forward-looking gauge of economic confidence. A company doesn't order a new fleet of trucks or a factory's worth of equipment unless it expects demand to justify the expense. That makes durable goods orders one of the cleanest windows into how businesses actually feel about the future, as opposed to how consumers feel about it, which is captured elsewhere.

The report matters because it sits upstream of a lot of what eventually shows up in GDP, employment, and corporate earnings. If orders are rising, factories ramp up production, hire workers, and place orders with their own suppliers — a ripple effect through the industrial economy. If orders are falling, that's often an early warning that businesses are pulling back on capital spending, which tends to precede slower hiring and weaker earnings in manufacturing-heavy sectors. Traders and the Federal Reserve both watch it because it's a real-economy data point, not a survey of sentiment — it reflects actual contracts and commitments, which makes it harder to fake and more useful as a check on whether the "story" the market is telling itself matches what businesses are actually doing with their money.

Mechanically, the Census Bureau surveys thousands of manufacturers each month and tallies the dollar value of new orders received for durable goods, compared to the prior month. The headline number is usually reported as a percentage change, month-over-month. Because a single volatile category — commercial aircraft orders from Boeing — can swing the entire headline number wildly (one big Boeing order can add several percentage points), analysts almost always look at "core" durable goods orders, which strips out both defense and aircraft. This core number, sometimes called "nondefense capital goods orders excluding aircraft," is considered the cleanest read on underlying business investment, because it removes the lumpy, one-off nature of plane and military contracts.

In the transcript, this report is flagged as one of the key events on next week's calendar — due out on Friday, the same day as the final University of Michigan consumer sentiment reading. The host, Mark Malik, frames it alongside other market-moving events that week: the Treasury auctioning 2-year, 5-year, and 7-year notes, Xi Jinping's state visit to Washington, and Costco's earnings. At that specific moment, the backdrop is a Fed that just hiked rates to a 3.75%–4% range with the 10-year Treasury yield sitting right at 5% amid an oil shock. In that environment, a durable goods report becomes more than routine — it's a real-time signal of whether business investment is holding up despite $100+ oil and a Fed unwilling to promise a stopping point.

Traders and investors use durable goods orders primarily as a business-investment thermometer. A strong core reading (rising orders excluding aircraft and defense) signals that companies are still confident enough to spend on equipment and expansion, which supports the case for continued economic strength — and, in a hawkish Fed environment like the one described in the transcript, can actually reinforce the case for more hikes, since it's evidence the economy can absorb higher rates. A weak reading, especially several months in a row, is often read as an early crack in the economic outlook, sometimes showing up in industrial and manufacturing stocks before it hits broader indices. Because the report is released with a lag (it covers activity from weeks earlier), it's used less as a real-time trading trigger and more as a confirmation or contradiction of the broader growth narrative the market has already been pricing in.

The biggest gotcha with this report is the aircraft distortion mentioned above. Boeing orders are enormous relative to almost everything else in the survey, and a single large order — or the cancellation of one — can make the headline number look dramatically stronger or weaker than the actual state of business investment. Anyone reading the headline "durable goods orders jumped 5%" without checking whether that was aircraft-driven is at risk of drawing the wrong conclusion entirely. This is exactly the kind of nuance the transcript itself warns about elsewhere with retail sales — Malik points out that August retail sales were "not adjusted for inflation" and that gas station sales were up 21% from a year ago, meaning part of the "strong" headline was just higher prices, not more actual buying. The same discipline applies to durable goods: always check what's driving the number before trusting the headline.

Durable goods data can also be revised significantly in subsequent months as more complete survey responses come in, which means the first released figure is sometimes materially wrong. This matters because markets often react to the initial print, and then have to partially unwind that reaction weeks later when the revision lands. The report can also "break" as a clean signal during periods of severe supply-chain disruption or extreme uncertainty, like the 2020 pandemic or major geopolitical shocks (an oil supply disruption like the one described in the transcript, where drone strikes hit Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline and pushed Brent crude toward $110). In those environments, businesses may place or cancel orders based on supply availability rather than genuine confidence in future demand, muddying the signal the report is supposed to provide.

Durable goods orders connect directly to several other concepts covered elsewhere in the market recap: it's a leading indicator, much like the University of Michigan sentiment survey it's released alongside, but where sentiment measures how people feel, durable goods measures what businesses are actually committing money to — the difference between talk and action. It also ties into the Fed's dual mandate calculus.

"That's a Fed looking at a strong economy and a hot inflation at the same time. And that is exactly the combination that justifies more hikes." — Mark Malik

A resilient durable goods report in that kind of environment would feed the same hawkish logic — evidence the economy can take more tightening without breaking, which keeps upward pressure on yields like the 10-year sitting at 5%, and keeps the "hawks" Malik references (in the context of the October Fed meeting) armed with another data point.
