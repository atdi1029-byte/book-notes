Existing Home Sales

Existing home sales count how many previously owned homes (single-family houses, condos, and co-ops) change hands in a given period. In the U.S. the headline number comes from the National Association of Realtors (NAR), which publishes it monthly. A sale is counted at closing, when the deed transfers and the money moves, not when a contract is signed. The figure is quoted as a seasonally adjusted annual rate (SAAR). That means NAR takes the month's pace, smooths out the usual spring surge and winter lull, and states it as if the pace held for twelve months. A reading of "3.8 million" means the market is closing deals at a speed that would produce 3.8 million sales over a year.

This number matters because a home sale starts a chain of spending that reaches far past the buyer and seller. Agents, mortgage lenders, appraisers, inspectors, title and escrow companies, and movers all get paid at closing. Then come the purchases that follow: paint, flooring, appliances, furniture, landscaping, and repairs. The video puts the ancillary spending at over $25,000 per transaction, and even if that figure is a rough estimate, the direction is right. Housing also affects labor mobility, since people who can't sell can't easily take jobs elsewhere. Economists therefore treat existing home sales as a read on consumer confidence, credit conditions, and the "velocity" of the housing economy. Housing is the largest asset most households own, so when sales stall, many other things stall with them.

Mechanically, a sale needs a willing seller, a willing buyer, and a price and financing arrangement both can live with. NAR gathers closing data from local multiple listing services and real estate associations, estimates the national total, and adjusts it for seasonality. Because it measures closings, it lags the market: a deal that closes in April usually went under contract in February or March. That is why analysts watch pending home sales, which count signed contracts, as a leading indicator. Sellers decide whether to list based on what they would give up by selling, and buyers decide based on the monthly payment they can afford. When either side's math breaks, listings dry up or buyers disappear, and the sales count drops. The headline number is the combined result of millions of individual decisions.

The video builds its case on this figure. It says existing home sales have fallen to an annualized pace near 3.8 million, a level last seen in 1995, when the U.S. had roughly 266 million people instead of about 340 million. That comparison is the point: a larger country is moving fewer homes. The video's worked example shows why. A homeowner with a $320,000 loan at 3% pays about $1,349 a month in principal and interest. Replacing it with the same loan at 6.85% costs about $2,097, which is $748 more every month. Add roughly $300 more in reassessed property tax and $250 more in insurance, and the monthly cost of moving to an identical house rises by about $1,298. Many owners decide not to list.

"We're transacting fewer residential homes per year today with 340 million citizens than we processed in 1995."

This is the rate lock-in effect, and it is the mechanism behind the sales slump. The video says about 85 million homeowners hold a first mortgage below 5%, and nearly 48 million are under 3.5%. Because mortgages are generally not portable to a new home, selling means giving up a cheap loan and taking a new one at today's rates. So fewer owners list their homes, and the supply of existing homes for sale stays thin. Thin supply keeps prices firm even though few buyers can afford them, and high prices combined with high rates keep buyers out. The result is a market with low volume but sticky prices. This differs from 2008, when forced sellers pushed prices down. Here the sellers can stay put, so they do.

Investors and traders read existing home sales alongside a handful of other data points. A rising trend, especially when pending sales lead it higher, tends to help homebuilders, mortgage lenders, title insurers, and home-improvement retailers such as hardware and flooring chains. A falling trend suggests weaker spending in those same groups. Watch months of supply, which is the current inventory divided by the monthly sales pace. Around six months is considered balanced, and lower numbers favor sellers. Compare the median sales price to its year-ago level, and watch mortgage rates, since sales often respond to moves in the 30-year rate with a lag of a month or two. For the Fed and for bond investors, a sustained rebound in sales is evidence that rates are low enough to unfreeze the economy, and persistent weakness is evidence that policy is restrictive.

There are several gotchas. First, the headline is a monthly annualized estimate, and it gets revised and bounces around, so one month proves little. Be careful comparing it with calendar-year totals, which are lower-variance and may differ from a given month's SAAR; always check which one is being quoted. Second, comparing today with 1995 without adjusting for population is a legitimate argument, but it is incomplete. Today's housing stock, household count, and turnover rates are all different, and sales as a share of homes owned is a fairer yardstick. Third, the video's "$42 billion drained" figure multiplies an estimated 1.7 million missing sales by $25,000. That is an illustration, not a measured loss, and those who wouldn't have moved may still spend on renovations. Fourth, existing sales exclude new construction, which is tracked separately.

When the market stays frozen, the costs spread. Sellers who need to move for a job, a parent's care, or a growing family face a hard choice between accepting a higher payment and staying where they are, and the video argues that many stay, which reduces labor mobility. The same lock-in extends into finance. Low-coupon mortgage bonds were priced assuming people would sell or refinance every seven years or so, but with prepayments near zero their effective life stretches toward twenty-five years. Holders, including banks sitting on unrealized losses, are stuck waiting for principal that comes back slowly. Frozen markets can also end abruptly. A sustained drop in rates, a recession that forces sales through job loss, or a wave of life events like death, divorce, and relocation can release pent-up supply and move prices quickly in either direction.

Existing home sales connect to many other concepts in this guide. The mortgage rate and the spread over Treasury yields set the monthly payment that drives the lock-in. Prepayment speed and duration (also called extension risk) explain how a slow housing market changes the value of agency mortgage-backed securities. Unrealized held-to-maturity losses at regional banks follow from the same low-coupon holdings. Pending home sales, housing starts, and the Case-Shiller index are the neighboring housing indicators, and home equity lines of credit are the workaround many owners try instead of selling. The video's practical advice, such as weighing a HELOC against your blended rate or renting out the home, is aimed at owners who stay put. Knowing the data helps you tell a cyclical slump from a structural freeze.
