Treasury Futures

A Treasury future is a standardized contract, traded on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, to buy or sell a US government bond at a set price on a set date in the future. You agree on the price today and settle later. The common contracts are named by the bond's maturity: the 2-year, 5-year, 10-year, Ultra 10-year, and 30-year "bond" and "Ultra bond" futures. Each contract represents $100,000 of face value (the 2-year uses $200,000). You never need to put up the full $100,000. You post a small deposit called margin, usually a few percent of the contract's value, and the exchange marks your account to market every day. That makes a future a way to own bond price exposure without owning the bond.

It matters because Treasury yields are the base layer of the financial system. The 10-year yield sets the reference point for mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs, and the discount rate used to value stocks. Futures are where much of the trading in that market actually happens. They are liquid around the clock, cheap to trade, and easy to scale. When the video said the 10-year hit about 5.26% and the 30-year hit 5.59%, a large part of that price discovery took place in futures pits and screens. So the pension fund, the mutual fund, and the hedge fund all connect through this one instrument.

Mechanically, a future moves opposite to yields. If you are "long" a 10-year future, you profit when bond prices rise and yields fall. You lose when yields rise. Say you buy one contract at 110 (meaning 110% of face value, or $110,000). If it falls to 109, you lose $1,000, and your broker takes that cash from your margin account that evening. If it rises to 111, you collect $1,000. Money changes hands every day, so losses are paid in cash as they happen instead of accumulating on paper. This is what separates a future from simply holding a bond, where a loss stays unrealized until you sell.

There is a quirk in what you actually deliver. A future does not reference one bond. It allows a basket of eligible Treasuries, and the seller chooses which to hand over, usually the one that is cheapest to deliver. Because those bonds have different coupons and maturities, the exchange applies a conversion factor to make them comparable. Nearly all positions never reach delivery. Traders close them or "roll" them into the next quarterly contract, since contracts expire in March, June, September, and December. Still, the possibility of delivery is what ties the future's price to the cash bond market. If the two drift apart, someone can profit by trading the gap.

Now the video's central point: why would big institutions use futures instead of bonds? The speaker gave two reasons. First, futures are more liquid. You can buy several billion dollars of duration in one transaction without moving the price much. Second, mutual fund prospectuses often limit repo borrowing, which is the usual way to finance a bond position. A futures contract has financing built in. The cost of carrying the bond is baked into the price. So the fund gets leverage-like exposure without technically borrowing. A pension that needs more duration, meaning more sensitivity to interest rates, can add it in a day without selling stocks or other assets.

That demand has a visible side effect. When many pensions and mutual funds all want to buy futures rather than cash bonds, they push the future's price above where the cash bonds say it should be, a premium. The video put the average spread at roughly five basis points, or five hundredths of one percent. That sounds tiny, and for a retail investor it is. But for a hedge fund it is a clear, repeatable signal. Hedge funds sell the expensive future, buy the cheaper cash bond, and wait for the two to converge at expiration. That is the basis trade, and Treasury futures are one half of it.

"Futures make a mutual fund look cheaper than it actually is."

This is the hidden-cost point, and it is easy to misread. When a fund holds a cash bond and borrows through repo, the interest it pays shows up as an expense. When it holds a future, the same financing cost is embedded in the futures price, so it never appears in the reported expense ratio. The video cited a Chicago Fed finding that this makes funds look cheaper than they are. The fund is paying the cost, just in a place that expense disclosure does not look. So if two bond funds show similar fees and one uses futures heavily, the futures-heavy fund may be carrying a financing drag you cannot see in the fee table. Look at the holdings and turnover, not just the headline fee.

Here is how the numbers in the video fit together. A 5 basis point basis is too small to matter unless it is multiplied. So hedge funds buy cash bonds, repo them out for cash, and buy more. The video cited a New York Fed finding that 75% of bilateral repo trades carry a 0% haircut, meaning no collateral cushion. It put aggregate hedge fund leverage on this financing at about 56 to 1, roughly $553 billion in positions against $9.8 billion of equity. At that ratio, a move of under two percent against the position would wipe out the equity. This is why the headline of "50 to 1 leverage" sounds alarming. The video added that this is leverage on one trade's financing, and that fund-level leverage for large traders is closer to 17 to 1.

Investors use Treasury futures in a few practical ways. Pensions and funds use them to adjust duration quickly and cheaply. Banks and corporations use them to hedge interest rate risk, for example a company that expects to issue debt can short futures to lock in today's rates. Traders use them to bet on the direction of rates, since a small margin deposit controls a large position. Macro investors watch futures positioning as a signal of what big players expect. And relative-value funds use the spread between futures and cash. The video suggested that spread is now shrinking. It said the trade "stopped actually paying," so funds are unwinding it, and some of the leverage has moved elsewhere, into swap spread trades that have no public positioning reports.

There are common misconceptions. The first is that a future is free leverage with no cost. The cost exists, but it is embedded in the price instead of charged as an interest line. The second is that a premium in futures means they are a better buy. The premium comes from demand pressure, and it is exactly what the arbitrageur is paid to remove. The third is that margin equals risk. A $3,000 margin deposit on a $100,000 contract does not cap your loss at $3,000. Your loss is the full price move, and you must keep adding cash. The fourth is that futures positions are always visible. Futures are reported to regulators, but the wider system of cash bonds, repo, and swaps does not appear in one place. This is the video's point about leverage migrating to where it cannot be seen.

Futures break in a few ways. The most direct is a margin spiral. When yields jump, as the video noted, the 10-year went from 4.96% to 5.26% in five sessions, and the MOVE index rose from 75.3 to 101.8 in a month. The exchange often raises margin requirements when volatility rises. Funds must then post more cash on short notice, and some sell Treasuries to raise it. Those sales push yields up further and trigger more margin calls. The basis trade can also fail on the cash side. If repo lenders cut financing or raise haircuts, funds are forced to sell bonds and buy back futures at the same time, which is exactly what happened in March 2020, when the Fed had to step in to restore order.

Treasury futures connect to several other ideas. They link to the basis trade and repo haircuts, which supply the leverage. They link to duration, which is what the pensions are buying. They link to the MOVE index, which is the market's gauge of bond volatility and a key input to margin levels. And they link to swap spreads, where the video says the leverage has gone. The practical takeaway is to treat the futures market as a pipeline. Pension demand pushes prices up, hedge funds arbitrage the gap with borrowed money, and the whole structure works while financing is cheap and calm. It is fragile when volatility rises, financing costs jump, or the spread no longer pays.

For an ordinary investor, the lessons are simple. Rising yields hurt bond funds, and the video's advice to avoid long-duration funds follows from that. A fund that uses futures can look cheaper than it is, so read how it gets its exposure. And when you hear "50 to 1 leverage," ask what is being leveraged and how it is financed. The video's conclusion was that the basis trade is not blowing up today. It is being wound down because it stopped paying. Whether the risk has gone away or only moved to a less visible place is the question to keep asking.
