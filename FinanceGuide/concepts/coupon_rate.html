Coupon Rate (Low-Coupon Debt)

A coupon rate is the fixed annual interest rate a bond issuer promises to pay, expressed as a percentage of the bond's face value. If a company sells a bond with a $1,000 face value and a 2.5% coupon, it pays $25 of interest every year (usually split into two $12.50 payments) until the bond matures, when it hands back the $1,000 principal. The word "coupon" is a leftover from the days when bond certificates had literal paper coupons that holders clipped and mailed in to collect payment. The key idea is that the rate is set once, at issuance, and then never changes. Low-coupon debt simply means bonds issued when rates were low, so the company locked in a cheap price for borrowed money.

Coupon rates matter because they determine what debt actually costs a company, and that cost flows straight into profits, dividends, hiring, and survival. Interest is a fixed obligation: it must be paid whether sales are strong or weak, and bondholders get paid before shareholders see a dollar. A company with a 2.5% coupon has a very different financial life from an identical company paying 8%. This is why the video treats 2020-21 borrowing as a ticking clock. Cheap debt is a gift while it lasts, but it carries a hidden expiration date, because eventually the principal comes due and the company has to borrow again at whatever the market charges that day.

Mechanically, the coupon rate is set when the bond is sold, and it reflects two things: the benchmark government rate at that moment, and a credit spread, the extra yield investors demand for the risk that this particular company might not pay. In 2020-21, central banks held rates near zero, so even mid-quality companies could issue three- and five-year bonds at 2% to 3%. The coupon is then frozen. Years later, when the bond matures, the company must repay principal in cash. Most do not have that cash sitting around, so they refinance: they sell new bonds and use the proceeds to pay off the old ones. The new bonds carry a coupon reflecting today's rates and today's spread, not the old ones.

The video's example makes the arithmetic concrete. A mid-size industrial or consumer company carries $500 million in debt at a 2.5% coupon, costing $12.5 million a year in interest. Refinance that same $500 million at 8.5%, in the middle of the 7.5% to 9% range the host cites, and the annual bill becomes $42.5 million. The principal has not grown by a penny, yet the company now burns an extra $30 million of cash every year. The host's headline figure, $1.8 trillion of corporate paper maturing between late 2026 and the end of 2027, is that same math multiplied across thousands of issuers.

"Instead of rolling over a $100 million note at 2.5%, the refinancing yield today sits between 7.5 and 9%."

Investors use this concept as a screening tool. The most useful habit is reading the debt maturity schedule in a company's 10-K filing, which lists how much debt comes due each year. A company with most of its debt maturing in 2026-27 at old low coupons faces a coming step-up in costs; a company whose debt matures in 2035, or which already refinanced, does not. Pair that with the interest coverage ratio (operating earnings divided by interest expense). The video suggests wanting at least three to one. A company at 4x coverage today could drop below 2x after refinancing, which is the signal that dividends, buybacks, or jobs may be cut.

There are real nuances the video glosses over. First, a company does not owe the new rate on all its debt at once; only the maturing slice reprices, so the pain arrives in stages as each bond comes due. Second, many companies spread maturities in a "ladder," so no single year is a cliff. Third, cash-rich firms can simply repay from reserves, and they even earn higher interest on that cash, so rising rates can help them. Fourth, the 7.5% to 9% range applies mainly to lower-rated borrowers. Investment-grade companies refinance at much lower spreads, so applying the worst-case rate to "corporate America" overstates the typical increase. The risk is concentrated in weaker, highly leveraged names.

When refinancing fails, the consequences cascade. A company that cannot roll its debt at a tolerable price faces a sequence of choices, each worse than the last. It can cut the dividend, since shareholders rank behind lenders. It can freeze hiring, cancel bonuses, or lay off staff. It can sell assets, often at bad prices. If earnings fall far enough, it can breach covenants, the financial tests written into loan agreements, which lets lenders demand repayment or force a restructuring. The end of the line is a Chapter 11 bankruptcy, where shareholders are usually wiped out or heavily diluted. The video's "zombie company" label describes firms whose operating cash does not even cover current interest, leaving them dependent on lenders' willingness to keep rolling the debt.

The video's "expired teaser rate" analogy captures the emotional logic well: a family on a 0% introductory credit card feels rich until the promotion ends and the rate resets to 24%. The comparison is imperfect, because a corporate bond's rate does not reset automatically. It stays fixed until maturity, and that fixed rate is precisely what protected companies for several years. The danger sits at the maturity date, not before it. Still, the analogy gets the lesson right: the cheap period was temporary, and the lifestyle (in the company's case, buybacks and expansion) was built on the assumption that it would last.

Coupon rate connects to several other ideas worth knowing. Yield to maturity is what a bond actually earns at its current market price, and it is the number that becomes the new coupon on fresh issuance. Bond prices move opposite to yields, so old 2.5% bonds trade below face value when new ones pay 8%. The interest coverage ratio and debt-to-EBITDA measure whether a company can bear the new cost. The maturity wall describes many bonds expiring in a short window. And credit spreads explain why two companies refinancing on the same day pay different rates. Understanding these together lets you read a balance sheet the way a credit analyst does: not just what a company owes, but when, and at what price it must borrow next.
