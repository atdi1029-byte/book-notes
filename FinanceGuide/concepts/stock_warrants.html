Stock Warrants

A stock warrant is a contract that gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy shares of a company at a fixed price — called the strike price — before some expiration date. If that sounds like a stock option, that's because the two are close cousins. The critical difference is who's on the other side of the trade. Options that trade on exchanges are contracts between two investors; when one investor exercises a call, another investor (or a market maker) has to deliver the shares. A warrant, by contrast, is issued directly by the company itself. When a warrant is exercised, the company creates brand new shares and sells them to the warrant holder at the strike price. That means warrants are dilutive — they increase the total share count — in a way that ordinary options trading never does.

Warrants matter because they've become a favorite tool for structuring deals between companies, not just a way for retail traders to speculate. When one company wants another to commit real money, real engineering time, or real supply chain effort to a partnership, cash payment alone doesn't always align incentives. A warrant lets Company A hand Company B a stake in its future stock price performance instead of, or in addition to, cash. If the deal succeeds and the stock goes up, the warrant holder profits handsomely. If the deal flops, the warrant can expire worthless. This turns the warrant into a way of saying "we're all in this together" — the supplier's upside becomes directly tied to the customer's success, which is exactly why they show up so often in supply agreements, financings, and strategic partnerships rather than in ordinary retail trading.

Mechanically, a warrant deal works in stages. First, the issuing company and the recipient agree on a strike price, a total share count, and an expiration date, all spelled out in a warrant agreement. Second — and this is where warrants get interesting in modern corporate deals — the company often attaches vesting conditions, meaning the warrants don't all become exercisable immediately. Instead, tranches of warrants unlock only as specific milestones are hit: a certain dollar amount spent, a delivery date met, a performance target achieved. Third, once vested, the holder can exercise at any point before expiration by paying the strike price per share, at which point the company issues new stock. If the holder never exercises — because the stock price never rises above the strike, making exercise unprofitable — the warrant simply expires and nothing happens.

In the week the Fed hiked rates to 3.75%–4% and the 10-year Treasury touched 5%, Generac Holdings (GNRC) surged 18.34% in a single day after announcing an up to $8 billion deal to supply industrial backup generators for Amazon's data centers, with $2.4 billion of initial deliveries scheduled for 2027 and 2028. As part of that agreement, Amazon received warrants to buy nearly 1.7 million Generac shares at a strike price of $200.93. Crucially, "most of those warrants vest only as Amazon's payments hit delivery milestones." That structure means Amazon doesn't get a free option on Generac stock just for signing a contract — Amazon has to actually follow through on the purchases, milestone by milestone, before it can cash in on any Generac stock appreciation. The warrants are the carrot that keeps both sides honest.

For investors and analysts, warrants attached to a corporate deal are a signal worth reading carefully. When you see a company issue warrants as part of a supply or partnership agreement, ask what the vesting structure implies. Milestone-based vesting, like in the Amazon-Generac deal, tells you the counterparty isn't just making a promise — its future stock upside is conditioned on performance the market can eventually verify through earnings reports, delivery announcements, or capital expenditure disclosures. Traders also watch the strike price itself as a marker of where a sophisticated counterparty thinks the stock is headed; Amazon didn't get warrants struck near GNRC's pre-announcement price, it got them at $200.93, a level that only pays off if the stock climbs meaningfully above where it traded before the deal was announced.

A common misconception is treating warrants the same way you'd treat listed call options — assuming they trade with the same liquidity, are priced purely off Black-Scholes-style models, and have no effect on the underlying company's share count. None of that holds. Warrants are usually illiquid or non-tradable altogether when issued privately as part of a commercial deal, as in Amazon's case. And because exercising a warrant creates new shares rather than transferring existing ones, large warrant grants are a form of potential future dilution for existing shareholders — something that shows up in a company's fully diluted share count and can matter a great deal when modeling per-share earnings years down the road, even if the near-term stock reaction is positive, as it was for Generac.

Warrants can also fail or underdeliver in specific ways worth knowing. If the vesting milestones are never hit — say Amazon scales back its data center generator orders, or the 2027-28 delivery timeline slips indefinitely — the warrants simply never vest, and the arrangement quietly evaporates without benefiting either side. Even if warrants do vest, if the stock never trades above the strike price before expiration, they expire worthless, just like an out-of-the-money call option. There's also counterparty risk in reverse: because the issuing company is directly on the hook to deliver shares, a company that grants too many warrants across multiple deals can end up with a share count that balloons faster than investors expected, pressuring the stock even if the underlying business is performing well — dilution that shows up gradually rather than all at once.

Warrants connect naturally to a handful of other concepts covered elsewhere in this guide. They share their payoff logic with plain call options — both are worthless below the strike and gain value above it — but differ in settlement mechanics and issuer identity. They're also a cousin of convertible bonds, which similarly give the holder upside participation in a company's stock tied to a fixed conversion price, and of employee stock options, which vest on time or performance milestones in much the same way Amazon's Generac warrants do. Understanding warrants also sharpens your read on deal announcements generally: when a press release mentions warrants alongside a supply or partnership agreement, it's worth digging into the strike price, the vesting schedule, and the milestone structure before assuming the stock pop is just hype — as the video's framing put it,

watch what the hyperscalers sign, not what the CEOs write, because contract is the only forecast with money behind it.
