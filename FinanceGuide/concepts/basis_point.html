Basis Point (bps)

A basis point (abbreviated bps, pronounced "bips") is simply one one-hundredth of a percentage point — that is, 0.01%. So 100 basis points equals 1.00%, and 25 basis points equals 0.25%. The term exists because percentage points, when applied to interest rates and bond yields, are too coarse a unit for how precisely markets actually move and how much those small moves matter. When a yield shifts from 4.37% to 4.38%, saying it moved "0.01 percentage points" is accurate but awkward. Saying it moved "one basis point" is precise, unambiguous, and instantly understood by every participant in the room. It is the shared language of fixed income, and fluency in it is entry-level literacy for following bond markets.

Basis points matter because interest rates govern the price of nearly everything in a modern economy. The rate on your mortgage, your car loan, the yield a corporation pays to borrow money, the discount rate used to value stocks — all of these trace back to prevailing interest rate levels. When those levels shift even slightly, the dollar consequences at scale are enormous. A single basis point move on a $10 trillion government bond market represents $1 billion in annualized interest cost. That is why professionals never say "the rate went up a little." They say exactly how many basis points it moved, because in fixed income, precision is not pedantry — it is money. Every basis point is a number someone is paying or receiving.

Mechanically, basis points are used to describe changes in yield, which is the effective annual return an investor earns on a bond. Yield and bond price move in opposite directions: when investors sell bonds, prices fall and yields rise. When yields rise, borrowing becomes more expensive for governments, corporations, and consumers. The mechanism works like this: a bond pays a fixed coupon payment. If new bonds are being issued at higher rates because inflation or Fed policy has shifted, then your existing bond paying the old lower rate is worth less — so its price drops until the yield (coupon divided by current price) matches the new market rate. A 26 basis point rise in yield does not sound dramatic, but it represents a meaningful repricing of risk and cost throughout the entire economy.

The video gives a vivid, real-world example of exactly this dynamic in motion. Over just four trading sessions — a week shortened by Labor Day — the 2-year Treasury yield climbed from 4.37% to approximately 4.63%. That is a move of 26 basis points in under a week, which Mark Malik describes as "26 basis points of tightening before the Fed even sits down to have their first government donut." The 2-year yield is particularly sensitive to Federal Reserve expectations because it reflects what the market believes short-term rates will be over the next two years. When oil shock drove wholesale and consumer prices higher, traders didn't wait for the Fed to act — they repriced the entire front end of the yield curve by 26 basis points, effectively doing the Fed's work for it.

Traders and investors use basis points as a precise signal-reading tool. When the market assigns an 85% probability of a 25 basis point hike — as it did heading into the September FOMC meeting described in the video — that number comes from pricing in federal funds futures contracts. Traders are essentially betting on where the Fed funds rate will land. A 25 bps hike means the target range moves up by exactly a quarter point, from 3.50–3.75% to 3.75–4.00%. Every piece of incoming data — CPI, PPI, consumer sentiment — shifts the probability of that hike, and you can watch those odds move in real time. When core CPI came in 0.1 percentage point hotter than expected, the probability of a hike jumped from roughly 50% to the "mid to high 80s." That single tenth of a point miss, translating to about 10 basis points, moved market sentiment dramatically.

One important nuance: basis points describe changes, not levels. People sometimes confuse the two. If someone says "the Fed raised rates 25 basis points," that means the rate went up by 0.25% — not that the rate is now 0.25%. Context matters. Additionally, when you see basis points used in the context of credit spreads — the extra yield a corporate bond pays above a Treasury — even small basis point moves carry outsized meaning. A spread widening from 50 bps to 150 bps does not mean much changed in absolute percentage terms (0.5% to 1.5%), but it signals that credit markets are suddenly pricing in dramatically more default risk. The number of basis points is always a relative signal, and you must know what it is relative to.

Where basis point thinking can mislead investors is in low-rate environments versus high-rate environments. A 25 basis point move when rates are at 0.25% is a 100% increase in the rate — it doubles your cost of borrowing. The same 25 basis point move when rates are at 5.00% is a 5% increase — much less dramatic in relative terms, but it still reaches into the real economy. Another gotcha: basis points apply to yields, not to bond prices, and the relationship between the two is nonlinear. The price sensitivity of a bond to yield changes is captured by a measure called duration — a bond with high duration loses much more price value per basis point of yield increase than a short-duration bond. Ignoring duration while watching basis points is like tracking speed without knowing the car's mass.

Basis points connect directly to several other core fixed income concepts that appear throughout this guide. The yield curve — the relationship between short-term and long-term yields — is analyzed entirely in basis point terms; an inverted curve might mean the 2-year yield is 50 bps above the 10-year, and that spread matters enormously as a recession signal. The Fed funds rate moves in 25 or 50 basis point increments by convention. Mortgage rates typically track the 10-year Treasury yield with a spread of roughly 150–200 bps. And as the video illustrates, when the 10-year hit 4.96% — the highest since 2023 — the ripple effects hit the Russell 2000 (small companies borrowing at floating rates) harder than mega-cap tech. Understanding basis points is the first step to reading all of these connected signals fluently.
