Allowed Return on Equity (Regulated Utilities)

A regulated utility is a company that has been granted a legal monopoly over a service, such as electricity delivery in a region. In exchange, the government sets the prices it can charge. Because regulators won't let it charge whatever the market will bear, they promise it a fair profit instead. That promise is the allowed return on equity (ROE): the annual profit, as a percentage of the shareholders' invested money, that a state commission agrees the utility may earn. If the commission sets it at 9.8% and shareholders have put $1 billion into the business, the utility is allowed to collect roughly $98 million a year in profit. Think of it as a regulator-approved salary for the owners' capital.

This matters because it sets the economics of one of the largest and most capital-hungry industries in America. Electricity touches every household and business, and the bill you pay is built partly out of this number. Investors who buy utility stocks are mostly buying the allowed ROE and the growth of the asset base it applies to. Borrowers who buy utility bonds are lending against a revenue stream the government has effectively promised to protect. And ratepayers feel it directly: every extra tenth of a percentage point of allowed ROE, multiplied across billions of dollars of equity, is collected from customers as part of their monthly rates. Mechanically, regulators use cost-of-service ratemaking. In a rate case, the commission adds up what the utility needs to collect, called the revenue requirement. The formula is: operating expenses, plus depreciation, plus taxes, plus a return on the rate base. The rate base is the net value of the poles, wires, substations and power plants the utility has built to serve the public. The return is the rate base multiplied by a blended cost of capital, where the equity slice earns the allowed ROE and the debt slice earns the utility's actual interest cost. Rates are then set so that total customer payments equal that requirement. Approve a bigger rate base or a higher ROE, and rates rise.

The video gives the key distinction and its consequence. State commissions, the narrator says, set an ROE "typically between 9% and 10.5%," while utilities can issue bonds at "six or 7%." Here is the incentive. Every dollar of new infrastructure added to the rate base earns the allowed return, and the utility's profit grows with the rate base. Suppose a utility builds $5 billion of substations and transmission lines for data centers, funded about half by equity. That adds $2.5 billion of equity earning roughly 10%, or about $250 million of extra annual profit, collected from the rate base for decades. Growing the base is the business model.

"The more money they borrow and spend on mega scale infrastructure projects, the larger their rate base becomes and the larger their absolute profit check at the end of the year."

Traders and investors use allowed ROE as a core signal. Utility analysts track each company's authorized ROE against its earned ROE, because the gap shows how well management turns the regulator's permission into real profit. They compare authorized ROEs across states to rank which regulators are "constructive," meaning generous and predictable, and which are hostile. They also model rate base growth, since earnings per share tend to grow at about the same pace. A company announcing a $60 billion five-year capital plan with 8% annual rate base growth is telegraphing roughly 6–8% earnings growth. Bond investors watch the same cases because a cut in allowed ROE or a denied cost recovery changes credit quality.

The first gotcha is that the video's contrast of 10% against 6–7% is a little misleading. Equity and debt are different things. Shareholders bear the risk of losses and take the last claim on the company's assets, so they legitimately demand a higher return than lenders do. Utilities also cannot fund everything with debt: regulators set a target capital structure, often about 50% equity, and only that portion earns the allowed ROE. Debt is passed through at its cost with no markup. So the spread does not apply to every borrowed dollar. The real incentive is subtler: the equity share of every new project earns a regulator-set return that is comfortably above what the same capital would cost in a competitive market, if the ROE is set too high.

A second misconception is the word "guarantee." The video says commissions "guarantee" a 10% return, but what is guaranteed is the opportunity to earn it, not the result. Regulators decide which costs are "prudent" and may disallow spending they find wasteful, which is how projects with big overruns, such as nuclear plants, have left shareholders with losses. Regulatory lag, the delay between when a utility spends money and when rates catch up, often leaves earned ROE below authorized. And demand surprises, storms, or higher interest costs can eat into returns. On the other side, nationwide authorized ROEs have mostly stayed near 9.5–10% in recent years. Most utilities do not earn windfalls.

Things break down in several ways. If regulators set the ROE too high, ratepayers overpay and the utility has every incentive to over-build, a well-known problem called the Averch-Johnson effect, where a firm with a guaranteed return on capital uses more capital than is efficient. If regulators set it too low, utilities struggle to raise money, delay upgrades, and the grid weakens, which can hurt reliability just as demand surges. Political pressure makes this worse. Commissions are often elected or appointed by elected officials, and a bill spike can push them to cut ROE, as consumer advocates urge. The utility, for its part, can pause projects or ask for riders and trackers that speed up recovery. Either failure ends up on the customer's meter.

The video's second point is allocation: who pays for what. A fair system sets rates by cost causation, meaning customers who cause a cost pay for it. Data centers are huge new loads, and if their substations and lines are folded into the general rate base, everyone's bill rises while the utility earns its allowed return on the build. Regulators have responded with large-load tariffs, which require big customers to sign long-term contracts, pay minimum charges, or cover the cost of dedicated infrastructure. Ratepayers can also take part in rate cases to challenge the ROE requested, the proposed rate base, or the cost-allocation. This is the practical form of the video's advice to "participate in state rate case interventions."

Allowed ROE connects to many other ideas in finance. It is a form of cost of equity, usually estimated by regulators with models such as the discounted cash flow or the capital asset pricing model, then judged against peer utilities. It ties to capital structure, because the equity ratio determines how much of the rate base earns the higher return. It explains why utility stocks behave like bond proxies, trading inversely to interest rates, because their income is steady and regulated. It links to capital expenditure cycles and credit ratings, since heavy borrowing pressures leverage metrics. Understanding ROE lets you read a utility's capital plan, its rate case and your own bill as parts of one machine.
