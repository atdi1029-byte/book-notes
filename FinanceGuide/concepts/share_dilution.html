Share Dilution

Share dilution is what happens when a company's ownership pie is divided into more slices. If a company has 100 shares and you own 10, you own 10% of it. If the company then creates 25 new shares and hands them to someone else, there are 125 shares, and your 10 now represent 8%. Nothing was taken from your account, and your share count never changed. But your claim on the company's profits, assets, and future value shrank, because the same business is now split among more owners. Dilution is not a fee or a penalty. It is arithmetic, and it is one of the most important things to check before buying any stock, especially a new one.

It matters because a share is not a piece of "the company" in the abstract. It is a fraction, and the fraction depends on the denominator. Investors often fixate on the numerator, meaning how much the business earns, and forget to ask how many shares will split that earnings pile. Earnings of $1 billion are wonderful if they are divided among 1 billion shares and mediocre if divided among 5 billion. Dilution affects earnings per share, voting power, dividends per share, and ultimately the stock price. Over years, a company that quietly issues shares every quarter can grow its total profits and still deliver nothing to its owners, because each owner's slice kept getting thinner.

Mechanically, dilution happens whenever new shares are created. The common routes are a fresh stock sale to raise cash, employee stock options and grants, acquisitions paid for in stock, and the conversion of other securities into shares. That last route is the one in the video. A convertible note is a loan that, instead of being repaid in cash with interest, converts into shares at some point, usually a funding round or an IPO. Until then it sits on the books as a debt. When conversion happens, the lender trades the IOU for shares, the share count rises, and every existing holder's percentage falls. The key detail is that the number of shares delivered depends on a price or valuation agreed earlier, often with a discount for the early lender.

The video gives a vivid, real-world example. Anthropic's filing for a NASDAQ listing showed an $80.7 billion net loss for the first half of the year, but the operating loss was only $693 million. The gap was driven mostly by a $72.4 billion charge for the change in fair value of convertible notes. Those notes are set to convert into shares for Amazon Web Services when the IPO closes. As Anthropic grew more valuable, the shares those notes would turn into grew more valuable too, so the liability grew and the accounting loss ballooned. The charge isn't cash spent on chips. It measures how much the early lenders' claim swelled.

"Every dollar of fair value that went to the note holders is a slice of the company the IPO buyers actually don't get. So you're not buying the whole pie. You're buying what's left after the early money has already eaten."

The video's restaurant analogy makes the logic concrete. You borrow from your brother-in-law and, instead of interest, promise him a piece of the place. The restaurant takes off, so his piece is now worth a fortune. You never lost cash, but you owe him a far more valuable slice. Accounting calls that growth a loss, because a liability got bigger. In dilution terms, the bill is paid in share count. When the notes convert at the IPO, new shares appear, owned by the early money, and they exist before any public buyer gets to purchase one. The IPO buyer is not diluted by a cash payment. They simply start out owning a smaller fraction of the company than the headline valuation might suggest.

Investors use dilution in several practical ways. First, read the share count, not just the price. Market capitalization is price times shares, so you need the fully diluted share count, which includes options, warrants, and convertibles that will become shares, rather than only shares outstanding today. Second, compare the per-share numbers. The video mentions a first-half loss of $39.88 per share, and that figure only means something once you know the share count it was divided by and how that count changes at the IPO. Third, watch for steady issuers. A rising share count year after year, particularly from stock compensation, is a signal that reported profit growth may not reach you. Many investors favor companies that buy back shares, which shrinks the denominator.

There are several gotchas. Dilution is not automatically bad. If a company raises money by selling shares and uses it to grow value by more than the ownership it gave up, existing holders come out ahead. Owning 8% of a business that doubled beats owning 10% of one that stood still. A second misconception is that dilution is the same as a loss of money. It is a loss of percentage, and whether that matters depends on what the company did with the proceeds. A third is confusing accounting losses with cash losses, as the headlines did. A fair-value charge on convertible notes is real as a claim on future ownership but involves no cash leaving the company. Finally, a share count in a draft filing can be blank or provisional, so early per-share figures can shift.

Dilution turns dangerous when it is large, repeated, or forced. A company in trouble may sell shares at a depressed price to survive, handing over far more ownership per dollar raised, and then must do it again, a pattern called a dilution spiral. Convertible structures that reset to lower prices as the stock falls can feed this loop, because lower prices mean more shares issued, which pushes the price down further. Another risk is a hidden overhang: convertibles, warrants, and unvested options not obvious in the headline share count. Control is a risk too. Anthropic's structure includes a single founder share carrying 50.1% of voting power and non-voting shares for the converting notes, so public buyers can be diluted in economics and still have almost no say.

Dilution connects to several other ideas. It sits inside valuation, since price per share only makes sense alongside share count. It links to IPO mechanics, where early investors, lenders, and employees hold claims that convert before the public arrives, and to lockups, which govern when those insiders may sell. It ties to fair value accounting, which explains why a rising valuation can create a reported loss, and to dual-class share structures, which separate economic ownership from voting power. In conversation, the clean way to put it is this: before you ask what a company is worth, ask how many ways the pie has been cut and who is already at the table.
