Storage Injection and Withdrawal Seasonality

Natural gas is one of the few major commodities whose demand swings wildly with the calendar, and storage injection and withdrawal seasonality is how the market copes with that. Roughly from April through October, demand is low, because homes need little heat and power plants face moderate loads. Producers keep pumping, so surplus gas is injected into underground reservoirs such as depleted fields and salt caverns. From November through March, the flow reverses. Cold weather drives heating demand far above what wells can supply on a given day, so gas is withdrawn from storage to fill the gap. Storage is the market's shock absorber, moving summer's surplus into winter's shortage.

This matters because natural gas is expensive to move and awkward to store, so the stored volume going into winter works like the economy's energy safety margin. When storage is full, a cold snap is an inconvenience. When it is thin, a cold snap becomes a price spike, and that spike reaches households through heating bills, to industrial users through input costs, and to power markets through electricity prices. It also shapes politics. Europe's rules require countries to fill storage toward a target, because the memory of 2022 is fresh. In the video, the key contrast is simple: Europe is below its seasonal norm while the US is above it, and that explains much of why the two prices behave so differently.

Mechanically, the cycle runs in a few steps. First, in shoulder and summer months, supply exceeds demand, so the extra gas flows into storage at a rate measured in billions of cubic feet per week. Second, inventory builds until it peaks, typically around late October or early November. Third, as heating demand takes over, weekly numbers flip from net injections to net withdrawals. Fourth, inventories bottom out in late March or April, and the cycle restarts. Storage has physical limits: there is a maximum working gas capacity (the gas actually available for use, as opposed to permanent "cushion gas" that keeps pressure up), and injection and withdrawal rates are capped by compressors and pipes. You can't fill or empty a reservoir instantly.

The video gives clear numbers. The EIA reported 3,500 billion cubic feet of working gas in US storage as of October 2nd, about 68 Bcf (roughly 2%) above the five-year average for that week, after an 85 Bcf injection the prior week. That is below last year by about 3.6%, but above average is not drained. The EIA expects the US to enter heating season about 2% above the five-year norm. Europe, by contrast, was only about 74% full, the lowest for this time of year since records began in 2011 and roughly 15 points under the five-year average. The EU target is 90% by December 1st, though countries may fall short by up to 10 points when prices are high. Same fuel, same season, opposite positions.

Traders and investors read storage as a scoreboard. The weekly EIA report, released Thursdays, is compared with consensus estimates, and the market moves on the surprise, not the absolute figure. More important than the raw level is the deviation from the five-year average, since seasonality is already priced in. A surplus tends to cap prices and a deficit supports them. Traders also watch the shape of the futures curve: when winter contracts trade well above fall contracts, the market is paying someone to store gas. In Europe, analysts track the gas infrastructure data on fill levels against the regulatory trajectory, and watch whether TTF rises as the deadline nears. Weather forecasts, especially for January, are the other major input.

"Above average is not drained."

There are several gotchas. The first is mixing up level with trend: a storage level can be low while injections are strong, or high while they are fading. The second is assuming that a big price gap between regions means cross-border flows will quickly close it. The video's central lesson is that LNG export capacity is a hard ceiling. Gulf Coast terminals ran about 17.4 Bcf/day in October, roughly 16% of the ~110.6 Bcf/day US output, and a higher European bid cannot push more through a full liquefaction train. The spread flows to the cargo owner, not into Henry Hub. The third gotcha is the headline-friendly assumption that "Europe's shortage means your bill rises." The EIA projects average US gas-heating households to pay about $640 this winter, about 9% less than last year. Also, the video says it could not source the widely repeated "98% terminal utilization" claim, a reminder to verify viral numbers.

Seasonality breaks when weather, supply or infrastructure surprises the schedule. A brutal cold snap can pull gas from storage faster than the system was built to deliver; on January 23rd, cash gas at Henry Hub for weekend delivery jumped above $28, matching what Europe pays now, and that was driven by weather, not exports. Europe's risk is a mirror image: if it enters January with low storage and a cold month, there is little cushion left and prices can gap higher, especially with the ban on long-term Russian LNG contracts due January 1st, 2027. Other failure modes include terminal outages (Freeport's delayed restart and Cove Point maintenance reduced US exports this October), and extreme draws that expose how much gas can physically leave storage per day.

Storage seasonality connects to several other ideas. It links to the futures curve and contango, because the summer-winter spread pays for storage. It ties to LNG as a regional arbitrage: Henry Hub and TTF can diverge because liquefaction capacity, not willingness to pay, limits flows, and new capacity (Golden Pass, Corpus Christi Stage 3) gradually tightens that link. It also connects to substitute fuels. Heating oil, a distillate, has its own storage story: East Coast distillate stocks were 32% below the five-year average in September, and the EIA expects oil-heated households (about 4.1 million) to pay roughly 21% more, about $2,115. The practical takeaway is to watch each fuel's inventory against its own seasonal norm, not the headline spread. Where stocks are thin, the shock lands.
