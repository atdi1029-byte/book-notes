Mortgage Prepayment Speed

Mortgage prepayment speed measures how fast homeowners pay off their loans ahead of the contractual schedule. A 30-year mortgage is written to be repaid slowly over 360 months, but almost no loan runs its full term. Owners sell, refinance, or occasionally pay extra, and each of those events returns principal to the lender early. Prepayment speed puts a number on that behavior across a whole pool of mortgages. The standard shorthand is the CPR (conditional prepayment rate), the annualized percentage of a pool's remaining balance that prepays in a year. A CPR of 6% means roughly 6% of the outstanding principal gets paid off early over twelve months. Higher means faster, and lower means slower.

The number matters because of who owns these loans. Most U.S. mortgages are bundled into mortgage-backed securities (MBS), bonds that pass homeowners' monthly payments through to investors, including pension funds, insurers, banks, and the Fed. Those investors are not buying a fixed-term bond with a known maturity date. They are buying a stream of cash whose timing is decided by millions of households making personal decisions: moving for a job, having a third child, or refinancing because rates fell. Prepayment speed is the variable that converts a pile of 30-year loans into an asset that might return your money in four years or in twenty-five. Pricing, hedging, and risk all hang on it.

Here is the mechanism step by step. Each month a homeowner pays interest plus a slice of principal. That is the scheduled part. On top of it, some borrowers pay off the entire balance because they sold the house or refinanced into a new loan. The servicer passes that lump sum straight through to MBS holders at par (100 cents on the dollar), no matter what the bond was trading at in the market. Analysts split prepayments into turnover (people moving, which happens in any rate environment) and refinancing (people responding to lower rates). Because the pool shrinks as loans vanish, the future interest income shrinks too, so the investor's average cash-flow timing, called duration, shortens or lengthens depending on how fast the pool is draining.

Now apply it to the video's numbers. During the 2020 and 2021 bond mania, institutions bought hundreds of billions of dollars of agency MBS carrying 2% and 3% coupons. They priced them using the historical pattern the host describes: borrowers "moved every 5 to seven years, refinanced when spreads contracted, and prepaid the principal at par." That made the paper behave like a roughly seven-year asset. Then rates jumped to around 6.85%, and the video says 85 million homeowners hold mortgages below 5%, with nearly 48 million below 3.5%. Nobody with a 2.75% loan sells or refinances voluntarily, so speeds fell toward zero. The same bonds now look like 25-year assets.

"What institutions thought was a 7-year liquid cash flow asset has absolutely officially transformed into a 25-year ultra-low yielding debt anchor."

The video's own payment arithmetic shows why borrowers behave this way. A $320,000 loan at 3% costs about $1,349 a month in principal and interest. The same loan at 6.85% costs about $2,097, a penalty of roughly $748 a month for 30 years. (The transcript garbles the figure as "$2,97," but $1,349 plus $748 gives $2,097.) Add reassessed property taxes and a new insurance policy, and the video puts the total cost of moving at about $1,298 more per month. Any rational owner compares that bill to the benefit of selling. This is called the lock-in effect, and it is the individual-level cause of the pool-level slowdown. Each household's decision makes sense, and together they stop the principal from coming back.

Traders and portfolio managers use prepayment speed as a core pricing input. An MBS buyer builds a model that projects speeds under different rate scenarios, then discounts the resulting cash flows. Desks watch published CPR and PSA figures (a benchmark that scales speeds relative to a standard ramp-up curve), monthly factor data from Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and Ginnie Mae, and the gap between a pool's coupon and the current mortgage rate, called the refinance incentive. Pools are also sorted by coupon, loan size, geography, and seasoning, because those traits change behavior. A trader who expects speeds to stay slow buys or avoids specific coupons accordingly, and hedges the longer duration with Treasuries or swaps. Slow speeds on low-coupon pools are a signal about housing turnover as well as a bond input.

There are several gotchas. First, slow prepayment is not automatically good or bad. If an investor holds a bond paying more than the market rate, slow prepayment is welcome, because the bond stays high-yielding longer. The problem in the video is the opposite: coupons of 2% to 3% sit far below market yields, so every extra year of life is a year of below-market income. Second, speeds are not purely about rates. Death, divorce, job moves, and defaults also end loans, so even a deeply locked-in pool never hits true zero. Third, the transcript's "near zero" is a simplification. Low-coupon pools still prepay slowly through turnover, and the host is describing a direction, not a precise reading.

When prepayment behavior flips, the risk is called extension risk, the mirror image of contraction risk, where rates fall and borrowers refinance so fast that investors get cash back early and must reinvest at worse yields. Extension is what the video describes, and it hurts in a particular way. Rates rise, so the bond's price falls. Prepayments slow, so the bond's duration lengthens at the same moment, which makes the price fall further. This is called negative convexity: the instrument gets more rate-sensitive precisely when you least want it to. The video ties this to regional banks sitting on held-to-maturity mortgage holdings trading near 75 cents on the dollar, "waiting for a borrower to refinance who cannot legally afford to refinance." The wording is dramatic. Borrowers can refinance if they choose, but doing so would raise their payment sharply, so in practice they don't.

Prepayment speed connects to several other ideas worth knowing. It links to duration and convexity, since speed determines both for any MBS. It links to unrealized losses on bank balance sheets, because longer-lived bonds lose more value when rates rise. It ties to the housing market through existing home sales: the video cites about 3.8 million annualized transactions, a level it compares to 1995, and slow turnover is exactly what holds speeds down. And it relates to the MBS spread, the extra yield investors demand over Treasuries, which tends to widen when prepayment uncertainty rises. In short, speed is where household decisions and bond math meet. If you can explain why a homeowner's 2.75% mortgage turns a bank's seven-year bond into a 25-year one, you understand the idea.
