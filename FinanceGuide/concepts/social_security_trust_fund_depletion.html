Social Security Trust Fund Depletion

Social Security is mostly a pay-as-you-go system. Payroll taxes collected from today's workers, 12.4% of wages up to a cap and split between employee and employer, are used to pay today's retirees. The trust funds are the accounting reserves that hold any surplus from years when tax income and interest exceeded benefits paid. They are not a pile of cash. They hold special-issue Treasury securities, which are government IOUs. There are two of them: the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) fund, which pays retirement and survivor benefits, and the Disability Insurance (DI) fund. Trust fund depletion is the date when the reserves run out and the program can only spend what it collects in that year.

It matters because Social Security is the largest federal program and the main income source for many retirees. The video opens with 71.5 million Americans waiting on their cost-of-living adjustment, and that scale is the point. Any change to the program touches roughly one in five Americans directly. It also touches everyone who plans to retire later, because their benefit assumptions come from the current formula. For markets, the effects run through Treasury issuance, deficit projections, and political risk. Any fix will probably involve tax changes, benefit changes, or both. For an individual, it decides how much of retirement income you can treat as certain and how much you must provide for yourself.

The mechanism works in steps. Each year, payroll taxes and income taxes on benefits flow into the funds, and benefit checks and administrative costs flow out. Any leftover is invested in special Treasury securities that earn interest. For years the program ran surpluses, and the reserves grew. Once the baby boom generation retired in large numbers, benefit outlays began to exceed total income. The gap is covered by drawing down the reserves, redeeming those securities, which the Treasury must repay from general revenue. Each year the balance shrinks. When it hits zero, the program is limited by a legal rule: it can pay benefits only from incoming revenue. The trustees' yearly report projects the date and how much is still payable.

The video cites the 2026 trustees report. The combined funds, treated as if they were one pool, run dry in 2034, after which incoming taxes cover about 83% of scheduled benefits. The retirement fund alone, OASI, is depleted sooner, in late 2032, with about 78% payable. The distinction matters because the two funds are legally separate. Combining them assumes Congress would allow borrowing between them, as it has done before, so the retirement fund's own date is the sharper warning. Apply 78% to the video's $2,88x average benefit (the transcript garbles the figure) and you can see the scale: a cut of more than a fifth on a check that many households depend on.

"The retirement fund alone gets there even sooner, late in 2032 with about 78% payable. That's why your own savings and investments need to do more than just sit there."

Investors and planners use the depletion date as a stress test, not a prediction of collapse. A sensible approach is to run your retirement plan twice: once with the full scheduled benefit, and once with a haircut of around 20 to 25% beginning in the early-to-mid 2030s. If the plan still works under the haircut, you are robust to the legislative outcome. If it fails, you have a measurable savings gap to close. This is the video's practical point: because the government check is not designed to outpace prices, the part of your portfolio that is meant to grow faster than inflation, such as diversified equities, has to carry more weight. Younger workers should assume they will face the post-fix version of the program, whatever it turns out to be.

The gotchas begin with the phrase "going broke." Depletion does not mean benefits go to zero. Payroll taxes keep arriving every paycheck, so even with an empty trust fund, roughly 78% to 83% of scheduled benefits remain payable, according to the figures in the video. Second, the date is a projection that moves each year with wages, employment, birth rates, immigration, mortality, and interest rates. The 2026 report's dates are estimates built on assumptions, and they have shifted by a year or two between reports. Third, the "trust fund" is not a hoard of idle cash that the government spent. The securities are real legal obligations, but repaying them depends on the federal budget. Finally, a headline cut of 17 to 22% is a cut relative to scheduled benefits, which include future COLAs.

If nothing is done, the shortfall hits automatically. The agency cannot pay more than the law allows, so every beneficiary would see a proportional reduction, and that reduction would grow if the gap widens over time. The realistic risk is that Congress waits until the last moment and chooses abrupt changes: a payroll tax increase, raising or removing the wage cap, a higher retirement age, a changed benefit formula, or means-testing. Each puts the cost on a different group. Retirees on fixed incomes with little other savings are most exposed, and there is little time to adapt. Because the OASI date is the earliest, the political pressure will probably build around 2030 to 2032, not 2034, and it could surface in election campaigns well before then.

Depletion connects directly to the other topics in the video. The COLA is meant to protect benefits from inflation, but the video argues it arrives late, is measured on a worker-based index (CPI-W), and is trimmed by Medicare Part B premiums and by tax thresholds set in 1983 and 1993 and never indexed. Depletion adds a second, bigger threat on top of that: even a perfectly measured COLA applies to a benefit that could be cut by roughly a fifth. Together they argue for the same response, which is tax-aware withdrawal sequencing, watching Medicare's income-based surcharge (IRMAA), and building personal assets that compound faster than prices. Treat the benefit as valuable but not guaranteed to be whole, and plan so that your retirement does not rest on it alone.
