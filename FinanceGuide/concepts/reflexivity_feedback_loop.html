Reflexivity (Price-Narrative Feedback Loop)

Reflexivity is the idea that prices do not just reflect what people believe about the world. They also change what people believe, and those changed beliefs then change the world and the price again. The term comes from the investor George Soros, who borrowed it from social science. In a purely passive view of markets, reality sits on one side and the price is a mirror that reports on it. Reflexivity says the mirror talks back. A price becomes a headline, the headline shapes opinion, opinion drives money, and money moves the price. The result is a circle rather than a one-way street. Beginners often think of a price as a measurement, like a thermometer reading. A better picture is a thermometer that, once people look at it, changes the temperature of the room.

This matters because prices are used as information, and information gets acted on. A stock price influences whether a company can raise money cheaply, whether employees want to join, and whether lenders feel safe. A home price index shapes how confident owners feel and how much they spend. A prediction market price shapes how voters, donors, journalists, and traders think a race will go. If the price were only a passive reading, none of that would matter. Because the price feeds back into the thing it describes, a wrong number can become self-sustaining for a while, and a right number can become more extreme than the facts justify. That is how booms inflate past reason and how busts feed on themselves.

Mechanically, the loop has four steps that repeat. First, a price appears, often in a market that is small or thinly traded. Second, someone with an audience quotes it, on television, in a headline, on a billboard, or in a social feed. Third, the quote attracts new participants, and many arrive out of FOMO, the fear of missing out, because a number that everyone is repeating feels like a story you should already be part of. Fourth, their buying pushes the price further in the same direction, which makes the original narrative look confirmed. Then the cycle starts again with a stronger-looking number. Each lap adds conviction, but it does not necessarily add new information. The loop can run on attention alone.

The video gives a clean modern example. On election day 2026, Kalshi priced Democratic control of the House at 94%, with more than $237 million riding on that one question. Kalshi put its Senate odds on a billboard in New York City, and numbers like these were all over headlines and feeds. Underneath, the picture was thinner than the billboard suggested. On Polymarket's international site, the top 1% of wallets placed about 70% of the money bet on Congress this cycle, according to Reuters citing the Anti-Corruption Data Collective. Only 39,820 wallets had bet on the 2026 congressional races as of August, and 80% of the markets examined had fewer than 100 wallets in them. Ten wallets accounted for 17% of volume and had bets in 426 of 470 seats.

"A thin market can get quoted on television. The quote can pull in new money. The new money can make the price look even more convincing."

The presenter's point is that this can happen without anyone doing anything wrong. One confident trader in a market with fewer than 100 wallets can be most of the market, so the price looks like a consensus while really being one person's opinion with a big check attached. Once that number is quoted widely, it draws in people who treat it as the voice of the crowd. Their money deepens the appearance of agreement. Nobody has to lie, fake a trade, or break a rule. He is explicit that nobody has shown any 2026 midterm market was manipulated, and that the loop is simply what can happen when a small market gets a big microphone. That distinction matters: reflexivity is a structural feature of how information and money interact, not an accusation of fraud.

Investors and traders use reflexivity in several practical ways. The first is to ask what is sitting behind a price, not only what the price says. The presenter weights every price in his Boom versus Doom index by the money actually in the market, trusting a price with a lot of capital behind it more than one with a little. The second is to look for stretches where price momentum is being driven by the story itself, such as a surge that follows a media mention rather than new facts. The third is to watch for late, sharp moves in small markets, which can mark where a single large wallet looked like a consensus. Professional traders sometimes ride these loops early, but they plan their exit before the crowd arrives.

There are common misconceptions worth clearing up. Reflexivity does not mean prices are meaningless, and it does not mean markets are always wrong. The presenter acknowledges that markets called 2024 better than polling averages, and that Polymarket claims its election markets are accurate 98% of the time one day before an election. A feedback loop can push a price toward a truth just as easily as away from it. Nor does a 94% price mean certainty. It is a probability, meaning the underdog wins roughly one time in 17. And one outcome cannot grade a forecast. Judging whether prices were good requires calibration: collecting many races priced near 80% and checking whether about eight in ten came in.

Reflexive loops fail when they run out of fuel or meet an outside reality they cannot bend. A narrative can lift a price only while new buyers keep arriving. When the supply of fresh money dries up, or a hard fact lands, the same loop runs in reverse, and the price falls faster than it rose because everyone who joined for the story now wants out. Election night is a clear case of an external check, since votes are counted regardless of what the price says. The 2022 PredictIt example in the video shows the violence of this: Republicans were priced as high as 82% to win the Senate on election day, and as results came in that night the price fell to 39%, with Nevada not called until the following Saturday.

Reflexivity connects to several other ideas. It is closely related to momentum and herding, where people follow what others are doing, and to liquidity, because thin markets are the easiest to push around and the hardest to trust. It also links to anchoring, since a quoted number like 94% sticks in a reader's mind as a fact. Bubbles and crashes are the large-scale versions of the same loop. The business angle adds fuel: with Intercontinental Exchange investing $1.6 billion in Polymarket and Kalshi raising $1 billion at a $22 billion valuation, odds have become a product, and products need volume. The practical lesson is to treat any widely quoted price as both a signal and a participant.
