Controlled Company (Governance Exemption)

A controlled company is a public company in which one person, group, or entity holds more than 50% of the voting power in the election of directors. Stock exchanges such as NASDAQ and the NYSE treat these companies differently from widely held ones. Most listed companies must follow a set of corporate governance rules: a majority of the board must be independent, independent directors must sit on the committee that sets executive pay, and independent directors must pick board nominees. A controlled company can opt out of those three requirements. The idea is simple. If one holder already decides every vote, then a rule that says "protect minority shareholders by giving independent directors the power" has little to protect, because the controlling holder can replace those directors whenever it wants. The exemption is a formal acknowledgment that the shareholders do not all stand on equal footing.

This matters because it determines what you are actually buying when you purchase a share. Most people assume that owning stock means owning a slice of the company and getting a say in how it is run. In practice, ownership and control can be separated cleanly. The economic interest is your claim on profits, and the voting interest is your power to choose the board and approve major decisions. A controlled company is one where those two pieces have been deliberately pulled apart. The public provides the capital, while a small group keeps the steering wheel. That affects who the board answers to, how executives are paid, whether a takeover can ever happen, and whether your interests are weighed when a conflict arises. For index funds and 401k holders, who end up owning these shares automatically, the question is not academic.

Mechanically, control usually comes from a dual-class or multi-class share structure. The company issues different classes of stock with different voting rights. Public investors get a class with one vote per share, and insiders hold a class with ten votes per share, or in some cases a block with a fixed percentage of the total vote regardless of how many shares exist. Step by step: the company's charter creates the classes; the insiders receive the high-vote class before the IPO; the public buys the low-vote class; and the exchange then tests whether a single holder or group controls more than 50% of the total votes. If yes, the company may declare itself controlled and must say so in its filings. Notice that the test is about votes, not about share count or dollars invested. A holder with a tiny economic stake can clear the bar if the structure is written that way.

The Anthropic filing described in the video is a vivid example. The shares sold to the public are Class A shares with one vote each. But a founder LLC was issued a single Class F share just before the offering, and that one share carries 50.1% of the total voting power of the company. A separate Class T share, held by the Long-Term Benefit Trust, lets five trustees elect the directors who make up a majority of the board. On top of that, the convertible notes that convert for Amazon Web Services at the IPO become Class N shares, where the N stands for no vote. Under NASDAQ rules, the host explained, this makes Anthropic a controlled company. One share, held by one LLC, outweighs every Class A share that the public will ever buy, combined.

"They want your money. They don't want your vote."

Look at what 50.1% really means. It is not a figure that grows or shrinks with the stock price. It is a fixed slice of the total vote, set by the charter, and the extra 0.1% above half is the entire point. It means the Class F holder wins any vote that comes down to a simple majority, no matter how many Class A shares exist or how many are sold later. If Anthropic issues billions of dollars of new Class A stock, the Class F share keeps its 50.1% unless the charter says otherwise. So when you buy Class A, you are buying a claim on the profits and an almost purely symbolic vote. You can vote on a proposal, but you cannot change the outcome of one the Class F holder opposes. That is the trade the investors in this IPO are being asked to accept.

How do investors use this information? First, they read it as a risk factor and a pricing input. In the prospectus, look for the phrases "controlled company" and "we may rely on exemptions," and look for the cover-page breakdown of voting power. Second, they ask whether the controlling holder has earned trust. A founder with a long record of treating outside shareholders fairly is a different bet than a holder with a record of related-party deals. Third, they watch which exemptions are actually used. Declaring yourself eligible is not the same as using the exemption. Some controlled companies still keep independent boards and committees voluntarily. A company that does is sending a signal; one that drops every protection is sending a different one. Index providers have also debated whether to include or limit weight on companies with unequal voting rights, which is another place the structure can matter to a portfolio.

The common misconception is that "controlled" means "badly run" or "a scam." It does not. Some of the best long-term compounders in history have had dual-class control, because founders could invest for decades without worrying about activist pressure or quarterly pressure. The real nuance is that the exemption removes checks, not competence. A second misconception is that the exemption removes all rules. It does not. The company still has to follow securities law, file honest financials, have an audit committee that meets the independence rules for audit committees, and obey fiduciary duties under state law. What disappears are three specific exchange-level requirements. A third misunderstanding is mixing up economic ownership and control: in this structure, the Class F holder could own a small fraction of the equity and still win every vote.

When does this arrangement fail? The danger is that the controlling holder's interests and the public's interests diverge, and nobody on the board has the independence or the power to push back. Examples include paying executives generously, approving deals with entities the controller owns, blocking a takeover that would have rewarded outside holders, or changing the capital structure in ways that dilute the public. Because Class A holders cannot remove directors, their only remedy is to sell, which pushes the price down, or to sue under state fiduciary law, which is slow and uncertain. There is a second, quieter risk: succession. Control tied to a person or a trust depends on that person or trust staying wise and aligned. Some charters add sunset provisions that end the super-voting power after a set number of years or when a holder sells below a threshold, and those provisions are worth reading closely.

Controlled-company status connects to several other ideas in the video. It sits alongside the convertible-note story: the $72.4 billion fair-value charge was a receipt for value already promised to early investors, and the Class N no-vote shares show that those same early investors may hold economics without governance. Together they paint the picture of a pie that was sliced up before you arrived and a table where you do not get a chair. It also links to dilution, because the more shares issued, the smaller your slice, while the Class F vote stays fixed. And it ties to the host's broader warning that a headline number is rarely the full story. Before buying an IPO, read three things: the income statement, the share count, and the voting table. The last one tells you who is really in charge.
