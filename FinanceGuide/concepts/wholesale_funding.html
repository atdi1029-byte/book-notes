Wholesale Funding

Wholesale funding is money a bank borrows from institutions and markets instead of collecting it from ordinary customers. A retail deposit is the cash in your checking or savings account. It is spread across millions of small balances, it is sticky, and it often pays almost nothing. Wholesale funding is the opposite. It is large, negotiated, and priced at the going market rate. The main sources are Federal Home Loan Bank (FHLB) advances, overnight borrowing between banks in the federal funds market, repurchase agreements, brokered certificates of deposit, and large uninsured corporate deposits. A bank can see wholesale funding as a way to raise cash on demand. A skeptic sees it as the most expensive money the bank can get.

Wholesale funding matters because it shows how much stress a bank is under. A healthy bank with a loyal deposit base can run on cheap money. When the video says regional banks pay "2/10 of a percent to 4/10 of a percent on consumer checking," that low cost of funds is the profit engine of banking. When a bank has to replace that money with borrowed funds costing 4% or more, its whole earnings model changes. It also spreads into the real economy. A bank that pays more for money has less room to lend, so it tightens credit on home equity lines, small business facilities, and credit cards. This is the "stealth liquidity squeeze" the video describes.

The mechanics start with the balance sheet. A bank's assets, such as loans and bonds, must be funded by liabilities, mostly deposits. Suppose depositors withdraw $1 billion. The bank has to find $1 billion in cash from somewhere. It has three choices. It can sell assets, it can raise new equity, or it can borrow. Borrowing is often the fastest choice. The bank posts collateral, such as mortgages or securities, and takes an FHLB advance or borrows overnight. The cash arrives within hours, and the withdrawals are covered. The catch is that the borrowing carries the market rate, and the bank must roll it over again and again. Deposits leave quietly, and wholesale funding replaces them at a price that shows up in the income statement.

The video explains why a bank would borrow at 4% instead of selling assets. Banks classify bonds as held-to-maturity (HTM) when they intend to keep them until they mature. These bonds are carried at cost, not at market value. The video's number is $325.1 billion in unrealized losses across US commercial banks, according to the FDIC's quarterly banking profile. If a bank sold even a small piece of its HTM portfolio, accounting rules could "taint" the whole portfolio and force the bank to recognize those losses. So the bank borrows instead. The video calls this the second catalyst: the trapped bank pays "4% plus to borrow cash just to backfill deposits that were previously sitting there for free." Wholesale funding is the pressure valve that lets the bank avoid selling.

The margin math is where the damage shows. Net interest margin (NIM) is the interest a bank earns on assets minus the interest it pays on liabilities, divided by earning assets. The video sets up the extreme case: assets yielding 2% and funded by wholesale money costing 4%. That is a negative spread of two percentage points on every dollar replaced. Take a $10 billion bank that loses $1 billion of deposits and backfills them with 4% borrowing. The replaced dollars used to cost close to nothing, so the bank now pays about $40 million a year more. Meanwhile the bonds it holds keep paying the old 2%. In the video's words, the bank is "slowly liquidating your internal capital to keep the lights on."

Investors and traders use wholesale funding as a diagnostic. They look for several things in bank filings and the quarterly call reports:

  • A rising share of borrowings relative to total liabilities.
  • A rising proportion of uninsured deposits.
  • Growth in FHLB advances from one quarter to the next.
  • A falling net interest margin.
  • Growing unrealized losses on securities compared with tangible common equity.

Analysts also compare the loan-to-deposit ratio and the "cost of total deposits" across peers. A bank whose funding costs are jumping faster than its competitors' is either buying growth or plugging a hole. The video's point is that television analysts rate regional banks as "strong buys" while these funding metrics quietly deteriorate. Tangible common equity, which strips out goodwill and intangibles, is the number to hold against the unrealized losses.

"They pay 4% plus to borrow cash just to backfill deposits that were previously sitting there for free."

There are nuances the video skips. Wholesale funding is not automatically a red flag. Many well-run banks use it deliberately, for example to match the funding of a specific loan or to manage seasonal swings. FHLB advances are also collateralized and reliable, and they are one reason the system was designed the way it was. What matters is why the bank is borrowing and what it does with the money. A bank borrowing to fund profitable new loans is different from a bank borrowing to replace fleeing deposits against low-yielding bonds. Also, the "2% assets against 4% liabilities" scenario is the extreme. Real banks hold a mix of loans that have repriced upward, and their margins compress by degrees, not always into negative territory. Read the video's numbers as an illustration of the worst case, not the average bank.

Wholesale funding fails in a specific way. Deposits are stable in part because they are insured and small, and wholesale lenders are neither. They can refuse to roll over overnight loans, demand more collateral, or cut credit lines in a single day. That is a funding run, and it can happen faster than a classic bank run because it is done by computer and by treasurers who are professionally alert. The 2023 collapses of Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank showed how quickly uninsured deposits can leave. If the bank runs out of eligible collateral, it faces a choice. It can sell securities at a loss and realize the hole, or it can go to the regulators. This is why the video treats wholesale borrowing as the step just before intervention.

The concept connects to several others. The first is interest rate risk. When yields rise, bond prices fall, which creates the unrealized losses in the first place. The second is duration mismatch: long-dated assets funded by short-dated liabilities that reprice every day. The third is the deposit beta, which measures how much of a rate increase a bank passes on to depositors. Low betas protect margins until customers move their money to Treasury bills and government money market funds, as the video's first catalyst describes. Finally, it connects to capital ratios and credit availability. A bank squeezed on both sides shrinks its loan book, which is how bank stress becomes consumer stress.

For a household, the practical lesson is to watch behavior, not headlines. Look at how your bank pays and prices. If it pays far less than Treasury bills or money market funds yield, it can afford to because it is not competing for your cash. When deposit rates jump above the market, the bank may be paying up to plug holes. Keep balances within FDIC insurance limits, which are $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Notice tighter lending, lower credit limits, and slower wire approvals, which the video ties to funding stress. None of these signs proves a bank is failing. Together they tell you whether wholesale funding is a routine tool or a lifeline.
