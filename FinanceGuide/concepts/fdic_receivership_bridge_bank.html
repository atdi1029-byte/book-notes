FDIC Receivership and Bridge Bank Resolution

When a bank fails, it doesn't vanish overnight or get liquidated in a courtroom for years. A regulator, usually the state banking authority or the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, closes the bank and appoints the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation as its receiver. The receiver steps into the shoes of the failed institution. It controls the bank's assets, collects what borrowers owe, pays what the bank owes, and decides what to sell. The FDIC can also act as conservator, which means temporarily running a troubled bank, but receivership is the final stage. The bank's shareholders and management lose control, and the old corporate shell exists only to be wound down.

Receivership matters because it decides who gets paid, in what order, and how much of a bank's failure spills onto ordinary people. Banks are unusual businesses. They fund long-term loans with short-term deposits, so confidence is part of the balance sheet. If depositors think their money is stuck, they run, and the panic can spread to healthy banks. A well-run resolution keeps payments flowing, protects insured depositors quickly, and limits damage to the wider economy. It also sets a pecking order of claims, and that order is the hidden subject of the video. Whoever sits at the front of the line gets made whole first. Everyone behind them shares whatever remains.

The mechanics run in a fairly fixed sequence. A regulator closes the bank, usually on a Friday afternoon, and names the FDIC receiver. The FDIC takes inventory of the loans, securities, and obligations. It then picks a resolution, and the law requires the least-cost resolution, meaning the method that costs the Deposit Insurance Fund the least. Options include a straight sale of the bank, a payout of insured deposits, or a bridge bank. A bridge bank is a temporary, FDIC-controlled national bank chartered to take over the failed bank's deposits and many of its assets. It can operate for up to two years, with extensions, while the FDIC looks for a buyer. Customers see open branches and working debit cards.

The video's example is Silicon Valley Bank, which regulators closed on March 10, 2023, with the FDIC as receiver. Days earlier it had pulled its Federal Home Loan Bank advance up from $20 billion on March 1 to $30 billion on March 10, according to the Government Accountability Office. The FDIC then moved the business into a bridge bank. That bridge bank repaid the full $30 billion to the Home Loan Bank of San Francisco. It also paid $266.5 million in prepayment fees and another $18.8 million in waiver fees. The lender got its principal back and a premium for being repaid early. The receiver treated the advance as a senior claim and paid it first.

"They're not smarter than everybody else. They are senior to everybody else."

The seniority comes from Title 12 of the U.S. Code, section 1430(e). It gives a Home Loan Bank's security interest priority over the claims of other parties, and it names a receiver specifically. The video points out narrow exceptions, mainly other lenders with their own perfected claim on the same collateral. This is why the system can say it has never lost money on an advance. Every advance is fully collateralized, and in failure the Home Loan Bank collects from that collateral before the rest of the creditor line starts to form. The risk didn't disappear. It was passed along to whoever stands behind the secured lender.

Investors and savers can use this in a few practical ways. If you hold more than $250,000 at one bank, the amount above the limit is an uninsured claim against the receivership. It sits behind secured lenders, and you may get paid only in part and only over time. You can spread cash across banks, use an insured sweep network, or hold Treasury bills, which are direct government obligations. For bank stock owners, the video suggests reading the free quarterly call report, Schedule RC-M item 5.a, which shows Home Loan Bank advances. Rising advances alongside shrinking deposits suggest a bank is "renting" its funding, and the best collateral is already pledged away.

Several misconceptions are worth clearing up. A bridge bank is not a bailout of shareholders. In SVB's case, stockholders and bondholders were wiped out. Receivership also doesn't mean depositors lose everything. Insured depositors are protected up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Uninsured depositors rank ahead of general creditors under U.S. depositor preference law, but secured lenders are paid from their collateral before that waterfall begins. Also, "nobody lost money" can be misleading. The Home Loan Bank was repaid, but that payment came out of the failed bank's assets. A Richmond Fed paper cited in the video found that heavier advance use raises expected losses to the FDIC's insurance fund.

Resolution can go wrong in several ways. If a failed bank is so large that the least-cost method is hard to execute, the FDIC may struggle to find a buyer in a single weekend. In March 2023 regulators invoked a systemic risk exception to protect all SVB and Signature depositors, which shows the normal rules sometimes bend under stress. When secured lenders take the best collateral, the remaining assets may not cover insured claims, so the Deposit Insurance Fund absorbs the gap. That fund is replenished through special assessments on surviving banks, which are in turn passed on to customers. Bridge banks also have a deadline, so a failed sale process can push the FDIC toward a slower liquidation.

This concept ties several of the video's themes together. Home Loan Bank advances are the secured claim that comes first. The Fed's discount window is the stigmatized alternative, which is why banks often prefer the "quiet door." Unrealized securities losses, which the FDIC put at $326.7 billion, matter because banks can pledge underwater bonds as collateral instead of selling them. Uninsured deposits, which rose an estimated $317 billion in the second quarter, are the money most exposed if a bank fails. The practical rule for understanding any bank's risk is to ask who is ahead of you in line, and how much good collateral has already been promised to them.
