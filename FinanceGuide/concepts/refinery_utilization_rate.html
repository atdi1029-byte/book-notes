Refinery Utilization Rate

Refinery utilization rate measures what percentage of a refinery's total processing capacity is actually being run at any given time, expressed as a percentage of the maximum barrels per day the refining system could theoretically process. A rate of 96.8% means American refiners are converting crude oil into finished fuels — gasoline, diesel, jet fuel — at 96.8% of their maximum possible throughput. This number is published weekly by the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) and is one of the most closely watched, least understood numbers in energy markets. It is not a measure of oil supply. It is a measure of how hard the industrial machinery that turns oil into usable fuel is being pushed, week after week.

This number matters because refining capacity, not crude oil, is usually the actual bottleneck in fuel markets. As the video bluntly puts it, "you don't burn crude oil. Nobody does. Crude is a raw ingredient." Crude can be sitting in tankers by the millions of barrels, and prices for gasoline and diesel can still spike to record highs — because the physical plants that convert crude into something a truck or a car can actually burn are running flat out and can't process any more. When utilization is near its ceiling, there's no slack left in the system. Any unplanned outage — a fire, a hurricane, a mechanical failure — removes capacity that cannot be replaced quickly, and prices for finished fuel spike even while crude oil is falling.

Mechanically, refineries run a seasonal cycle. Every autumn, once summer driving season winds down, refiners are supposed to throttle back and take units offline for scheduled maintenance — called turnaround season. This is deliberate: pumps, towers, and catalytic crackers wear down and need inspection and repair, and doing that work in September and October, before winter heating-oil demand and before next summer's driving season, is the industry's normal rhythm. Utilization rates drop by a few points during these weeks as a matter of routine housekeeping. Skipping or shortening turnaround season doesn't make the wear and tear disappear — it just defers the day the equipment forces a shutdown, and that day tends to arrive as an unplanned, more disruptive outage rather than a scheduled one.

The video's real-world example is precise and damning. Last September, utilization fell in an orderly, visible sequence: 94.9%, then 93.3%, then 93%, then 91.4% — a normal seasonal decline as refiners took units down for repairs. This September, utilization sat at 96.8%, a full three and a half points higher than the same point a year earlier, during the exact weeks refiners are supposed to be doing maintenance instead of maximizing output.

"American refiners are running three and a half points hotter than this time last year. During the exact weeks they're supposed to be actually shut down for repairs, my friends, that's not strength. That is deferred maintenance."

The reason refiners are skipping their normal seasonal slowdown connects directly to the diesel shortage driving the rest of the video. Diesel hit an all-time record of $6.53 a gallon even as crude oil broke below $100, because Russian refineries — knocked offline by war damage — stopped supplying the diesel export markets they once served, and that demand redirected to U.S. Gulf Coast refiners. With the world leaning harder than usual on American refining capacity, refiners have an incentive to keep running near their ceiling rather than take capacity offline for maintenance, even though every week of deferred repairs adds risk to the system. Utilization had actually peaked even higher, near 98%, in August, before easing slightly.

Traders and analysts use utilization rate less as a standalone number and more as a diagnostic for the health of the whole refining system, read alongside two companion numbers: the crack spread (the profit margin refiners earn turning crude into finished fuel, which sat over $111 a barrel on the Gulf Coast that week versus a normal $20-30) and distillate inventories (which were about 16 million barrels, or 13%, below their five-year average). A high utilization rate combined with a wide crack spread and thin inventories is the signature of a refining system with no cushion — every barrel matters, and there's no slack to absorb a disruption. The video's own trading advice is to watch whether utilization stays "pinned near 97% instead of dropping for maintenance," because that would mean refiners are continuing to borrow against next year's reliability to meet this year's demand.

The gotcha is that a high utilization rate can look like good news — refiners running hard, meeting demand, keeping fuel flowing — when it is actually a warning sign. People instinctively read "97% capacity" as strength, the same way a factory running near full output looks healthy. But in refining, capacity utilization near the ceiling during a period when it's supposed to be low is closer to a car engine redlined on a road trip when it was scheduled for an oil change. It runs fine for a while. The risk isn't today's output; it's what breaks three months from now because the inspection that would have caught it never happened. This is also why utilization has to be read in the context of the calendar — 96.8% in July is unremarkable, while 96.8% in late September, deep into turnaround season, is a genuine anomaly.

When deferred maintenance finally catches up with a refinery, the failure mode is an unplanned outage: a unit goes down unexpectedly rather than on a scheduled, staggered basis, and because so many refiners were all running hot at once instead of staggering their maintenance normally, multiple outages can cluster together and remove a large chunk of national capacity at once. Because refined products can't simply be shipped anywhere — the video notes the pipeline network runs in fixed directions, so Gulf Coast diesel can't easily reach New England, which is why the East Coast imports over a million barrels a day of finished product — localized refinery failures can cause regional fuel spikes even when national crude supply is abundant. This is the same "plumbing, not molecules" constraint that made a diesel export ban politically appealing but economically dangerous: refining industry estimates suggested a ban could idle 1.3 million barrels a day of capacity, about 7% of the country's refining, precisely because the system has so little spare running room left to absorb a shock.

Refinery utilization rate connects directly to the crack spread concept covered elsewhere in this guide — utilization is the mechanical cause, and the crack spread is the price effect. It also connects to the idea of distillate inventories as a buffer: when inventories are thin and utilization is maxed out, the system has no shock absorber left, which is exactly the setup the video describes as a "diesel trap" — a shortage of refining capacity that no amount of cheap crude oil can fix.
