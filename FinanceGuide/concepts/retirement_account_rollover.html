401(k) Rollover to IRA

A 401(k) rollover to an IRA moves your retirement savings from an employer-sponsored plan into an Individual Retirement Account that you open and control yourself. The 401(k) belongs to your employer's plan: the employer picks the menu of funds, hires the record keeper, and sets the rules. An IRA is held at a brokerage or bank of your choosing, usually with thousands of investment options. When you leave a job or retire, you can leave the money where it is, move it to a new employer's plan, cash it out (usually a bad idea), or roll it over. Done correctly, a rollover is not a taxable event. The money keeps its tax-deferred status and simply changes homes.

Rollovers matter far beyond individual convenience, because the people doing them are a huge, predictable flow of money. More than 10,000 Americans turn 65 every day, and when they retire, many leave the restrictive corporate plan for a self-directed IRA. Multiplied across millions of retirees, that is a steady stream of redemptions out of whatever the 401(k) held, including stable value funds. Trillions of dollars sit in 401(k) plans, so a change in how retirees behave can pressure the funds, insurers, and bond portfolios behind those plans. In the video, this retiree exit is the trigger that tests the whole stable value structure.

Mechanically, there are two routes. In a direct rollover, you ask the plan's record keeper to send the money straight to your IRA custodian, either by wire, by electronic transfer, or by a check made payable to the new custodian "for the benefit of" you. No taxes are withheld. In an indirect rollover, the plan cuts a check to you personally, and withholds 20% for federal taxes. You then have 60 days to deposit the full amount, including the withheld 20% from your own pocket, into the IRA. Miss the deadline and the unrolled portion becomes taxable income, plus a 10% penalty if you are under 59½. Almost everyone should choose the direct route.

The sequence goes like this. First, you open the receiving IRA, since the new custodian needs an account number. Second, you contact your old plan's record keeper and request a distribution with rollover instructions. Third, the record keeper sells or liquidates your holdings in the plan, because most plan investments cannot be moved "in kind." Fourth, the cash is sent. Fifth, the IRA custodian receives it and you invest it. The key point is the third step. Your balance must be converted into cash before it leaves, and that conversion is where a fund's own rules can slow things down. A plain index fund redeems in a day. A stable value fund may not.

The video uses rollovers as the pressure point for a $900 billion stable value market. Stable value funds hold intermediate bonds and use insurance "wrap" contracts to report a steady $1 share price. The host argues that bond yields rising toward 5.34% on the 10-year and 5.60% on the 30-year pushed the market-to-book ratio down to roughly 85–88%. A wrap contract is supposed to cover that gap when a worker retires or quits. So a retiree rolling over is exactly the kind of event the insurer must pay for. The video's claim is that mass rollovers could strain insurers and trigger a 12-month delay.

"Don't assume that a click on an online dashboard releases same-day wire of settlement when wrap contract provisions are under balance sheet stress."

For investors, the practical lesson is to treat a rollover as a project with a timeline, not a button. If you hold a stable value fund and plan to retire within a year, check three things early: the fund's market-to-book ratio in its annual disclosure, whether your plan has any transfer or withdrawal restrictions, and whether your employer is changing record keepers or terminating the plan. The host suggests that a ratio below 92% signals meaningful unrealized losses. Then plan the timing. Begin the paperwork months ahead, keep an emergency cash cushion outside the plan, and consider holding Treasury bills directly in an IRA or brokerage account where there is no wrap contract.

Several nuances get lost in the alarm. First, individual participants' rollovers are generally honored at book value, because the wrap contract covers "participant-initiated" benefit events like retirement, death, disability, or termination. The restrictions the video describes mostly apply to plan-level events, such as an employer terminating the plan, a record-keeper switch, or a mass exit. Second, the "equity wash" rule limits moving money directly into competing funds like money markets within the plan. It does not stop a rollover out of the plan altogether. Third, a stable value fund does not magically hold less than $1, since losses are amortized over time through the crediting rate. The video's 12-month gate is a legitimate contract feature, but how often it is triggered is much less certain than the video implies.

Where things break, the failure is a delay or a loss of access, not usually a loss of principal. If a plan-level trigger hits, the insurer can require up to 12 months' notice before paying out at book value. That means a retiree who planned to roll over in January might receive the money late, missing a planned house purchase, a required minimum distribution, or a chance to re-invest while markets move. The worst case is an insurer weakened by the very losses it guaranteed, so the promise depends on the insurer's solvency. Another trap is the indirect rollover. A retiree who receives a check, spends part of it, and cannot replace the withheld 20% creates an unplanned tax bill.

A rollover connects to nearly every other idea in the video. It is the redemption event that activates the wrap contract, and it is what produces the "run on the wrap" the host describes. It interacts with market-to-book ratio, since the lower that ratio falls, the more the insurer owes on each exit. It also ties to duration risk, because those intermediate bonds lose value as yields rise. Finally, it links to Treasury bills and money market funds as the alternative parking spots. Understanding a rollover means seeing that moving money is simple, but the instrument you are leaving can decide how quickly you can move it.
