Truth in Lending Act (TILA) APR Disclosure

The Truth in Lending Act (TILA) is a federal law passed in 1968 that forces lenders to disclose the true cost of borrowing in a standardized way, so consumers can compare loans on an apples-to-apples basis. The centerpiece of that disclosure is the Annual Percentage Rate, or APR — a single number that rolls the interest rate together with points, lender fees, and other financing costs into one comparable figure. This is distinct from the plain note rate (also called the interest rate), which only reflects interest charged on the principal. TILA doesn't cap what a lender can charge or ban aggressive marketing. It simply requires the real cost to be printed somewhere on the document, in a specific, defined way, even when the marketing headline says something far more attractive.

This disclosure matters because marketing numbers and financing costs are structurally incentivized to diverge, especially in an environment where sellers are subsidizing rates to move inventory. A big banner rate draws attention and drives foot traffic; the APR is the boring number regulators require so that attention-grabbing rate can't quietly hide fees, points, or a temporary teaser structure. Without a mandated, standardized APR, a buyer comparing a builder's promotional loan against a bank's plain-vanilla mortgage would have no reliable way to know which one actually costs less over time. The APR exists to protect the comparison itself — it's a market-transparency mechanism, not a marketing tool, and that's exactly why it typically gets printed in the smallest type on the page.

Mechanically, the APR calculation works by taking every financing cost associated with the loan — the interest rate (including any teaser or step-up structure over the life of the loan), discount points, origination fees, and certain closing costs — and converting them into a single annualized percentage that reflects the effective cost of borrowing if the loan is held to term. If a rate steps up over time (say, low in year one, higher in year two, and higher still for the remaining term), the APR isn't just the first-year number — it blends the entire structure into one rate. Lenders are legally required to calculate and disclose this figure using a standardized formula, which is why it can be compared across totally different loan products, lenders, and fee structures without the consumer having to reverse-engineer anything themselves.

The video's real-world example makes this concrete. A homebuilder in Greenville, South Carolina advertised a mortgage with a headline rate of 3.99% in year one, stepping up to 4.99% in year two, then settling at 5.99% for the remaining 28 years. The sign made it look like a sub-4% mortgage. But the same flyer's legally mandated disclosure listed an APR of 6.854% — the number that accounts for the full step-structure plus whatever points and fees the builder's mortgage arm baked in to make the teaser possible. As the video puts it:

"Both numbers are true. Only one of them is real."
The 3.99% is real in the sense that it's the rate charged in year one. But the APR is the number that tells you what the whole package actually costs.

Savvy buyers and investors use the APR as the default comparison tool specifically because it neutralizes marketing tricks. The practical move illustrated in the video is to take the disclosed APR and stack it directly against prevailing market benchmarks — the average 30-year fixed rate on lender rate sheets, or the average FHA rate, published weekly and widely available. In the example, market 30-year fixed rates were running at 7.19%, and FHA rates at 6.81%, against the builder's 6.854% APR. That comparison instantly reveals the real size of the discount: not the 320 basis points implied by the sign (3.99% vs. 7.19%), but roughly 34 basis points against the conventional benchmark — and the builder's package was actually slightly worse than a plain FHA loan. Anyone shopping a promotional mortgage should do exactly this: find the APR, then call an outside lender and compare it to what's actually available in the market that week.

The biggest misconception is treating the headline teaser rate as the loan's real cost, when it's often just the first leg of a multi-year step structure designed to look dramatic on a sign. A related gotcha is assuming a bigger gap between the teaser rate and market rates means a bigger discount — it doesn't, because the teaser is temporary and the APR captures the entire life of the loan, including years where the rate resets higher. Another subtlety: APR can actually run higher than a loan's simple note rate once fees and points are included, which is why an APR that looks only modestly below market can still represent a genuinely weak deal once you account for what the borrower gave up in fees to get there. The instinct to compare "sign rate versus market rate" instead of "APR versus market rate" is the single most common way buyers overstate how good a promotional financing deal actually is.

TILA disclosure breaks down as a consumer protection when buyers don't read past the sign, or when the asterisked fine print is functionally illegible — printed in type small enough that, as the video notes, you'd have to stop the car and walk up to read it. The law doesn't require the disclosure to be prominent, only present and accurate, which means full compliance can coexist with a badly misleading first impression. There's also a real economic edge case behind why builders lean on short teaser periods rather than permanent rate buydowns: research cited in the video (from the American Enterprise Institute) estimates that permanently buying a mortgage rate down by a full percentage point costs a builder roughly 3.2% of the sale price. Buying a rate down by more than two points, as in the example, would consume close to half the builder's entire gross margin on that house — which is exactly why the deal is structured as a 2-year step-down instead of a 30-year rate cut. The APR is what exposes that structural constraint to the buyer, whether or not the builder wants it exposed.

The APR disclosure connects directly to how sellers move subsidy value around without changing the total amount subsidized. In the video's data, Lennar's incentive rate as a percentage of sale price actually fell over the year — from 14.3% to 12.0% — while the average home price also dropped, from $383,000 to $372,000. Run the dollars and the total giveback per house barely moved, changing by about $871 on a roughly $400,000 home. What changed was the form of the subsidy: less money went into rate buydowns (invisible, temporary, and specific to one buyer's loan) and more went into the sticker price cut (permanent, visible in every future appraisal and comp on the street). Because APR disclosure makes rate buydowns transparent and comparable, it's part of what allows this kind of shift to be reconstructed from public numbers — connecting directly to concepts like builder incentive rates, gross margin compression, and the comp-based nature of home appraisals.
