Comparable Sales (Comps) / Appraisal Method

When an appraiser or a real estate agent needs to figure out what a house is worth, they don't pull a number out of thin air — they look at comparable sales, commonly called comps. Comps are recently sold homes that are similar in size, location, age, condition, and features to the property being valued. The appraisal method takes those comps, adjusts for differences (an extra bedroom here, a renovated kitchen there), and arrives at an estimated market value. This is the backbone of nearly every home valuation in America — it's how appraisers justify a mortgage amount to a bank, how agents set listing prices, and how county assessors sometimes calibrate property taxes. If you've ever wondered "how do they know what my house is worth," the answer is almost always: they looked at what the house next door, or three streets over, actually sold for.

Comps matter because they are the mechanism through which one transaction becomes everyone's problem — or everyone's benefit. A single home sale doesn't stay contained to the two parties involved. Once it closes and gets recorded, it becomes public data that every future appraiser, agent, and automated valuation model in that neighborhood will reference. This is why the video's closing insight is so important: a builder's decision to cut the sticker price on a new home rather than subsidize the mortgage rate isn't just a marketing choice — it's a decision that permanently alters the comp data for an entire subdivision. Comps are the transmission mechanism by which private pricing decisions become public, durable, neighborhood-wide facts.

Mechanically, here's how it works. An appraiser researching a home pulls three to six recent sales — typically within the last three to six months, within a mile or so, of similar square footage and style. They record the sale price as it was recorded on the deed, which is the actual dollar amount the property transferred for. They then make dollar adjustments for differences: if the comp has a pool and the subject property doesn't, subtract an estimated pool value; if the comp is 200 square feet smaller, add value per square foot. The adjusted comp prices get averaged or weighted to produce a final appraised value. Crucially, the appraiser can only see the number on the deed — the recorded sale price. They cannot see, and do not adjust for, financing incentives that never touched the price itself.

This is exactly the mechanism at play in the Lennar example. Lennar's incentive spend actually fell over the past year — from a 14.3% incentive rate to 12%, per the video — but the composition of that incentive shifted. A year ago, the average Lennar home sold for $383,000 with $54,769 in incentives, much of it buried in a mortgage rate buydown that never touches the recorded sale price. This quarter, the average home sold for $372,000 with $44,640 in incentives — a smaller rate buydown, but an $11,000 lower sticker price. That $372,000 is what gets recorded on the deed. That's the number every future appraiser pulls as a comp. The rate buydown, no matter how large, is invisible to the public record — it lived inside a private financing agreement between the builder's mortgage arm and one buyer.

"A rate buy down is invisible to your neighbors. A price cut is permanent and it shows up in every appraisal and every comp on that street forever. One of them props up the neighborhood. The other one reprices it."

Investors and analysts who follow homebuilder stocks watch which lever a builder pulls — price cuts versus rate buydowns — because it tells them something the headline incentive percentage doesn't. A builder holding sticker prices flat while stuffing incentives into rate buydowns is protecting the neighborhood's comp base, which protects the value of homes it hasn't sold yet and protects existing homeowners' equity. A builder cutting sticker prices, even by a small amount, is trading near-term unit sales for a repricing of the entire area. Homeowners with equity lines of credit, adjustable-rate mortgages tied to loan-to-value ratios, or plans to sell within a few years should care deeply about which strategy the new-construction developer down the street is using, because it will show up as their own home's next appraisal.

The common misconception is that all builder incentives are roughly equivalent — that a "$10,000 discount" is a $10,000 discount no matter how it's delivered. The math in the video destroys that assumption from the other direction too: the American Enterprise Institute found that buying a mortgage rate down by a full percentage point costs a builder roughly 3.2% of the sale price, permanently, for the life of the loan. A 2.21-percentage-point buydown (from 7.2% to 4.99%) would cost roughly 7.1% of the price — nearly half of Lennar's entire 15.8% gross margin on that one house. Builders literally cannot afford large permanent buydowns, which is why the "3.99%" on the sign is a two-year teaser, not a real 30-year rate — and why the APR disclosure (6.854% in the Greenville example, against a market average of 7.19%) revealed the whole package was worth only 34 basis points versus market, not the 320 basis points the sticker number implied.

Comps break down as a valuation tool under a few conditions, and it's worth knowing them. First, in a market flooded with rate-buydown incentives, comps can overstate true value, because the recorded price doesn't reflect the real economic cost the seller absorbed — this was a widely discussed distortion during periods of high incentive use. Second, comps lag: they only reflect what already closed, so in a fast-moving market they tell you where prices were, not where they're going. Third, thin markets — rural areas, unique properties, luxury homes — often lack enough truly comparable sales, forcing appraisers to stretch geography or timeframe, which weakens reliability. Finally, comps can create feedback loops: a builder cutting prices to move inventory drags down comps, which can pressure existing homeowners nearby to cut their own asking prices to stay competitive, compounding the effect across an entire subdivision.

This concept connects directly to loan-to-value ratio and mortgage underwriting: banks lend against appraised value, so a repriced neighborhood changes how much every future buyer there can borrow. It also connects to homebuilder gross margin economics — the reason builders prefer invisible rate buydowns over visible price cuts is precisely that price cuts show up in comps and permanently compress what the builder, and every neighbor, can command going forward. And it ties into the broader theme of the video: two incentive packages can have nearly identical dollar value to the builder's income statement, yet have completely different, durable effects on neighborhood wealth, depending entirely on which side of the closing document — the price line or the financing terms — the discount was booked to.
