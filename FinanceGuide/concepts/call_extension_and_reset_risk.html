Call Extension Risk and Reset Risk (Perpetual Bonds)

Most bonds are simple promises: a borrower gets your money today, pays interest along the way, and returns your principal on a fixed maturity date. A perpetual bond breaks that pattern. It has no maturity date at all, so the issuer is never obliged to repay the principal. Instead, it gets the right, but not the duty, to call the bond, meaning buy it back at par (face value), usually on a specific date five or so years after issue and on later dates after that. Banks sell these in two main forms: Europe's additional tier one (AT1) bonds, nicknamed CoCos, and the United States' fixed-to-reset preferred shares. They are the "junior" debt that sits just above common stock in a bank's capital structure.

Why should anyone outside a bank's treasury department care? Because this paper is the shock absorber of the financial system. After 2008, regulators decided taxpayers should not be the first to pay when a bank got into trouble, so they forced banks to hold a layer of capital that could be wiped out or converted to equity first. AT1s and bank preferreds are that layer. Their pricing tells you how nervous professional investors are about bank health, and it determines what lending costs the banks themselves. When this market seizes up, banks pay more for capital, and that eventually means tighter credit for businesses and households. It is a small market with an outsized signal.

Here is the mechanism step by step. A bank issues a perpetual bond with a stated coupon, say 7%, and a first call date five years out. Because the bond has no maturity, the investor's return depends on an assumption: that the bank will call it on that date. If it does, the investor gets par back and can reinvest. If the bank does not call, the bond lives on, and at the call date its coupon resets. The new coupon is typically the then-current benchmark rate (a five-year swap or Treasury rate) plus the original credit spread fixed on day one. So the investor holds an instrument whose life and income both hinge on a decision that belongs entirely to the issuer.

That structure creates the two risks in the title. Extension risk is the danger that the issuer does not call, so a bond you thought would last five years stays outstanding for ten, twenty, or forever, locked at whatever yield it then pays. Reset risk is the danger that the new coupon is not what you hoped. If the original spread was set when markets were euphoric and tight, the reset adds that thin spread to the benchmark, which may be well below what the market now demands for such risk. Together they mean the bond can end up underpaying you for a longer period than you ever planned on. The bond's price, in turn, drops to reflect this.

The bank's calling decision is economic, not sentimental. The bank calls if replacing the old bond with a new one costs less than leaving it in place. Suppose a bank has a perpetual paying a reset spread of 400 basis points over the benchmark. If new AT1s would cost it 600 over, calling and reissuing is expensive, so it may skip the call. Investors have long assumed banks would always call to protect their reputation in the market, and for years that held. But the assumption is a habit, not a contract. In 2020, 2022, and 2023 some issuers did surprise holders, and the market punished the whole class each time, reminding everyone the option belongs to the borrower.

The video gives a vivid, real-time example. Over the prior two years, yield-hungry money managers chased bank capital until Bloomberg's global CoCo (AT1) index spread fell below 200 basis points for the first time ever. Then, in the week France's bond spread over Germany jumped 32 basis points, it snapped back to 253. Year-to-date returns dropped from 3.6% in August to 1.6%. Bank of New York Mellon had to price its preferred shares 32.4 basis points wider than its summer post-crisis low. Banco BPM sought €400 million, saw €1.5 billion of early orders, then watched about half of that book vanish. Belfius's new AT1 slipped to 99 cents on the euro almost immediately.

"They are refusing to buy junior bank debt at tight spreads because extension risk and reset risk are biting. They're not willing to cushion bank balance sheets without a substantial hazard pay sweetener."

To see why the hazard pay is rational, consider the arithmetic of a tight spread. An AT1 priced at 200 over the benchmark gives you only a modest premium for risk that includes a total wipe-out, as Credit Suisse holders learned in March 2023 when roughly $17 billion of AT1s went to zero overnight. If the bank then declines to call and resets at that same slim spread, you are stuck with a thin premium for an indefinite period. Investors respond in three ways: demand a wider spread at issuance, insist on a higher initial coupon, or simply stay away. Traders also watch yield-to-call versus yield-to-worst and the bond's price relative to par. A price drifting below par signals the market doubts the call.

Practitioners watch a handful of signals. The index spread level (below 200 was "ridiculously tight"), new-issue concessions (the extra yield a bank gives to get a deal done), order-book behavior (a book that shrinks as pricing firms up is a warning), and first-day trading below par. A professional also compares the reset spread against where the bond's current market spread sits. If the market spread is far wider than the reset spread, the bond is "out of the money" to call, and the extension scenario becomes the base case. Some investors deliberately buy such bonds at deep discounts, betting the bank will call anyway, and treat any extension as a bonus yield.

There are several gotchas. First, a "perpetual" bond is priced as if it were short, so the quoted yield can mislead; yield-to-call assumes a call that may never come. Second, a high coupon is not safety; it is payment for subordination and these risks. Third, extension is not the same as default. The bank may keep paying, yet your capital stays trapped and the price falls. Fourth, regulators can also stop coupon payments on AT1s without it being a default, which compounds the problem. Finally, American fixed-to-reset preferreds and European AT1s differ in details such as conversion triggers, but both share the same call-and-reset skeleton. Never assume the two behave identically in a crisis.

When it all breaks, the damage comes fast because the market is thin. Buyers step back, new issues require big concessions, and existing bonds gap down, as with Belfius's fall to 99. In the worst case, the trigger events are not about calls at all: a bank's capital ratio falls below a threshold and the bond converts to equity or is written down, as with Credit Suisse. Extension and reset risk are the slow version of that pain, while write-down is the fast version. And because they concern the weakest layer of bank capital, stress in this corner spreads to bank equity, bank funding, and eventually lending.

This concept ties into several others in the video. The widening in French-German spreads is the sovereign counterpart: when a government's solvency is questioned, its domestic banks, which hold lots of its debt, become suspect too, and junior bank paper is the first thing sold. That selling is the flight-to-quality trade: money leaves junior credit and flows into short-duration Treasuries, which is why the 10-year US yield fell from above 5% to 4.96%. Related ideas include duration (extension lengthens it, hurting prices when rates rise), credit spreads, subordination, and new-issue concession. If you can explain how a bond that "should" last five years becomes a bond that lasts forever, and why investors demand extra pay for that possibility, you can talk about this market with confidence.
