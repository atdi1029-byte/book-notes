Tender Offer / Share Repurchase Program (Non-Traded Funds)

A tender offer in a non-traded fund is the formal, scheduled window in which the fund offers to buy back some of its own shares from investors. The word "tender" confuses people because it also describes a hostile takeover bid. Here it means something calmer: the fund announces a price, a deadline, and a maximum amount it will repurchase, and shareholders choose whether to "tender" (submit) shares. Non-traded business development companies (BDCs) and interval-style credit funds use this process because they have no stock exchange where holders can simply sell. The share repurchase program is the standing policy that governs these offers. It is the only exit door, and the fund controls how wide it opens.

This matters because the exit door is the real product you are buying. A non-traded private credit fund sells a yield of 10 to 12 percent, but the fine print promises only the chance to redeem, never a guarantee. The video describes roughly $7.1 billion of redemption requests hitting the largest private credit funds in the first half of the year, which it calls the largest liquidity call in direct lending history. That figure tests the promise of "quarterly liquidity." The market for private credit grew to around $1.7 trillion largely on retail and wealth-platform money, and those investors treat the fund like a savings vehicle. When redemption requests collide with the repurchase limit, the gap between expectation and contract becomes painfully visible.

Mechanically, the process runs on a quarterly rhythm. First, the board of directors decides whether to conduct a tender that quarter and how much to offer, usually capped at about 5 percent of net asset value (NAV) or outstanding shares. Second, the fund files a notice with the SEC and mails it to shareholders, stating the price (typically the most recent NAV), the deadline, and the dollar limit. Third, investors submit redemption notices during the window and can usually withdraw them before it closes. Fourth, the fund totals the requests. If they fit under the cap, everyone is paid in full. If they exceed it, the fund prorates, paying each requester the same percentage of what they asked for. Cash settles a few weeks later.

Here is the video's example, worked through with numbers. Suppose a fund has $10 billion of NAV and a 5 percent quarterly cap, so it can repurchase $500 million. If investors tender $1 billion, requests are twice the limit, and every requester receives 50 percent of the request. The video uses a smaller case: ask to withdraw $100,000, and if the pool is badly oversubscribed you might receive only $10,000, leaving 90 percent stuck. In that scenario the proration factor is 10 percent. The investor did nothing wrong, and the fund did nothing illegal. The contract worked exactly as written, which is the unsettling part of the story.

"Prorating means if you ask to withdraw $100,000 and the pool was oversubscribed, well, you might receive only $10,000. The remaining 90% of your asset stays pinned inside that illiquid vehicle."

Investors and analysts use tender results as a real-time stress gauge. The video's audit checklist makes this concrete: open the latest 10-Q (or the tender offer results filing, Schedule TO-I and its amendments) and find the line item on repurchases. Did the fund honor 100 percent of tenders, or restrict them under its cap? Full honoring suggests liquidity is comfortable. Proration suggests more people want out than the structure can handle. Watch the trend, not just one quarter. Requests that rise from 3 percent to 6 percent to 9 percent of NAV signal accelerating redemption velocity. Also compare requests to new subscriptions, because a fund relying on fresh inflows to fund exits is fragile.

Several nuances trip people up. First, the 5 percent cap is usually not a statute; it is a limit written into the fund's prospectus and board policy. The video calls it "statutory," but the more accurate description is "contractual and board-discretionary." The same discretion lets the board tender less than 5 percent, or skip a quarter entirely. Second, the fund price is NAV, which is partly based on management's own valuation of loans that never trade. So a tender at "100 cents on the dollar" is only as reliable as those marks. Third, quarterly liquidity is a feature offered at the board's discretion, not a right. Many investors also confuse these funds with interval funds, which have stricter mandatory repurchase rules, so check which structure you hold.

When the system breaks, the options narrow quickly. The board can prorate, which spreads the pain but signals trouble and often triggers even larger requests next quarter, because investors rush to get in line. It can sell loans at secondary market discounts, which crystallizes losses and lowers NAV for those who stay. It can borrow against a credit line to pay redeemers, which adds leverage to an already leveraged portfolio; the video notes that Fitch-rated vehicles alone issued over $25 billion in new corporate debt to support fund-level liquidity. Or it can suspend or cancel the tender altogether, which the prospectus permits. Each path transfers risk from the leaving investor to the remaining ones, creating a first-mover advantage that resembles a classic bank run.

The deeper cause is a maturity mismatch: loans with 3 to 4 year average maturities, funded by shares that investors expect to cash out every quarter. The video ties this to the rate environment. Loans priced at SOFR plus 600 to 700 basis points cost borrowers about 6.5 percent when SOFR was near zero, but roughly 10.4 to 11 percent with SOFR near 3.88 percent. Borrowers who cannot pay in cash may pay in kind (PIK), adding to the debt instead. That keeps reported defaults low and NAV flat, which delays write-downs. But once new money slows and investors compare 10 percent private yields to 5 percent Treasuries, the true health of the portfolio is tested at the exit gate.

A tender offer connects to several other checks in the video's audit list. The PIK share of income tells you whether reported yield is real cash; if more than 10 to 15 percent of interest is PIK, redemption pressure is more dangerous because the fund has less cash coming in. The asset coverage ratio, which requires BDCs to hold at least 150 percent of assets against debt, limits how much the fund can borrow to meet redemptions. And SOFR drives borrower stress. Read together, these show whether a fund can keep honoring tenders. In short: a high yield is partly a payment for illiquidity, and the tender results show how much of that cost you are actually bearing.

To discuss this confidently, remember four points. The tender is a scheduled, capped, board-controlled opportunity, not a guaranteed withdrawal right. Proration is the first visible sign of stress, and suspension is the last resort. A smooth tender history proves little if the NAV marks are generous or inflows are covering exits. Check the 10-Q or the tender filings every quarter, and size your position as money you could be unable to touch for years. As the video puts it, the volatility was never eliminated; it was stored behind a locked door. Understanding who holds the key, and what the contract lets them do, is the whole lesson.
