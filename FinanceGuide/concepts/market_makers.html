Market Makers

A market maker is a firm that stands ready, all day long, to both buy and sell a particular security at publicly quoted prices. Think of a currency exchange booth at an airport. It posts two prices: one it will pay you for your euros, and a slightly higher one it will charge to sell you euros. It does not care whether euros are going up or down. It cares that someone is always at the window. A stock market maker does the same thing for shares of Nvidia, Apple or a tiny ETF, with the quotes updating many times per second. If you want to sell, there is a firm willing to buy. If you want to buy, there is a firm willing to sell. That standing willingness is the service.

Why does this matter? Because without it, trading would be slow, awkward and expensive. Imagine wanting to sell 100 shares of a stock and having to wait until another person happened to want exactly 100 shares at your price. Market makers supply liquidity, which is the ability to trade quickly without moving the price much. That affects you directly. Your 401(k) index fund, your brokerage account and your retirement date all depend on being able to get in and out at fair prices. Tight, competitive quotes from market makers are a big reason that trading a share costs pennies today instead of the dollars it cost decades ago, when quotes were wide and the middlemen kept most of the gap.

Here is the mechanism, step by step. A market maker posts a bid, the price at which it will buy, and an ask (or offer), the price at which it will sell. The gap between them is the bid-ask spread. Say a stock is quoted $100.00 bid and $100.02 ask. A seller hits the bid and the firm buys at $100.00. A moment later a buyer lifts the ask and the firm sells at $100.02. The firm just earned two cents per share, with no opinion about the company. Do that millions of times a day across thousands of stocks and tiny spreads become a large business. The firm is not betting on direction. It is renting out its balance sheet and its speed, and collecting a fee for it.

The risk in that business is inventory. Every time the firm buys from a seller, it temporarily owns shares it did not plan on owning, and the price could fall before someone buys them. So market makers constantly manage that exposure. They hedge by trading related instruments, such as futures, options or other stocks, and they adjust their quotes to push inventory back toward zero. If they have bought too much, they lower both the bid and the ask to attract buyers and discourage sellers. If they have sold too much, they raise them. This is why quotes jump around even when no news has hit. The firm is not predicting anything. It is balancing its books.

Now the video's example. On the Thursday Nvidia opened down about 13% after the CEO resignation, the presenter pointed out that Vanda Research counted a record $111 million of net retail buying in the first 30 minutes, while 748 million shares traded over the whole day. Even priced at the day's low of $148.80, that is at least $111 billion of trading. Every one of those shares had a buyer and a seller. Who was on the other side of the retail orders? Often a market maker. The presenter lists the players with deep enough pockets to keep paying up for a $4 trillion company: pension funds, mutual funds, hedge funds and

"the big trading firms that make markets all day long."

Notice what that implies. When retail was a net buyer of $111 million, someone was a net seller to them. Market makers are often the ones who take the other side of small orders, which is why a record retail buying burst does not mean retail "moved" the stock. In fact, many retail orders are routed to wholesale market makers, who fill them from their own inventory at or slightly better than the public quote, then lay off the risk in the broader market. Retail flow was, at most, about one out of every $1,000 that traded that day. The market makers were part of the other $999, absorbing, quoting and re-hedging at enormous speed.

How do investors and traders use this knowledge? First, as a lens for reading volume. When a headline says "retail bought the dip," compare it against total volume, as the presenter did. A big move on enormous volume means big institutions and liquidity providers were fighting over the price. Second, watch the spread. A stock whose bid-ask spread suddenly widens is telling you that market makers are nervous and charging more for the risk. Third, use limit orders instead of market orders in chaotic moments. At the Thursday open, when Nvidia hit $148.80 within a minute of opening, a market order could fill at a price far from what you saw on screen, because quotes were thin and moving. A limit order caps what you will pay.

Here are the gotchas. Market makers are not charities, and they are not your counterparty out of loyalty. They are not the same as the "specialist" of old or as the exchange itself. They also do not set the long-term price. Fundamentals and the big buy-and-sell decisions of institutions do that. Market makers set the very short-term price at which a trade happens, and they adjust it to avoid being run over. A common misconception is that market makers "manipulate" the stock against retail. In normal conditions they are competing with each other, which pushes spreads down. Another subtlety: a market maker earning money on the spread is different from a trader making a directional bet. Many firms do both, but the two activities have different goals and risks.

What happens when it breaks? Market makers are willing to quote only while they can price risk. When news is shocking or the market is moving violently, they widen spreads, shrink the size they will trade, or step back entirely. That is how you get flash crashes, where prices briefly plunge to absurd levels because the quotes simply were not there. The May 2010 flash crash and the August 2015 ETF dislocations are the classic examples, when some shares printed at pennies. It also shows up around earnings. Nvidia reports after the bell on the Tuesday, when trading is thin and spreads are wide, so the first reaction can overshoot before the next session's liquidity returns. Thin after-hours books mean small orders can move prices more than you expect.

Market makers connect to several other ideas in the guide. They are the plumbing under leveraged ETFs like the 2x Nvidia funds that drew $42.5 billion in three days, because those funds need to trade enormous amounts at the close to rebalance, and market makers and swap dealers take the other side. They connect to volume and the "small print" the presenter loves, since high volume with tight spreads signals a healthy market. They also link to order flow, the study of who is buying and selling, and to options, where market makers hedging their positions can amplify moves. Understand the spread, the inventory and the hedge, and you can explain most of what happens beneath the price on your screen.
