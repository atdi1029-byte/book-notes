FDIC Deposit Insurance

FDIC deposit insurance is a guarantee from the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, an independent agency of the US government created in 1933, that if your bank fails, you will get your insured deposits back. The standard limit is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. It covers checking accounts, savings accounts, money market deposit accounts, and certificates of deposit. It does not cover stocks, bonds, mutual funds, crypto, or annuities, even if you bought them through a bank. The coverage is automatic. You don't sign up or pay a premium. If you opened an account at an FDIC-member bank, you're covered up to the limit. The backing is the full faith and credit of the United States.

It matters because it is the reason ordinary people don't sprint to the bank at the first rumor of trouble. Before 1933, a bank failure meant depositors simply lost their money, so any hint of weakness could trigger a bank run. Thousands of banks collapsed in the early 1930s partly because of this. Deposit insurance removes the incentive to run: if your money is safe either way, you have no reason to be first in line. That stability lets banks do their core job of taking short-term deposits and making long-term loans. It also shapes how households and businesses decide where to park cash, and how much risk they think they're taking by leaving a balance in a checking account.

Mechanically, the FDIC is funded by premiums that banks pay, not by taxpayers, into the Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF). When a bank fails, the chartering regulator closes it, often on a Friday afternoon, and appoints the FDIC as receiver. The FDIC then usually takes one of two paths. In a purchase and assumption, another healthy bank takes over the deposits and some assets, and customers often just find their accounts at a new bank on Monday. Or the FDIC does a payout, cutting checks to insured depositors, typically within a few business days. Either way, insured money is generally accessible almost immediately. Anything above the limit becomes a claim against the failed bank's estate and gets paid back only as assets are sold off.

The phrase "per ownership category" is where the real strategy lives, and it's what the video's advice about "a single registration" refers to. The FDIC insures different account registrations separately at the same bank. A single account is covered to $250,000. A joint account is covered to $250,000 per co-owner, so a couple has $500,000 there. Retirement accounts like IRAs get their own $250,000. Certain revocable trusts are covered up to $250,000 per beneficiary, with a cap. So one couple at one bank could legitimately insure well over a million dollars by using different categories. But the plain rule for a simple saver is this: keep each registration at each bank at or under $250,000. The FDIC's free EDIE calculator online will check your specific setup.

The video makes the case for why this limit suddenly feels relevant. The host describes $325.1 billion in unrealized losses on investment securities across US commercial banks, per the FDIC's own Quarterly Banking Profile, a figure that rose $19 billion in a single quarter as the 10-year Treasury yield pushed toward 5.27%. His argument runs like this: regional banks hold long-dated bonds bought at 2% coupons, and accounting rules let them carry those bonds in a held-to-maturity bucket at full face value. If depositors leave for Treasury bills paying over 4%, the bank must borrow at 4% or more to replace cheap deposits, and its net interest margin can turn negative.

"Once it's in that bucket, a magical statutory veil drops over the asset."

This is essentially the 2023 Silicon Valley Bank story. SVB held a large book of long-duration securities that had lost value as rates rose. When uninsured depositors, mostly startups and companies with balances far above $250,000, began withdrawing, the bank had to sell securities and realize the losses. It failed in about two days. Notably, the depositors who were fully insured were never in real danger. The people at risk were those over the limit. That's why the video's playbook is aimed at the limit itself: the FDIC's guarantee is strong, but it only protects the first $250,000 in each category. The video's claim that regulators are "hiding" insolvency is the host's opinion, and you should treat it as a thesis to check, not a fact. Unrealized losses are real, but a bank fails when it can't meet withdrawals, not merely because it has paper losses.

Investors and treasurers use this concept in practical ways. Anyone with cash above $250,000 has three main choices: spread it across several banks, use a service that splits deposits among many banks (a deposit sweep network such as IntraFi), or move it into Treasury bills or government money market funds. T-bills are backed directly by the US government and have no deposit limit, which is why the video notes that sophisticated depositors sweep cash there and earn over 4%. Traders also watch bank stress signals: falling regional bank stock prices, rising uninsured deposit percentages in a bank's filings, heavy borrowing from the Federal Home Loan Bank, and deposit outflows. A bank with a high share of uninsured deposits is more vulnerable to a run, because those depositors have real money at risk and a reason to leave.

There are several common misconceptions. First, the limit is per bank, not per account. Five accounts at the same bank under the same registration still total $250,000 of coverage. Second, two brand names may be one bank: an online savings brand might just be a division of a bank you already use, so check the FDIC certificate number. Third, credit unions aren't covered by the FDIC. They're insured by the NCUA, with the same $250,000 limit. Fourth, money market funds at a brokerage are not the same as money market deposit accounts; the funds aren't FDIC-insured. Fifth, an account at a fintech app is only insured if the money is actually held at a partner bank, and the pass-through coverage can break if the app's records are a mess, as customers of the 2024 Synapse collapse discovered.

What happens when it fails? For a single bank, insurance has worked very well: no insured depositor has lost a penny of insured funds since the FDIC began. The risk is elsewhere. Uninsured balances can take losses. In 2023, regulators invoked a systemic risk exception to protect all SVB and Signature Bank depositors, but that was a political decision, not a promise, and you shouldn't build a plan on it. The other risk is scale. The DIF holds roughly 1% or so of insured deposits, so a wave of large failures would require borrowing from the Treasury, and Congress could authorize more. That is a remote scenario, but it explains why the limit is a floor for planning rather than a reason to stop paying attention.

FDIC insurance connects to several other ideas in this guide. Duration risk and mark-to-market accounting explain why the bank's assets lose value when rates rise. Held-to-maturity versus available-for-sale accounting explains why those losses are hidden from headline capital ratios. Net interest margin explains how a bank can bleed slowly even without a run. Money market funds and T-bills are the alternative places deposits flee to, and the stealth credit squeeze the video describes, with banks quietly denying HELOCs and tightening loan terms, is what happens to borrowers when banks feel this pressure. The takeaway is simple: insurance protects your deposit, not your bank's health. Know your limit, know your registrations, and spread the rest.
