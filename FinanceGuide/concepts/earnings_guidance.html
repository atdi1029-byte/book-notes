Earnings Guidance (Forward Guidance — Corporate)

When a public company reports its quarterly earnings, it does two things: it tells you what already happened, and it tells you what it expects to happen next. That second part is called earnings guidance, or more broadly, forward guidance. It's the company's own official forecast — typically for the next quarter or the full fiscal year — covering metrics like revenue, earnings per share, or gross margin. Management delivers it during earnings calls, in press releases, or in SEC filings. Think of it as the company's public promise about its near-term future. It is not a guarantee, but Wall Street treats it almost like one. The moment guidance lands, analysts, algorithms, and fund managers compare it to what they were already expecting, and that gap — even a tiny one — can move a stock dramatically.

Guidance matters because markets are fundamentally forward-looking machines. Stock prices don't just reflect what a company earned last quarter; they reflect the discounted present value of all future cash flows. When a company updates its forward outlook, it is directly editing the inputs Wall Street uses to value the business. A guidance raise signals confidence and often implies stronger future profits, pushing the stock higher. A guidance cut signals trouble ahead — shrinking demand, rising costs, or lost competitive advantage — and compresses the valuation instantly. This is why a company can report a record quarter and still get punished: if guidance disappointed, the market is pricing the future, not celebrating the past. Understanding this is one of the most important mental shifts a new investor has to make.

Mechanically, here's how the guidance cycle works. Before a company reports, every major Wall Street bank and independent research firm publishes its own forecast — a consensus estimate — built from financial models, industry checks, and management commentary. These estimates are aggregated on platforms like Bloomberg or FactSet. When the company reports, it compares its actual results against that consensus (the earnings beat or miss), and then it issues fresh guidance for the period ahead. Traders immediately compare management's new guidance number against what the consensus already projected for that future period. If guidance comes in above consensus, the stock typically rallies. If it comes in below — even by a fraction of a percent — the stock often drops hard, regardless of how strong recent results were. The delta between guidance and consensus is the market's real moment of truth.

The clearest illustration of this from recent market action involves Broadcom (AVGO). In its fiscal third quarter 2026 earnings report, Broadcom posted revenue of $29.6 billion — up 86% year-over-year. Its AI semiconductor revenue hit $16.7 billion, up 221% year-over-year. Adjusted EPS came in at $3.32. By any historical measure, these are extraordinary numbers. And yet the stock fell 6% the next day. Why? Because fourth-quarter revenue guidance of $34.8 billion landed just below the Wall Street consensus of $35.05 billion. And gross margin guidance came in at 73%, compared to 78% the prior year. As the video puts it bluntly:

"Growing 86% into a shrinking margin is a different investment case than growing 86% into an expanding one."
The past results were irrelevant. The guidance miss repriced the story entirely.

Lululemon provides an equally instructive example, with a different underlying cause. The company cut its full-year revenue guidance to $10.35–$10.5 billion, down from a prior forecast that implied roughly flat growth. It also reported that comparable sales dropped 9% overall and 12% in the Americas. On the surface, adjusted earnings of $2.92 per share crushed the $1.82 estimate — but $0.86 of that beat came from a one-time tariff refund. Strip it out, and actual operating earnings were roughly $2.06. The guidance cut exposed what the earnings "beat" had masked: the core business was losing customers. The stock fell 17% in a single session. This illustrates a critical nuance — a guidance cut tells you management themselves no longer believes the old forecast, and that confession is often more damaging than the miss itself.

Experienced traders and investors use guidance in several practical ways. First, they track guidance revision trends over time — a company that consistently raises guidance is demonstrating operational control and demand visibility, while one that repeatedly cuts is signaling structural problems. Second, they decompose guidance quality: is management guiding conservatively (setting a low bar they'll easily clear next quarter) or aggressively (stretching to look confident)? Third, they parse the specific line items — revenue guidance tells you about demand, gross margin guidance tells you about pricing power and cost structure, and operating margin guidance tells you about efficiency. When Broadcom guided gross margins lower by five percentage points year-over-year, sophisticated investors immediately recalculated long-term free cash flow projections, which is what actually drives valuation multiples in a high-rate environment.

The most common misconception about guidance is that beating earnings is what matters most. It doesn't — not if guidance disappoints. This is the "buy the rumor, sell the news" phenomenon in its most pure form. A company can report a blowout quarter, and if its forward guidance is even slightly below where the street expected, the stock gets punished. The inverse is also true: a company can miss current-quarter estimates but raise full-year guidance and the stock rallies. Another nuance is guidance quality: some companies give precise point estimates, others give wide ranges, and some provide no formal guidance at all. Wide ranges or vague language can be interpreted as management signaling uncertainty, which itself becomes a negative catalyst. Markets hate ambiguity more than they hate bad news.

Guidance fails most dangerously when the reason for the cut goes deeper than a one-quarter speed bump. When a company cuts guidance twice in the same fiscal year — as Lululemon did — it signals that management doesn't fully understand its own business trajectory. That destroys credibility, and credibility is the currency guidance trades on. A single miss can be excused as macro headwinds or timing. A second cut in eight months implies the model itself is broken. Similarly, guidance can mislead when companies engineer one-time items into reported earnings to obscure operational weakness, as Lululemon did with the tariff refund. Investors who anchor to headline EPS without reading footnotes get caught. The discipline is always to ask: what did the business actually earn, and what does management actually think the business will earn next? Everything else is noise.

Forward guidance connects directly to several other foundational market concepts. It is the primary input into analyst price target revisions, which then trigger institutional buy/sell decisions. It feeds into valuation multiples — when guidance implies accelerating growth, markets apply higher price-to-earnings ratios; when it implies deceleration, multiples compress even if growth remains positive. Guidance also interacts with the interest rate environment: in a rising-rate world like the one described in the video — where the Fed may be "starting over" rather than cutting — future earnings get discounted more heavily, making guidance misses even more punishing. This is precisely why Broadcom's 221% AI growth rate still got sold: the question the market started asking wasn't "how fast are you growing?" but rather, as the video captures it,

"how much do you keep?"
That shift — from growth to margin retention — is a guidance story at its core.
