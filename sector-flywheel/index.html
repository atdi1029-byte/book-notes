The Sector Flywheel

Eleven sectors, 74 new names, and a simple goal: whatever the market is punishing this month, there should be a name in that corner worth buying.

The Flywheel's buying rule is simple. Rank every name by how cheap it is and buy the eight cheapest. That rule has a blind spot. Stocks in the same sector get cheap together and get expensive together, so a pool that is heavy in one sector keeps handing you the same bet eight times over. And a pool that is thin in a sector can't buy that sector when it is the one on sale.

Sectors take turns because they answer to different forces. Oil prices drive energy. Interest rates drive utilities and real estate. Shoppers drive retail. Drug-pricing politics drive health care. When one of those forces turns hostile, its sector goes on sale while the others carry on. It is rare for every force to turn at once, so a pool with real depth in all eleven sectors almost always has something juicy in it.

This volume is built for that. It maps what the Flywheel holds today, sector by sector, then adds 74 names that none of the other volumes cover (the Asset Atlas, the Flywheel Ledger, SPX Flywheel and the Bond Flywheel). They are mostly S&P 400 mid-caps, a few new S&P 500 spin-offs, and large foreign companies that trade in New York. Every one passed the Ledger's test: an established business with real earnings, cash returned to shareholders, and no existential question mark. Would you hold it for a year if the exit never came?

The sectors run from the thinnest part of the Flywheel to the fattest, so the names that fix the biggest gaps come first. Each entry keeps the Ledger format: what the business does, when it shines, what can hurt it, what to watch, and how to use it on a two-month hold. The meta line adds one number, the two-month swing. It is one standard deviation of every rolling two-month return over the last three years, so about two-thirds of two-month periods landed inside that band. A name with a ±5% swing rarely makes a flywheel-sized move on its own. That is fine for an anchor but slow for a trade. At ±12% and up, 10–15% moves come routinely. Why Sectors Take Turns

The textbook picture is the sector rotation clock. The economy moves through four phases, and each phase favors a different group of sectors. It is a tendency, not a law. Phases run for different lengths, and the market usually moves six to nine months before the economy does. But it explains why a sector can be the worst performer for a year and then lead the next one.

The sector rotation clock. Markets tend to move through it six to nine months ahead of the economy.

Early recovery starts in the depths. The Fed has cut, credit spreads are tightening, and earnings are at the bottom. Banks lead because a steepening yield curve widens their lending margin and loan losses stop rising. Consumer discretionary leads because pent-up demand and lower mortgage rates bring shoppers and home buyers back. Industrials and real estate follow. This is when the Flywheel's cyclical names are cheapest and the defensive ones are expensive.

Mid-cycle is the long middle. Growth is steady, inflation is tame, and the market rewards companies that can grow faster than the economy: technology and communication services, with industrials carried along by capital spending. Sector leadership is less extreme here, and most selloffs are about a single company or industry rather than the whole economy.

Late cycle brings inflation, a tight job market and a Fed that is raising rates. Commodity prices tend to peak late, so energy and materials lead. Investors start edging into health care and staples, whose earnings don't depend on the economy. Rate-sensitive sectors (utilities and real estate) get cheaper as yields rise, which is often the best entry they offer in a cycle.

Recession is when staples, utilities and health care hold up while everything cyclical falls, often 30–50% in the most sensitive names. That wreckage is the setup for the next early-recovery trade. The Flywheel rule of buying the cheapest works best right here, as long as the pool has cyclical names worth owning through the bottom. That is why the entries lean toward companies with balance sheets that survive a recession.

When that cycle wobbles, a cheapest-eight ranking can fill most of its slots from this one cluster. So the priority order for adding names is energy, utilities, materials, communication services, real estate and health care first, then depth in industrials and financials, and only then more consumer or technology names. Energy

On the Flywheel now: XOM, CVX. Two U.S. oil majors that trade as a pair, so the pool has one shot at an energy selloff. The names below split energy into the pieces that actually move differently: oil, natural gas, pipelines and offshore services.

Energy is the most cyclical sector in the market and the one most driven by a single number. When crude falls on an OPEC supply decision or a demand scare, producers fall harder than the oil price because their costs are fixed and their revenue isn't. That operating leverage is why energy names hit the cheapest-eight list in clusters. It also works in reverse, which is why an oversold producer can make a flywheel move in weeks. The trick is owning pieces that don't all answer to crude. Natural gas has its own market, driven by weather, LNG exports and power demand. Pipelines earn tolls on volume and trade more like utilities. Offshore services run on multi-year contracts that barely notice a three-month dip in oil.

As of late September 2026, energy is the opposite of on sale. The Iran war and the disruption in the Strait of Hormuz have Brent above $100 and most of the oil names below near their highs, with natural gas the exception. This section is for the next selloff, which a lasting reopening of Hormuz could bring quickly.

TTE TotalEnergies

Sector: Integrated Oil & Gas · Market cap: ~$203B · P/E: ~11x · Yield: ~4.6% · 2-month swing: ±9%

The French supermajor and the most diversified of the big oil companies. Oil and gas production of roughly 2.5 million barrels of oil equivalent a day is still the profit engine, but TotalEnergies also runs one of the three largest LNG portfolios in the world, a refining and chemicals business, and Integrated Power, a fast-growing arm that builds and trades renewable and gas-fired electricity. The pitch is that it gets paid on the way from oil to LNG to power, whichever way the energy transition actually goes.

It is also the major that never cut. When Shell and BP slashed their dividends in 2020, TotalEnergies held its payout, and it has returned a large share of cash flow through buybacks ever since. Since December 2025 its ordinary shares have traded directly on the NYSE under TTE, replacing the old ADRs. In 2026 it restarted its Mozambique LNG project (first cargoes are expected in 2029) and bought half of EPH's European gas-fired power plants. The Iran war that began in February 2026 cut output from its Qatar LNG interests but sent oil prices up. Second-quarter cash flow was $9.8 billion with Brent averaging about $104, the 2026 dividend was raised 5.9%, and buybacks run about $1.5 billion a quarter.

Shines when: Oil and European gas prices rise, LNG projects start up on schedule, and the market rewards cash returns over growth. It also benefits when U.S. majors look expensive and money goes looking for the same business at a lower multiple.

Dangers: Oil price declines, including a sharp one if the Strait of Hormuz fully reopens. Its Qatar LNG assets sit in the war zone. European windfall taxes and politics. Project risk in Mozambique LNG and other frontier regions. The power business earns lower returns than oil and gas and investors may not reward it. French dividend withholding tax.

Watch: Reports late Apr/Jul/Oct and early Feb. Brent, European gas prices, LNG sales volumes, the quarterly buyback amount, and refining margins. Shell and BP report the same week and set the tone.

How to use it: The value way to own a supermajor. It usually trades at a lower multiple than Exxon and Chevron, so a selloff in oil tends to leave it cheapest of the three. Earnings reactions run ±2–5%; the two-month moves come from Brent. Buy after an oil drop that pulls it to the bottom of its range. In September 2026 it sits near its 52-week high on war-driven oil, so this is one to wait on. If stuck, you collect a dividend in the mid-4%s from a company that didn't cut it in 2020.

CNQ Canadian Natural Resources

Sector: Oil Sands & E&P · Market cap: ~$99B · P/E: ~12x · Yield: ~3.8% · 2-month swing: ±11%

Canada's largest oil and gas producer, at roughly 1.6 to 1.7 million barrels of oil equivalent a day. What makes it different is the oil sands. Most of its oil comes from mining and upgrading bitumen at Horizon and the Athabasca Oil Sands Project, assets that produce for decades with almost no decline. A shale well loses most of its output in two years and has to be replaced. An oil sands mine keeps going, so Canadian Natural needs far less capital just to stand still, and more of its cash flow is free.

That free cash flow goes to shareholders under a formula tied to net debt. The dividend has been raised for 26 consecutive years, through the 2015 and 2020 oil crashes. The March 2026 raise (6.4%) was the 26th in a row. In 2026 it also bought Tourmaline's Peace River gas assets and produced a record 1.68 million barrels of oil equivalent a day in the second quarter, raising its production guidance twice.

Shines when: Oil rises, the discount on Canadian heavy crude (WCS) narrows, and operations run smoothly. The Trans Mountain pipeline expansion that opened in 2024 gave Canadian crude a route to the Pacific and narrowed that discount.

Dangers: Oil price declines hit it hard because the operation is levered to crude. Canadian climate policy (emissions caps, carbon pricing). Pipeline bottlenecks if Canadian output outgrows export capacity. Unplanned outages at the mines. Canadian dividend withholding tax.

Watch: Reports early Mar/May/Aug/Nov. WTI and the WCS differential, production versus guidance, net debt against its targets, and the dividend increase (usually announced with Q4 results).

How to use it: The long-life oil producer. It swings more than the majors (±11% over two months) but has a floor the shale names don't: low sustaining capital means it still generates cash at oil prices that squeeze others. Buy it when crude sells off. If stuck, you own a 26-year dividend grower with decades of reserves. As of September 2026 it is up about 60% from its 52-week low on war-driven oil, so this is a name to wait on, not chase.

PR Permian Resources

Sector: Shale E&P (Permian) · Market cap: ~$18B · P/E: ~14x · Yield: ~2.9% · 2-month swing: ±13%

A pure-play driller in the Delaware Basin, the western half of the Permian in Texas and New Mexico. It was formed in 2022 from the merger of Centennial Resource Development and Colgate Energy, then grew by buying Earthstone Energy in 2023, and it is now one of the largest independent producers in the basin. The co-CEOs came from the private equity-backed Colgate side and run it like owners, with a reputation as one of the lowest-cost operators in shale.

Returns come through a base dividend plus buybacks, and management has a habit of buying acreage cheaply from bigger companies that are trimming their portfolios. In 2026 it raised its base dividend 7%, spent about $1 billion on bolt-on acreage, simplified its share structure, and reported record free cash flow of $751 million in the second quarter.

Shines when: Oil rises, well costs fall, and bolt-on deals add inventory at good prices. Shale producers with long drilling inventories also get re-rated when the market starts worrying about how much Permian rock is left.

Dangers: Oil price declines, with more leverage to crude than the majors. Shale wells decline fast, so it has to keep drilling to hold production. Permian natural gas takeaway limits, which have pushed local gas prices below zero at times. An expensive acquisition would break the low-cost story.

Watch: Reports early May/Aug/Nov and late Feb. WTI, production per share, capital spending per barrel, and well costs. Diamondback reports around the same time and sets expectations for the basin.

How to use it: The high-beta oil trade in the sleeve. A ±13% two-month swing means an oil rebound can deliver the whole 15% target. As of September 2026 it is up about 80% from its 52-week low, so the entry is the next oil selloff, not today. Buy after an oil selloff pulls it to the bottom of its range, and sell into the rebound rather than waiting for more. If stuck, the dividend is modest and the stock tracks crude, so being stuck is really a bet on oil.

RRC Range Resources

Sector: Natural Gas E&P · Market cap: ~$9.1B · P/E: ~11x · Yield: ~1.0% · 2-month swing: ±12%

The company that drilled the first commercial Marcellus shale well in 2004 and turned Appalachia into the largest natural gas field in the country. Range produces over two billion cubic feet equivalent a day from southwest Pennsylvania, with a large share of natural gas liquids (propane, butane and ethane) that it sells into export markets. Its inventory is one of the longest in the industry, measured in decades of drilling locations.

Natural gas is a different market from oil. It is driven by U.S. weather, storage levels, LNG export demand on the Gulf Coast, and power plants, which now include the ones feeding data centers. That is exactly why it belongs in this section: it can be cheap when oil isn't, and the reverse. In 2026 gas has stayed weak, around $3, even with oil above $100, and that split is the point. In February Range raised its dividend about 11% and its buyback authorization to $1.5 billion, and it signed a 10-year deal to supply gas to a Midwest power plant starting in late 2027.

Shines when: Cold winters, LNG export plants ramping up, rising power demand, and low storage. Strong NGL prices add a second tailwind.

Dangers: Warm winters and full storage can crush gas prices for a year at a time. Appalachian pipeline constraints mean Range often sells gas at a discount to the Henry Hub benchmark. NGL prices depend partly on exports to China.

Watch: Reports late Apr/Jul/Oct and Feb. Henry Hub prices, the weekly EIA storage report (Thursdays), LNG feed-gas volumes, and weather forecasts. EQT reports first and sets the tone for Appalachia.

How to use it: The natural gas slot. Gas is seasonal, so the classic setup is buying in late spring or summer when storage is full and gas is cheap, and selling into the winter heating season. The ±12% two-month swing is mostly gas prices. If stuck, the dividend is small, so being stuck is a bet on the next winter.

ENB Enbridge

Sector: Pipelines & Gas Utility · Market cap: ~$106B · P/E: ~26x · Yield: ~5.8% · 2-month swing: ±6%

North America's largest pipeline company. The Mainline system carries around three million barrels of Canadian crude a day into the U.S. Midwest, a large share of all Canadian oil exports. On the gas side, Enbridge owns major interstate pipelines such as Texas Eastern, and after buying three U.S. gas utilities from Dominion in 2024 it is also the largest natural gas utility in North America by volume. A smaller renewables arm rounds it out.

The business is built like a toll road. Almost all of its earnings come from regulated rates or long-term contracts, so commodity prices barely matter. What matters is interest rates, because Enbridge carries a lot of debt and its 6%-ish dividend competes with bond yields. The dividend has been raised for 31 consecutive years. 2026 has been busy. In July, Michigan's Supreme Court voided the permit for the Line 5 tunnel under the Straits of Mackinac, delaying the project. In September Enbridge agreed to buy Tallgrass's crude pipelines (including the Pony Express line) for $2.55 billion, paid for partly with about $3 billion of new shares. CEO Greg Ebel retires at the end of 2026 and hands over to Michele Harradence.

Shines when: Interest rates fall, Canadian oil production grows and fills the Mainline, and new gas pipeline projects for LNG export and power plants get approved.

Dangers: Rising rates. High debt (around five times earnings before interest, tax and depreciation). Line 5 legal fights in Michigan. Slow dividend growth (about 3% a year). Canadian withholding tax on the dividend.

Watch: Reports mid-Feb, early May, late Jul/early Aug, early Nov. The 10-year Treasury yield, Mainline volumes, distributable cash flow per share, and debt-to-EBITDA.

How to use it: An energy name that trades on rates, not oil. That makes it a diversifier inside the sector, but it is a slow mover (±6% over two months). Buy it when rates spike and pull it down with utilities, and treat a 6–8% bounce as a good result. In September 2026 that is roughly where it sits, near its 52-week low with the 10-year yield above 5%. If stuck, you collect one of the highest safe yields in the sleeve.

DTM DT Midstream

Sector: Natural Gas Pipelines · Market cap: ~$13B · P/E: ~28x · Yield: ~2.8% · 2-month swing: ±9%

A natural gas pipeline company spun off from DTE Energy in 2021. It gathers gas in the Haynesville (Louisiana) and Appalachia, and owns interstate pipelines including NEXUS and Vector, which carry Appalachian gas into Michigan, Ohio and Ontario. At the end of 2024 it bought three regulated pipelines (Guardian, Midwestern and Viking) from ONEOK for about $1.2 billion. Most revenue comes from demand charges, which customers pay whether they ship gas or not.

Two long trends run through its system. The Haynesville feeds the Gulf Coast LNG export plants, and the Midwest pipelines serve utilities that need more gas for power plants, including data-center load. In 2026 it approved about $300 million of expansions, raised its dividend 7%, and added a new NEXUS connection to a gas plant serving an Ohio data center. The stock has pulled back about 18% from its peak without a company-specific reason, which analysts put down to valuation.

Shines when: LNG exports grow, Midwest utilities sign new capacity contracts, rates fall, and the market pays up for gas-for-power stories.

Dangers: Haynesville drilling slows when gas prices are low. Customer concentration in a few producers and utilities. Rising rates. Pipeline projects can be delayed by permits and lawsuits.

Watch: Reports mid-Feb, early May, late Jul/early Aug, late Oct. Adjusted EBITDA versus guidance, contracted capacity, new project announcements, and Haynesville rig counts.

How to use it: The gas-growth pipeline. It moves more than Enbridge (±9% over two months) because investors treat it as an LNG and power demand story, not just a toll road. Buy on a rate spike or a gas price scare that drags it down with the producers. If stuck, the contracted cash flow and a growing dividend make it an easy hold.

FTI TechnipFMC

Sector: Offshore Oil Services · Market cap: ~$28B · P/E: ~25x · Yield: ~0.3% · 2-month swing: ±12%

The leader in subsea oil and gas equipment: the trees, manifolds, flexible pipe and control systems that sit on the seabed and connect offshore wells to production vessels. TechnipFMC's edge is doing the whole job as one contract (it calls this iEPCI) and building standardized, pre-designed equipment (Subsea 2.0) instead of engineering every project from scratch. That cuts delivery times and lets it earn better margins than the old custom model. A smaller Surface Technologies unit sells onshore wellheads and fracking equipment.

Its customers are the deepwater giants: Petrobras in Brazil, Exxon in Guyana, Equinor, TotalEnergies. Deepwater projects are planned years ahead and cost less per barrel than most shale, so orders keep coming through normal oil price swings. In 2026 it targeted about $10 billion of subsea orders and raised its full-year EBITDA guidance to about $2.2 billion. The stock still fell after the second quarter when orders and backlog slipped slightly, a reminder of how order-driven it is. It has roughly doubled over the past year.

Shines when: Offshore project approvals keep coming, subsea orders run above $1 billion a quarter, and margins expand as older low-priced contracts roll off.

Dangers: A deep, lasting oil downturn that makes customers delay projects. Execution problems on fixed-price contracts. Customer concentration in a handful of majors and national oil companies.

Watch: Reports late Apr/Jul/Oct and late Feb. Subsea inbound orders, backlog, subsea margins, and buybacks. Offshore project approval (FID) announcements from Petrobras and the majors.

How to use it: The long-cycle energy name. It still sells off with oil, but its backlog means the business usually doesn't deserve it, which makes those selloffs good entries. Earnings reactions run ±5–10% on orders. If stuck, the dividend is tiny; the carry is the backlog growing underneath you.

Utilities

On the Flywheel now: DUK, SO. Two large Southeast electric utilities with the same regulator style, the same interest-rate sensitivity and the same data-center story, so they trade as a pair. The names below add different regulators, different countries, gas and water.

Utilities are the most bond-like sector in the market. Their earnings are set by regulators, who allow a return on the money the utility invests in its grid, so profits grow slowly and predictably and most of the cash goes out as dividends. That makes the stocks trade against Treasury yields. When the 10-year yield jumps, utilities fall, even though nothing about the business has changed. That is the flywheel setup in its purest form: a selloff driven by something outside the company, which reverses when the outside force fades. The catch is that utilities move slowly, with two-month swings of ±5–8%, so the realistic target is the low end of the 5–15% range. Spreading across regulators matters because the one thing that does hurt a utility's business is a bad rate case or a wildfire liability, and those hit one state at a time.

NGG National Grid

Sector: Regulated Networks (U.K./U.S.) · Market cap: ~$76B · P/E: ~17x · Yield: ~4.3% · 2-month swing: ±8%

The owner of the high-voltage electricity transmission network in England and Wales, the regional power distribution networks in the Midlands, the South West and Wales, and regulated electric and gas utilities in New York and Massachusetts. Roughly half the business is in each country. After selling its U.K. gas transmission business and other side assets, it is now almost entirely a wires company.

National Grid is in the middle of the largest investment program in its history, rebuilding the British grid to connect offshore wind and new demand. In May 2026 it laid out a plan to invest £70 billion or more through 2031, targeting about 10% annual growth in its asset base and 8–10% annual growth in earnings per share. The cost of that plan (a £7 billion rights issue in 2024, net debt above £44 billion, and a scrip dividend that adds shares) is why the stock trades near its lows. A new CEO, Zoë Yujnovich, took over in late 2025.

Shines when: U.K. and U.S. bond yields fall, Ofgem and New York regulators grant good returns, the pound strengthens against the dollar, and the investment plan runs on budget.

Dangers: Rising yields in either country. Regulatory cuts, such as the reduced returns U.S. regulators set for its New England transmission in March 2026. Storm costs in the Northeast. More share issuance if the investment plan grows again.

Watch: Reports mid-May (full year) and early Nov (half year). U.K. gilt and U.S. Treasury yields, Ofgem decisions, New York rate cases, and the pound. One ADR equals five ordinary shares; U.K. dividends carry no withholding tax.

How to use it: A utility with two regulators and two bond markets behind it, so it doesn't move in lockstep with Duke and Southern. It swings more than most utilities (±8%) because the pound adds a second lever. Buy when gilt yields spike. If stuck, the dividend rises with U.K. inflation.

FTS Fortis

Sector: Regulated Utility (Canada/U.S.) · Market cap: ~$27B · P/E: ~22x · Yield: ~3.5% · 2-month swing: ±5%

A Newfoundland-based holding company for ten regulated utilities in Canada, the U.S. and the Caribbean. The biggest pieces are American: ITC, the largest independent electricity transmission company in the U.S.; Tucson Electric Power in Arizona; and Central Hudson in New York. FortisBC and FortisAlberta are the main Canadian pieces. Almost all of the business is regulated, and more than half is in the U.S.

Fortis has raised its dividend for 52 consecutive years, one of the longest streaks in Canada, and guides to 4–6% annual increases through 2030 backed by about 7% annual growth in its asset base. Possible upside comes from transmission projects in the Midwest (through ITC) and a data-center supply deal in Tucson.

Shines when: Bond yields fall, transmission spending in the U.S. Midwest accelerates, and Arizona load growth turns into new investment.

Dangers: Rising yields. Arizona regulation, which has been tough on returns in the past. The Canadian dollar. Slow growth is the whole design, so upside surprises are rare.

Watch: Reports Feb, early May, late Jul/early Aug, early Nov. The dividend raise usually comes with Q3 results in the fall. Capital plan updates, rate case outcomes and the 10-year yield.

How to use it: The steadiest name in the whole guide (±5% over two months). It is an anchor, not a trade: a rate spike can pull it to a level where the dividend is unusually high, and a 5–7% recovery is a good outcome. If stuck, you own a 52-year dividend grower with a growth plan through 2030.

OGE OGE Energy

Health Care

On the Flywheel now: BRK-B, JPM, BAC, GS, MS, V, MA, CPAY. Eight names, but all American, and all tied to the same U.S. rate, credit and deal cycle. Financials make money from the price of money, and the different businesses in the sector want different things from it. Banks want a steep yield curve (borrow short, lend long) and low loan losses. Japan's banks are enjoying the first real rate increases in decades, India's are riding a growth economy, and Canada's depend on a heavily indebted housing market. That spread is what makes a financial selloff in one place a buying opportunity instead of a trap everywhere. The warning sign for the whole sector is a credit event, such as a bank failure or a private-credit blowup, which hits everything at once.

How to use it: A staples name that behaves like health care: steady, brand-driven, and not tied to food or drink. It swings about ±8%. Buy after a weak flu season quarter, since flu seasons vary but the brands don't. If stuck, you own global brands with pricing power.

BUD Anheuser-Busch InBev

Sector: Beer · Market cap: ~$152B · P/E: ~17x · Yield: ~1.8% · 2-month swing: ±11%

The world's largest brewer, selling about a quarter of the world's beer: Michelob Ultra, Corona, Stella Artois, Budweiser and hundreds of local brands. Its biggest profit pools are in Latin America (Mexico, Brazil, Colombia) and the U.S. In many emerging markets it controls the distribution network, which gives it pricing power local rivals can't match.

After years of paying down debt from its 2016 SABMiller takeover, AB InBev is back to returning cash: a $6 billion buyback, a 15% dividend increase and a new interim dividend. Beer volumes returned to growth in the second quarter of 2026, earnings per share grew 23%, and in September it laid out a plan targeting 5–9% annual profit growth.

Shines when: Volumes grow, emerging-market currencies strengthen, and premium brands like Michelob Ultra and Corona take share.

Dangers: Long-term declines in beer drinking (moderation, weight-loss drugs, younger drinkers). Emerging-market currencies. Still meaningful debt.

Watch: Reports mid-Feb, early May, late Jul, late Oct. Volumes, revenue per hectolitre, EBITDA growth and the Brazilian real.

CCEP Coca-Cola Europacific Partners

Shines when: Volumes grow, pricing holds, and the euro strengthens against the dollar.

FMX FEMSA

A Mexican conglomerate whose crown jewel is OXXO, the convenience-store chain on almost every corner in Mexico, with more than 20,000 stores. Shines when: The Mexican consumer is strong, the peso strengthens, and OXXO same-store sales and Spin growth accelerate.

USFD US Foods

The second-largest foodservice distributor in the U.S. after Sysco, delivering food and supplies to about 250,000 restaurants, hospitals, schools and hotels. Merger talks with Performance Food Group, pushed by an activist investor, ended in November 2025 over antitrust concerns. INGR Ingredion

On the Flywheel now: NVDA, MSFT, AAPL, AVGO, TSM, INTC, DELL, IBM, HPQ, GLW, APH, KEYS, JBL, ARW, MCHP, NXPI, SNPS, SMH. Eighteen names, most of them riding the chip and data-center cycle. The last thing the pool needs is more of that. The six names below were picked because they answer to something else: software budgets, insurers and engineers, factory automation, Apple's product cycle and licensing fees.

Technology is the market's largest sector and its biggest source of both gains and drawdowns. In 2025 and 2026 it split in two. Semiconductors and data-center hardware soared on AI spending, while many software companies fell 30–55% on fears that AI agents would replace their products or undercut their pricing. That split is useful. The Flywheel's chip names get cheap when AI spending wobbles. The software names below get cheap when the "AI eats software" story flares up, which often happens when the chip names are doing well. Owning both sides means a tech selloff of either kind has something to buy. Every software name here is profitable, sells to customers who can't easily switch, and kept growing through its stock's decline.

Also new in tech, but the same bet. Three names none of the other volumes cover are worth knowing, though they add to the AI-hardware cluster rather than balancing it: P (Everpure, the former Pure Storage, renamed in February 2026 and trading as P since April, now in the S&P 500 and selling flash storage to hyperscalers), Q (Qnity Electronics, DuPont's chip-materials business, spun off in November 2025) and SNX (TD SYNNEX, the largest IT distributor, whose Hyve unit builds AI servers). MANH Manhattan Associates

Sector: Supply-Chain Software · Market cap: ~$12B · Forward P/E: ~34x · Yield: none · 2-month swing: ±18%

The leading maker of warehouse-management and supply-chain software. When a retailer, grocer or third-party logistics company runs a large distribution center, there is a good chance Manhattan's software tells every worker and robot what to pick, pack and ship. It also sells transportation and store-inventory software. Ripping out a warehouse system is risky and expensive, so customers stay for decades, and the business is moving them to cloud subscriptions.

The stock fell about 55% from its high by February 2026 on fears that AI would disrupt software, and the company cut 6% of its workforce in June. Then second-quarter results showed cloud revenue up 26% and contracted future revenue up 23%, guidance was raised, and the stock jumped 27% in a day. A new CFO took over in March and a $500 million buyback is authorized.

Shines when: Cloud bookings grow, retailers and logistics firms invest in automation, and the AI-disruption fear fades.

Dangers: The AI-agent disruption narrative. Services revenue (almost half of sales) slowing. Retail and logistics spending cycles. A still-high valuation.

Watch: Reports late Jan/Apr/Jul/Oct. Cloud revenue growth, remaining performance obligations (contracted future revenue), services revenue and guidance.

How to use it: A mission-critical software name that swings hard (±18%). The playbook is exactly what 2026 showed: buy when the AI fear peaks and the results don't confirm it. If stuck, the business keeps compounding, but there is no dividend.

GWRE Guidewire Software

Sector: Insurance Software · Market cap: ~$12B · Forward P/E: ~28x · Yield: none · 2-month swing: ±17%

The core software for property and casualty insurers: the systems that write policies, handle billing and process claims. Most large insurers in North America, and many abroad, run on Guidewire or are moving to it. Replacing an insurer's core system is a multi-year project, so once a customer is on Guidewire it effectively never leaves. The company is moving its customers from licensed software to its cloud platform, which raises revenue per customer over time.

Results have kept beating expectations (revenue grew about 15% in its latest quarter), but the stock fell about 26% after that report and is down more than 40% over the past year, as investors worry that AI will erode what software companies can charge. Guidewire is responding by selling AI features on top of its core platform.

Shines when: Insurers accelerate cloud migrations, annual recurring revenue growth holds up, and margins expand as the cloud transition matures.

Dangers: AI pressure on software pricing. Long, lumpy sales cycles. Implementation problems at big customers. A premium valuation even after the fall.

Watch: Fiscal year ends July; reports early Mar, early Jun, early Sep, early Dec. Annual recurring revenue, cloud migrations and free cash flow.

How to use it: The insurance industry's operating system, at a much lower price than a year ago. It swings a lot (±17%). Buy after post-earnings drops that the numbers don't justify. If stuck, the customer lock-in is about as strong as software gets.

BSY Bentley Systems

Sector: Infrastructure Engineering Software · Market cap: ~$9.6B · P/E: ~35x trailing, ~20x forward · Yield: ~0.9% · 2-month swing: ±11%

The software that engineers use to design and maintain roads, bridges, rail, water systems, power grids, mines and plants. Bentley is to infrastructure what Autodesk is to buildings, and its tools are standard at large engineering firms and public agencies. More than 90% of revenue is subscriptions, and the Bentley family still owns a large share of the company.

The business has been steady: second-quarter 2026 revenue grew about 13%, recurring revenue growth accelerated to 12%, and margins stayed near 28%. The stock is still down about 45% from its high on the same AI-and-software worries as the rest of the group. Bentley plans to start charging for AI features in 2027 and bought back $155 million of stock in the first half.

Shines when: Infrastructure, utility-grid and mining spending grows, and the market starts pricing Bentley as an AI beneficiary rather than a victim.

Dangers: The AI-disruption narrative. Government budget cycles. China exposure. Family control.

Watch: Reports late Feb, early May, early Aug, early Nov. Annual recurring revenue growth, net revenue retention and free cash flow.

How to use it: A steady infrastructure compounder priced like a broken software stock. It swings about ±11%. If stuck, you own the standard tools for an infrastructure build-out that will run for decades.

CGNX Cognex

Sector: Machine Vision · Market cap: ~$9.7B · P/E: ~56x trailing, ~29x forward · Yield: ~0.6% · 2-month swing: ±17%

The leader in machine vision: cameras, sensors and software that let factory and warehouse machines see. Cognex systems inspect parts on auto and electronics assembly lines, read barcodes on packages flying through distribution centers, and guide robots. Its biggest customers are in logistics (think e-commerce warehouses), automotive, consumer electronics and packaging.

In September 2026 Cognex agreed to buy RealSense, the depth-camera maker spun out of Intel in 2025, for $500 million in cash, to push into vision for mobile and humanoid robots. It has a net cash balance sheet, about $755 million at midyear, and CEO Matt Moschner is steering it toward robotics and AI-based vision.

Shines when: Factory and warehouse automation spending picks up, consumer electronics makers retool for new products, and robotics adoption accelerates.

Dangers: A cyclical, lumpy business. Large customers can delay projects. Chinese competition in lower-end vision systems. A high multiple (about 29 times forward earnings).

Watch: Reports early Feb, late Apr, late Jul, late Oct. Logistics and automotive revenue, gross margin and next-quarter guidance.

How to use it: An automation and robotics play that moves with factory capital spending, not with data centers. It swings hard (±17%). Buy on industrial-slowdown scares. If stuck, the balance sheet is clean.

CRUS Cirrus Logic

Sector: Semiconductors · Market cap: ~$6.0B · P/E: ~15x · Yield: none · 2-month swing: ±15%

A chip company that is, in practice, an Apple supplier: about 90% of revenue comes from Apple. Cirrus makes the audio chips (the amplifiers and codecs behind iPhone speakers and microphones) and a growing set of mixed-signal chips for haptics, camera control, power and the sensors behind Face ID. The strategy is to win more content per iPhone, and in 2026 it added new camera and power chips.

In March 2026 Cirrus joined Apple's American Manufacturing Program and the stock rose about 60% by May. It has since given much of that back: guidance for the current quarter was below last year, and a shortage of memory and components hurt the PC outlook. It sits about a third below its high.

Dangers: Extreme dependence on one customer. Apple designing chips in-house. Weak iPhone cycles. No dividend.

How to use it: A cheap (about 15 times earnings) Apple-cycle trade that doesn't depend on AI spending. It swings about ±15%, and its best moves come around the iPhone launch each autumn. If stuck, the balance sheet is strong and the buyback steady.

DLB Dolby Laboratories

Shines when: Device shipments recover, Atmos and Vision spread into cars and live sports broadcasting, and new licensing deals land.

Dangers: A consumer electronics slump. Competition from free codecs. A new CEO. Family control.

Watch: Fiscal year ends September; reports late Jan/early Feb, late Apr/early May, late Jul/early Aug, mid-Nov. Licensing revenue, new-market growth (auto, broadcast) and guidance.

How to use it: A toll on devices, not on data centers, with a dividend. It is a slow mover (±8%). Buy on weak device-cycle quarters. If stuck, you own a high-margin licensing business with a 2.5% dividend and buybacks.
