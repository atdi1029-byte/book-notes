The Bond Flywheel

Bonds that actually swing. Same test as the Ledger: would you hold it for a year if the exit never came?

Most bonds can't make a flywheel move. A two-year Treasury or a short-term fund will never do 5–15% in two months, so this guide leaves out nearly the whole bond market and keeps only the funds that can: long-duration Treasuries, zero-coupon STRIPS, long TIPS, long corporates, and unhedged foreign and emerging-market government bonds, where currency moves pile on top of rate moves. Everything here pays a yield while you wait and has no earnings date, no CEO, and no chance of going to zero. That is why bonds pass the "stuck for a year" test. The risk is different: a slow, grinding bear market when inflation or government deficits push long rates up for years.

The math is short. A bond fund's price moves about duration × the change in yield, in the opposite direction, and on top of that you earn the yield (the carry) while you hold it. TLT has a duration of about 16, so a 0.5% (50 basis point) fall in long yields is roughly +8% before the two months of interest. To judge how big a move is normal, use the MOVE index: a typical (one standard deviation) two-month move in long yields is about 0.4 × MOVE basis points. With MOVE around 80, that's about 32bp, or ±5% on TLT. So 5% is routine, 10% needs a real shift in the macro story, and 15% needs a regime change. The 10Y and MOVE inputs on the DSS Index Health dashboard are the natural signal for this sleeve.

The last column is the lesson people miss: the carry cushion is how far yields can rise over two months before the interest is wiped out, and on long bonds it's only a few basis points. The yield keeps you comfortable over a year of being stuck, but it gives a two-month trade almost no protection. You are trading the direction of rates, not collecting income. Between trades, the cash sits in T-bill funds (SGOV, BIL) earning the Fed funds rate. Those are for parking and don't appear below. Yields and asset sizes here are approximate as of mid-2026 and drift constantly; durations are stable.

U.S. Long Treasuries

The core of the sleeve. No credit risk, the deepest market in the world, and prices that are pure rate expectations. They rally hardest when growth scares hit and inflation is contained, and they fall hardest when inflation or deficits push the long end up.

Nominal & realTLTTLHEDVZROZLTPZ

TLT iShares 20+ Year Treasury Bond ETF

Category: Long Treasuries · AUM: ~$50B · Duration: ~16 · Yield: ~4.5–5% · Expense ratio: 0.15%

The benchmark long-bond trade and one of the most traded ETFs in the world: a basket of U.S. Treasuries maturing in 20 to 30 years, paying monthly, with an options market deep enough that institutions use it to express views on rates. Launched in 2002, it spent most of its first eighteen years as the thing that went up when stocks went down. It rose about a third in 2008, roughly 10% in August 2019 alone during the trade-war scare, and around 20% in the first weeks of the 2020 crash.

Then came the worst bond bear market in modern history. From its March 2020 peak near $180, TLT fell roughly half by October 2023 as inflation hit 9% and the Fed raised rates from zero to over 5%. In 2022 alone it lost about 31%, and stocks fell alongside it, which broke the "bonds hedge stocks" rule for a generation of investors. That same bottom produced the textbook flywheel trade. In October 2023 the 30-year yield touched 5%. On November 1 the Treasury slowed the growth of its long-bond auctions, two days later a soft jobs report followed, and TLT ran from about $82 to about $100 by late December, over 20% in two months.

Shines when: Growth scares hit with inflation contained (weak payrolls, rising jobless claims, ISM below 50), the Fed turns toward cutting before markets expect it, equity selloffs driven by growth rather than inflation, and long-bond auctions come in strong.

Dangers: Inflation reaccelerating. The term premium (the extra yield investors demand for tying up money for decades) rising on deficits, heavy auction supply, or worries about Fed independence. The first Fed cut is often "sell the news": after the 50bp cut in September 2024, TLT peaked within days and fell ~15% as the 10-year rose from 3.6% to 4.8%. Stock–bond correlation flips positive when inflation runs above ~3%. In April 2025, during the tariff shock, the 30-year yield jumped about 50bp in a week while stocks fell, and the bond market's "yippy" reaction helped force the tariff pause. After Moody's took away the last U.S. AAA rating in May 2025, a weak 20-year auction pushed the 30-year above 5.1%.

Watch: The jobs report (first Friday), CPI (mid-month), FOMC decisions and the quarterly dot plot, the Treasury's quarterly refunding announcement (early Feb/May/Aug/Nov), and the monthly 20- and 30-year auctions. A "tail" (the auction clearing at a higher yield than expected) is bad; a "stop-through" (clearing at a lower one) is good. The MOVE index and the 30-year yield chart itself.

How to use it: The cleanest setup is buying when the 30-year is stretched near a multi-year high (5% has acted as a ceiling several times since 2023), MOVE is elevated, and the macro data starts to crack, then selling once the market has priced in the Fed cuts. Don't buy into the first cut. It needs about a 55bp fall in long yields for +10%. If stuck: ~4.5–5% paid monthly, state-tax-free, no default risk, but "stuck" in long bonds can mean a slow bleed that lasts quarters. The 2020–2023 drawdown is the worst case to size against.

TLH iShares 10–20 Year Treasury Bond ETF

Category: Long Treasuries · AUM: ~$10B · Duration: ~12 · Yield: ~4.5% · Expense ratio: 0.15%

TLT's quieter sibling. It holds Treasuries maturing in 10 to 20 years, so it skips the 30-year bond where most of the fiscal and term-premium fear shows up. The 20-year part of the curve has been the cheapest point on the curve since the Treasury brought back the 20-year bond in 2020, and demand there is thinner than at 10 or 30 years. That's one reason the 20-year often yields more than the 30-year.

The practical difference from TLT is sensitivity: about three-quarters of TLT's rate exposure. When the curve is steepening because deficit worries push the long end up while the Fed cuts short rates, TLH holds up better than TLT. When a pure growth scare rallies the whole curve, it lags.

Shines when: Rates fall across the curve, especially when the 30-year is weighed down by supply fears the 10–20 year sector doesn't share.

Dangers: The same inflation and rate risk as TLT, with a smaller payoff. It needs about 75bp for a +10% move, which is a large two-month move in the 10–20 year sector.

Watch: The monthly 20-year auction (usually the third week), the 10-year auction, and the gap between the 10-year and 30-year yields.

How to use it: The "I think rates fall, but I'm worried about the long end" version of the trade, for when deficits and auction sizes are in the headlines. Better for the 5–8% end of the flywheel target than the 15% end. If stuck, it bleeds less than TLT in a steepening curve.

EDV Vanguard Extended Duration Treasury ETF

Category: Treasury STRIPS · AUM: ~$3–4B · Duration: ~24 · Yield: ~4.8–5% · Expense ratio: ~0.05%

TLT with the coupons removed. EDV holds STRIPS, which are Treasury bonds split into their separate interest and principal payments so that each piece pays one lump sum at maturity. A zero-coupon bond has a duration equal to its maturity, so a basket of 20–30 year STRIPS behaves like a bond with a duration in the mid-20s, about 1.5 times TLT. Every basis point is worth half again as much.

That leverage works both ways. EDV fell more than 60% from its 2020 peak to the 2023 low, deeper than TLT's roughly 50%. In the October–December 2023 rally it gained about a third, against a little over 20% for TLT. It's cheap to hold (Vanguard's fee is a third of TLT's), but its options are much thinner.

Shines when: The same conditions as TLT, in larger size. It is the most efficient way to make the 10–15% flywheel target on a rate call without leverage or futures.

Dangers: A drawdown 1.5x TLT's. The distribution comes from the STRIPS' built-in price accretion rather than coupons, so it's paid quarterly and can be uneven. Wider bid-ask spreads than TLT when markets are stressed.

Watch: Everything on the TLT list. The 30-year yield matters most, since STRIPS sit at the very long end.

How to use it: Use it when conviction is high and the entry is at a stretched level. A position half the size of a TLT position gives similar dollar exposure with less capital tied up. It needs only about 38bp for +10%. If stuck, the yield is similar to TLT's but the swings aren't. Hold it only if you'd be fine with a 25% drawdown on the position.

ZROZ PIMCO 25+ Year Zero Coupon U.S. Treasury Index ETF

Category: Treasury STRIPS · AUM: ~$1.5B · Duration: ~27 · Yield: ~4.8–5% · Expense ratio: 0.15%

The longest-duration unleveraged Treasury fund you can buy: principal STRIPS maturing in 25 years or more, with a duration near 27. That's about 1.7 times TLT. A 1% fall in long yields is worth close to 30%, which is equity-like volatility from a portfolio with no credit risk at all. iShares' GOVZ (25+ year STRIPS, lower fee) is a near-identical alternative.

ZROZ is what bond traders reach for when they want the biggest possible expression of "long rates are going down" without futures. It's also the fund most exposed to the long-end risks of the 2020s: the U.S. deficit, foreign buyers stepping back, and the term premium moving back to historically normal levels after a decade of Fed bond-buying pushed it below zero.

Shines when: A sharp fall in 30-year yields, such as a recession scare, a flight to safety, a surprise Fed pivot, or a fiscal surprise that reduces long-bond supply.

Dangers: Everything that hurts TLT, at 1.7x. Smaller fund, thinner trading, and a wider spread than TLT or EDV.

Watch: The 30-year yield and the 30-year auctions (a new issue in Feb/May/Aug/Nov with reopenings in between), plus refunding announcements.

How to use it: Only for the highest-conviction entries. About 34bp gets you +10%, and 52bp gets you the full 15%. Size it small: a 5% ZROZ position carries the rate risk of about 8.5% in TLT. If stuck, the same yield and much bigger swings. This is a trading vehicle, not a comfortable hold.

LTPZ PIMCO 15+ Year U.S. TIPS Index ETF

Category: Long TIPS (real rates) · AUM: ~$0.7B · Duration: ~18 · Real yield: ~2.5% plus inflation · Expense ratio: 0.20%

Long-dated Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities. The principal adjusts with CPI, so what you're trading is the real yield, the return after inflation. This is the rate that stock valuations are most directly priced against, and it's what gold tends to move against. TLT is a bet on nominal yields falling. LTPZ is a bet on real yields falling, and it still works if inflation rises, as long as the Fed doesn't respond with higher real rates.

Real yields had their own crash: from about −0.5% on long TIPS in 2021 to over 2.5% by late 2023, and LTPZ lost about a third in 2022. Long real yields near 2.5% are high by the standards of the last twenty years, which is the case for owning it. Distributions are lumpy because they include the monthly inflation adjustment, so they're large when CPI runs hot and can be near zero when it doesn't.

Shines when: Real yields fall, for example when the Fed eases while inflation stays sticky, or when a growth scare comes with no disinflation. It also does well when inflation expectations rise faster than nominal yields.

Dangers: A hawkish Fed pushing real rates up. Deflation scares, which hurt TIPS against regular Treasuries. A small fund with wider spreads.

Watch: The 10- and 30-year real yield (TIPS yield), breakeven inflation (the nominal yield minus the TIPS yield), CPI, the TIPS auction calendar, and the Fed's messaging on inflation tolerance.

How to use it: The flywheel choice when you think rates fall but inflation won't cooperate, or when your gold or growth-stock view depends on real rates. Its duration of about 18 makes it as powerful as TLT. If stuck, you're paid the real yield plus actual inflation, which is the most comfortable carry in this guide during an inflationary stretch.

Long Corporate Credit

Rate exposure plus credit spread. These swing on Treasury yields and on the extra yield corporates pay over Treasuries. The two can offset each other: in a growth scare Treasury yields fall while spreads widen. Only long-duration investment-grade funds make the cut. High yield is too short to swing except in a crash.

Investment gradeVCLTLQD

VCLT Vanguard Long-Term Corporate Bond ETF

Category: Long Investment-Grade Corporate · AUM: ~$10B · Duration: ~13 · Yield: ~5.5% · Expense ratio: ~0.04%

Investment-grade corporate bonds maturing in more than 10 years, issued by the same large companies that fill the Ledger: telecoms (AT&T, Verizon, Comcast), pharma (Pfizer, AbbVie, Amgen), banks, utilities, railroads, and increasingly the AI hyperscalers. Oracle, Meta, and others sold tens of billions of dollars of long bonds in late 2025 to fund data centers. The duration is close to TLT's, and the yield runs roughly a percentage point higher because of the credit spread.

That spread is the second driver. Long IG spreads were near their tightest in decades through 2025, which means the extra yield is thin and the room for spreads to tighten further is small. Most of the upside is in Treasury yields falling. The downside is that yields and spreads can both move against you, as they did in 2022.

Shines when: Treasury yields fall while the economy holds up (a soft landing, disinflation without recession), so spreads stay tight or tighten.

Dangers: A recession scare, where Treasury yields fall but spreads widen and give back much of the gain. A flood of new supply from AI capex borrowing. Downgrades of large issuers to junk.

Watch: Investment-grade spread (the ICE BofA IG OAS), new-issue supply (September and January are the heaviest months), hyperscaler bond deals, and everything on the TLT list.

How to use it: The "soft landing with falling rates" version of TLT, with an extra point of carry. It needs about 70bp combined (Treasury yield plus spread) for +10%. When the macro call is a recession, TLT or EDV is cleaner. If stuck, ~5.5% monthly is the highest carry of the long-duration funds.

LQD iShares iBoxx $ Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF

Category: Investment-Grade Corporate · AUM: ~$30B · Duration: ~8 · Yield: ~5% · Expense ratio: 0.14%

The benchmark IG corporate ETF, with thousands of bonds across all maturities, heavy institutional use, and a liquid options market. Its duration of 8 is at the bottom edge of what can make a flywheel move. It's included because of what it does in panics. In March 2020, as bond dealers stopped making markets, LQD traded at about a 5% discount to the value of the bonds it held. It was the most visible sign the corporate bond market had stopped working. When the Fed announced on March 23 that it would buy corporate bonds and IG ETFs, LQD jumped roughly 7% within days, and the discount closed.

That episode is the setup. Outside a crisis, LQD is a slower VCLT. In a crisis it is a panic-and-rescue trade with a clear catalyst: the policy response.

Shines when: Spreads snap back after a panic, the Fed or Treasury steps in, or rates fall in a soft landing.

Dangers: Normal-times moves are too small for the flywheel (about 130bp needed for +10%). Spread widening in recessions. Discounts to NAV in stress can grow before they close.

Watch: IG spreads, LQD's premium or discount to NAV (published daily), Fed facility announcements in a crisis, and new-issue supply.

How to use it: Keep it on the shelf for a crisis: when spreads have blown out, LQD trades at a discount to NAV, and a policy response is announced or clearly coming. That's a 5–10% two-month move with an obvious exit. The rest of the time, use VCLT for credit duration. If stuck, ~5% carry and a lower-volatility ride than the long funds.

International Bonds

The swing here comes from two things at once: foreign interest rates and the dollar. Unhedged foreign bonds priced in dollars gain when the dollar weakens, so a 5% fall in the dollar is worth about 5% before any rate move. That makes them the flywheel's dollar trade with a yield attached. Currency-hedged funds (like BNDX) remove the swing and are left out.

BWX SPDR Bloomberg International Treasury Bond ETF

Category: Developed ex-U.S. Government (unhedged) · AUM: ~$1.3B · Duration: ~7–8 · Yield: ~3% · Expense ratio: 0.35%

Government bonds of Japan, France, Germany, Italy, the U.K., Canada, Australia, and a dozen smaller developed countries, held in their own currencies. Japan is the largest weight, so BWX is largely a trade on the yen and the euro against the dollar, with foreign rate moves on top. In practice it moves more with the dollar index (DXY) than with any bond market.

That showed in 2025. The dollar fell about 10% in the first half of the year, its worst start in decades, and unhedged foreign bond funds posted gains that a U.S. bond fund couldn't match. Japan is the wild card in the other direction. The Bank of Japan ended negative rates and yield-curve control in 2024, and long Japanese yields rose to records (the 30-year above 3%) through 2025 amid fiscal worries. Rising JGB yields hurt BWX's biggest holding even as a stronger yen helps it.

Shines when: The dollar weakens (the Fed cutting faster than other central banks, U.S. growth lagging, capital leaving the U.S.), foreign central banks cut, and the yen strengthens.

Dangers: A dollar rally, the classic risk-off move that hits BWX even if foreign yields fall. Japanese fiscal stress pushing JGB yields up. A small fund with a 0.35% fee and wide-ish spreads.

Watch: DXY and USD/JPY, the gap between U.S. and German/Japanese yields, and ECB and BoJ meetings. BoJ policy surprises have moved the yen 3–5% in days.

How to use it: This is the flywheel expression of a weak-dollar view. When DXY breaks down on a chart and the Fed is easing faster than the ECB and BoJ, BWX can do 5–10% in two months. If stuck, the yield is only ~3%, and a strong-dollar regime can mean years of drift, as in 2014–2016 and 2021–2022.

IGOV iShares International Treasury Bond ETF

Category: Developed ex-U.S. Government (unhedged) · AUM: ~$1B · Duration: ~8 · Yield: ~3% · Expense ratio: 0.35%

iShares' version of BWX: developed-market government bonds outside the U.S., unhedged, with Japan and the large eurozone issuers dominating. The two funds track different indices but end up with similar country mixes and move almost together. Pick whichever has the tighter spread on the day.

The reason to know both is that the flywheel trade here is a macro view expressed through a thin market. Neither fund is large, and when the dollar trade gets crowded, spreads widen on both. Having two options helps with entries and exits.

Shines when: The same as BWX: a weakening dollar and falling foreign yields.

Dangers: A dollar rally, rising JGB and European yields, and thin liquidity.

Watch: DXY, EUR/USD, USD/JPY, and European and Japanese 10-year yields.

How to use it: Interchangeable with BWX. Use limit orders on both. If stuck: a low yield and a dollar-driven drift.

EMLC VanEck J.P. Morgan EM Local Currency Bond ETF

Category: Emerging-Market Local-Currency Government · AUM: ~$3B · Duration: ~5 · Yield: ~6–6.5% · Expense ratio: 0.30%

Government bonds of Brazil, Mexico, Indonesia, South Africa, India (added to the J.P. Morgan index from 2024), Malaysia, Thailand, Poland, China, and others, each in its own currency and capped so no single country dominates. The yields are real money. Brazil's policy rate was 15% through 2025, well above its inflation rate, and several others pay 7–10%. That carry is the base case. The swing comes from the currencies.

EM currencies move more than developed ones, and they tend to move together. A weak dollar and a risk-on market lift them all, while a dollar squeeze or a global selloff drops them all. After a lost decade (EMLC's price fell by more than a third from 2011 to 2022), 2025 was the turn: the weak dollar and high local yields produced double-digit gains, one of the best years in the fund's history.

Shines when: The dollar weakens, commodity prices rise (which helps Brazil, South Africa, Indonesia), EM central banks cut from high real rates, and global risk appetite is healthy.

Dangers: A dollar squeeze or global risk-off, when the currencies and the bonds fall together. Country blowups (South Africa and Turkey have both had currency crashes). Capital controls. The 2013 taper tantrum hit EM local debt hard.

Watch: DXY, the EM currency index, USD/BRL and USD/MXN, commodity prices, Fed–EM rate differentials, and elections in the largest index countries.

How to use it: The highest-carry swing trade in the guide. When the dollar is rolling over and risk appetite is intact, EMLC can do 5–8% in two months with 6%+ yield underneath. If stuck: you're paid well, but a strong-dollar regime means a slow grind down in which the yield only partly offsets the currency losses.

EMB iShares J.P. Morgan USD Emerging Markets Bond ETF

Category: Emerging-Market Sovereign (USD) · AUM: ~$15B · Duration: ~7 · Yield: ~6–6.5% · Expense ratio: 0.39%

Emerging-market government and quasi-government bonds issued in dollars: Mexico, Saudi Arabia, Indonesia, Turkey, the UAE, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, and dozens of smaller issuers down to frontier credits like Argentina, Egypt, and Nigeria. There's no currency exposure. The two drivers are U.S. Treasury yields and the EM credit spread, the extra yield these countries pay over Treasuries to borrow in dollars.

That makes EMB a cousin of long corporate credit with a different risk: sovereign default instead of corporate default. Its best flywheel moves come when both drivers line up. Treasury yields fall and spreads tighten, typically when the Fed turns dovish without a recession. Frontier credits can add big moves: restructurings in places like Ghana, Zambia, and Sri Lanka have produced sharp recoveries once deals were done.

Shines when: U.S. yields fall with risk appetite intact, oil and commodity prices are stable, and EM spreads tighten.

Dangers: A recession scare (spreads widen and offset the Treasury rally), sovereign defaults, oil crashes hurting Gulf and Latin American issuers, and geopolitical shocks.

Watch: The EMBI spread, 10-year Treasury yields, oil, and rating-agency actions on the large issuers (Mexico, Saudi, Turkey).

How to use it: The "Fed eases, world stays calm" trade, with more carry than VCLT. It's slower than the long Treasury funds (about 130bp combined for +10%), so it fits a 5–8% target. If stuck: ~6% carry and a well-diversified book, the most comfortable hold in the international section.

For the Curious

Popular bond vehicles that fail the flywheel test, and a few ways to do the same trades outside an ETF.

HYG iShares iBoxx $ High Yield Corporate Bond ETF

Category: High Yield · AUM: ~$15–18B · Duration: ~3 · Yield: ~6.5–7% · Expense ratio: 0.49%

The most-traded junk bond ETF, used by hedge funds as a quick credit hedge, with options volume like a large-cap stock. Its duration of about 3 means rates barely move it. It trades like a quiet, low-beta stock index with a 7% yield. The exception is a crisis: HYG fell over 20% in a month in March 2020, then rallied hard after the Fed said in April it would buy high-yield ETFs too.

Why it's popular: Liquid, high-yielding, and a widely watched risk-appetite gauge. When HYG breaks down, equities often follow.

Flywheel verdict: No in normal times. A 5% move needs about 130bp of spread tightening. It's worth watching as a signal and worth buying only after a blowout with a policy response, the same crisis trade as LQD with more credit risk.

TMF Direxion Daily 20+ Year Treasury Bull 3X Shares

Category: Leveraged Long Treasuries · Daily target: 3x the 20+ year index · Expense ratio: ~1%

Three times the daily move of long Treasuries, reset every day. The daily reset causes volatility decay: in a choppy market the fund loses value even if TLT ends flat. In a bear market it's catastrophic. TMF lost more than 90% from its 2020 peak to the 2023 low, while retail investors poured record inflows into it on the way down, each wave expecting the bottom.

Why it's popular: The obvious way to lever a "rates must come down" conviction, and a favorite of retail rate-cut bets.

Flywheel verdict: No. Being stuck in a leveraged daily-reset fund doesn't mean waiting. It means the position slowly decays. If you want more rate sensitivity, EDV and ZROZ give 1.5–1.7x TLT with no decay.

TBT ProShares UltraShort 20+ Year Treasury (and TMV, −3x)

Category: Inverse Leveraged Treasuries · Daily target: −2x (TBT) / −3x (TMV) · Expense ratio: ~0.9–1%

The bet that long yields rise, with leverage. They were big winners in 2022, when TBT roughly doubled. They also carry the same daily-reset decay as TMF, and you pay the bond's yield instead of earning it, so being wrong while rates go sideways is expensive.

Why it's popular: The simplest way to short the bond market in an account that can't short.

Flywheel verdict: No. It fails the "stuck for a year" test outright: flat rates for a year bleed it steadily. It's a short-term hedge or a tactical tool when you're sure a yield breakout is underway.

TLTW iShares 20+ Year Treasury Bond BuyWrite Strategy ETF

Category: Covered-Call Treasuries · Yield: double digits (mostly option premium) · Expense ratio: 0.35%

Holds TLT and sells a one-month call option on it slightly above the price, every month. The option premium becomes a double-digit distribution yield. The catch is that selling calls caps the upside, which is the very move this guide is built around, and leaves you with the full downside.

Why it's popular: A headline yield in the teens from a Treasury fund, and income investors read the yield before the strategy.

Flywheel verdict: No. It gives up the swing you're trading for. The idea is useful in a different form: if you're already stuck in TLT, selling your own calls against it after a rally stalls turns a stuck position into income.

/ZB · /UB U.S. Treasury Bond Futures

Category: Futures (CME) · Underlying: 15–25 year bonds (/ZB) and 25+ year bonds (/UB) · Nearly 24-hour trading

The instruments bond professionals actually trade. /ZB (the "bond") and /UB (the "ultra bond") track the long end, and /ZN and /TN do the same for the 10-year. There's no expense ratio and no ETF spread, trading runs nearly around the clock, and the margin makes the exposure capital-efficient. The ultra bond has more rate sensitivity than any ETF here. CME's micro Treasury yield futures let you trade the yield itself in small sizes.

Why it's popular: The deepest, cheapest way to trade rates, and the market where the move actually happens. TLT follows the futures, not the other way around.

Flywheel verdict: The efficient version of the same trade for anyone set up for futures. But the leverage is built in, positions roll quarterly, and "stuck" means posting margin rather than collecting a yield. It's a tool for the same ideas, not a replacement for the ETF sleeve.

FGBL · Gilt · JGB Foreign Government Bond Futures

Category: Futures · German Bund (Eurex FGBL), Italian BTP (Eurex FBTP), U.K. Long Gilt (ICE), Japanese 10-year JGB (Osaka)

The way to trade a single country's rates directly, with no currency exposure beyond margin. These are the markets behind some of the most violent bond moves of the decade. In September 2022, the U.K.'s unfunded "mini-budget" pushed 30-year gilt yields from about 3.7% to over 5% in days. Pension funds using leveraged liability-matching strategies (LDI) were forced to sell, and the Bank of England had to step in with emergency purchases. Japan's long end has been the slow-motion version: after decades of yields pinned near zero, the 30-year JGB climbed past 3% in 2025 as the Bank of Japan stepped back and fiscal worries grew.

Why it's popular: Relative-value and macro trades (Bund against Treasuries, BTP spreads against Bunds) and the crisis setups the ETFs can't isolate.

Flywheel verdict: Outside the sleeve. These are futures on foreign exchanges with margin and rolls. U.S.-listed single-country government bond ETFs are either tiny or don't exist, so for a flywheel-sized international bond view, BWX, IGOV, and EMLC are the practical choices.

DIRECT Buying Long Treasuries or STRIPS Directly

Category: Individual securities · Via a brokerage bond desk or TreasuryDirect · No expense ratio

Instead of a fund, buy a single 30-year Treasury or a long STRIPS. The price swings are the same as the equivalent ETF, but there's one key difference: a bond has a maturity date and a fund doesn't. If the trade fails, holding to maturity guarantees you the yield you bought at. A fund is always rolling into new bonds, so it has no date when you're guaranteed to be made whole.

Why it's popular: Locking in a known yield, like 5% for 30 years, with certainty.

Flywheel verdict: A better "stuck" outcome than any fund, and a worse trading experience: wider bid-ask spreads on small lots and no options. It's worth it when the entry yield is one you'd happily lock in for decades, which is the ultimate version of the flywheel test.
