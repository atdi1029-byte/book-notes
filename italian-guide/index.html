1. The Portuguese Advantage

What you already own, what you have to buy, and a map of the whole trip before you start.

Portuguese and Italian are two children of the same parent. Latin spread across the Roman world as a spoken language, and in each province it drifted in its own direction for fifteen centuries. Italian is the drift that happened in Tuscany; Portuguese is the drift that happened on the Atlantic edge of Iberia. The two drifts were different, but they started from the same words, the same two genders, the same verb system with its three conjugation classes, the same way of building a sentence around a conjugated verb, and the same subjunctive mood. That shared skeleton is why an Italian newspaper already half-reads itself to you.

The shared material is bigger than it feels. Linguists who count overlapping vocabulary put Portuguese and Italian at roughly 80 percent shared roots. Grammar overlaps even more: the verb tenses line up almost one for one, adjectives agree the same way, pronouns do the same jobs, and a sentence usually has the same number of words in the same order. Take any ordinary sentence and lay the two languages side by side:

Ontem comprei um livro muito interessante na livraria perto da estação.

Ieri ho comprato un libro molto interessante nella libreria vicino alla stazione.

Yesterday I bought a very interesting book in the bookshop near the station.

Nine words of eleven are transparent. Livro is libro, interessante is interessante, estação is stazione, muito is molto, na is nella, perto da is vicino alla. The two words that are not transparent are the whole lesson of this guide in miniature. Ontem became ieri: a plain vocabulary gap, one of a few hundred everyday words you simply have to learn. And comprei became ho comprato: not a vocabulary gap at all, but a grammar shift. Italian speech does not use the simple past for yesterday. It uses the compound past, the one Portuguese reserves for tenho comprado, and it means something different by it. Vocabulary gaps are filled by exposure. Grammar shifts are filled by understanding, and understanding is what this guide is for.

Where the twenty percent lives

The gap between the two languages is not spread evenly. It is concentrated in a dozen places, and if you know where those places are you can walk around them. Most learners who come from Portuguese never get a map of the gap; they absorb Italian the way a beginner from any language would and spend months relearning things they already knew under another name. Three kinds of difference

It helps to sort every difference you meet into one of three bins, because each bin is handled differently.

Vocabulary gaps are words whose Italian form shares no root with the Portuguese one: ontem and ieri, janela and finestra, cadeira and sedia, trabalho and lavoro, rua and via, cachorro and cane. There are perhaps six hundred of these in everyday speech. They are the easiest kind of difference because they look foreign: you will never be tempted to say ontem in Italian. You simply learn them, and Chapter 26 lists the ones that matter, grouped by topic. The second half of this bin is subtler: words that exist in both languages but where Italian habitually uses a different one. Falar has an Italian cousin, favellare, but nobody has used it in three hundred years; Italians parlano. Comer survives only in the literary comere; Italians mangiano. The Swap List in Chapter 3 teaches you to convert words and also warns you when the converted word is a museum piece.

Shape shifts are words that share a root but changed their surface in different directions. Noite and notte, filho and figlio, chave and chiave, coração and cuore, dor and dolore. Portuguese and Italian each applied a set of sound changes to Latin, and those changes were regular. If you know the rules, you can run a Portuguese word forward to Latin and back down to Italian in your head. Latin ct became Portuguese it and Italian tt; so oito is otto, leite is latte, feito is fatto, peito is petto. Portuguese dropped every l and n that stood between two vowels; Italian kept them. So wherever a Portuguese word has two vowels touching or a nasal vowel, suspect a lost consonant: cor is colore, lua is luna, mão is mano, pão is pane, saúde is salute. Chapter 3 gives you about thirty of these rules with the percentage of the time each one works.

Grammar shifts are the twelve items in the table above: cases where Portuguese and Italian build the same meaning with different machinery. These are the subject of Parts II and III, which are the heart of the book. For each one you will get the Portuguese structure you know, the Italian structure beside it, a stated rule for converting one to the other, and a red box showing the exact sentence a Portuguese speaker produces when the habit fires unchecked.

The habits that will hurt you

Knowing a sister language is an enormous asset and a specific liability. The asset is comprehension: from the first day you understand far more than you can say. The liability is that your mouth knows Portuguese so well that it will produce Portuguese-shaped Italian without asking permission. These are the five reflexes that betray Portuguese speakers most, and that this guide returns to again and again.

The five reflexes

1. Adding -s for the plural. You will say due libros for weeks. Italian plurals change the vowel: due libri. The reflex fades, but only if you catch it every time. (Chapter 5)

2. Reaching for stare whenever you would say estar. Estou feliz is sono felice. Está frio is fa freddo. Onde está? is dov'è? Italian stare is a narrow verb; estar is a wide one. (Chapter 8)

3. Using the simple past in conversation. Mangiai is grammatically fine and sounds like a nineteenth-century novel, or like Sicily. Spoken Italian wants ho mangiato. (Chapter 9)

4. Swallowing final vowels and softening ti and di. Brazilian Portuguese turns final -e into -i and ti into tchi. Italian does neither: sette is SET-te, not SE-tchi. This single habit marks you as Brazilian more than any other. (Chapter 2)

5. Building a future subjunctive or a personal infinitive. Both are Portuguese inventions that Italian never had. Se você quiser is se vuoi; antes de saírmos is prima di uscire. (Chapters 13 and 16)

How Italians will hear you

Italians are used to being understood by Spanish speakers and are mildly surprised when a Portuguese speaker understands them, because spoken Portuguese is nearly opaque to them. Your comprehension will seem to them like a trick. Your accent will read as Brazilian and warm, and the errors you make will be the errors of a Romance speaker: wrong article, wrong auxiliary, a -s on a plural. None of these block communication. What does block it is pronunciation that keeps Portuguese vowel reduction, because Italian carries its grammar in its final vowels. Parlo, parla, parli, parlo and parlò are five different forms distinguished only by the last sound. Say them with a reduced Brazilian final vowel and the listener is guessing who did what, and when.

That is why this guide puts the sound system second, before any grammar. Italian is a language where the endings are the grammar, so you have to be able to hear and say the endings before anything else makes sense. A Portuguese speaker who fixes the five pronunciation habits of Chapter 2 and learns the twelve grammar shifts of the table above has, in practical terms, learned Italian. The rest is words, and words come free with reading.

2. The Sound System

Italian spelling tells you exactly how to say a word. The only thing in the way is your Portuguese mouth.

Italian is one of the few languages where the writing system is honest. With about ten rules you can pronounce any word you read, and with the same rules you can spell any word you hear. There are no silent letters except h, no vowel that changes its sound depending on mood, and no equivalent of the Portuguese final -o that is written one way and said another. The challenge for a Portuguese speaker is not learning the Italian rules, which take an hour. It is switching off the Portuguese rules, which have been running automatically since childhood and which turn clean Italian into a Brazilian-flavored approximation. This chapter lists the Italian rules, then names the Portuguese reflexes one by one so you can hear yourself doing them.

Seven vowels, all of them full

Italian has seven vowel sounds: a, i, u, an open and a closed e, and an open and a closed o. Portuguese has those same seven in stressed position, and then it has a dozen more: five nasal vowels, nasal diphthongs, and a whole set of reduced vowels that appear in unstressed syllables. Italian has none of the extras. Every vowel in an Italian word, stressed or not, is said with its full value, with the mouth in the same position it would be in if the vowel were stressed. The Italian a is always the a of casa, never the murmured a at the end of Brazilian casa. The Italian o is always a real ô or ó, never the u that Brazilian puts at the end of carro. The Italian e is always ê or é, never the i of Brazilian tarde.

Reflex 1: reducing the final vowel

Brazilian Portuguese says tarde as TAR-dji and carro as KA-hu. Carry that into Italian and sette becomes SE-tchi and libro becomes LI-bru. Italians hear the first as a foreign word and the second as a plural that went wrong, because libri ends in a vowel very close to the one you just produced. Say sette as SET-te with the final e exactly as open and clear as the first one, and libro as LI-bro with a round, full o.

This matters more in Italian than it would in almost any other language because the final vowel is the grammar. Parlo is I speak, parla is he speaks, parli is you speak, parle is not a word. Amico is one friend, amici is several, amica is a woman, amiche several women. Reduce the ending and you have deleted the information the listener needs. European Portuguese speakers have the opposite reflex, swallowing unstressed vowels entirely (telefone as tl-FON), and need the same fix: every Italian vowel gets its full syllable.

Nasal vowels are the second habit. Portuguese bem, um, com and são are single nasal vowels with no consonant at the end. Italian bene, un, con and sono are vowel plus an actual n, with the tongue touching the ridge behind the teeth. The vowel before it stays clean. When you say un libro, the n is a real consonant that almost glues onto the l; it is not the nasal hum of um livro. Practice con, non, in, un, bene, sento, tanto and listen for the moment your tongue makes contact.

Open and closed e and o: your advantage

Here Portuguese gives you something most learners of Italian lack. English, French, Spanish and German speakers struggle to hear the difference between pesca with an open e (peach) and pesca with a closed e (fishing), or between botte open (blows) and botte closed (barrel). You already make this distinction every day: avó and avô, pé and pê, sede (thirst, open) and sede (headquarters, closed). The Italian open è is your é; the Italian closed é is your ê; open ò is ó and closed ó is ô.

Better still, the two languages usually agree about which one a given word has, because both inherited it from the same Latin vowel. Pé is open and so is the e in piede; bolo has a closed ô and so does bolo. The agreement is not perfect and Italians themselves disagree by region (a Milanese and a Roman will say perché differently), so this is not something to lose sleep over. The practical rule is: when a Portuguese cognate exists, use its vowel quality. You will be right most of the time, and when you are wrong you will sound like someone from another part of Italy, which is how every Italian sounds to every other Italian.

The hard/soft switch, both directions

Italian uses h to keep c and g hard before e and i, and uses a silent i to keep them soft before a, o and u. Portuguese does the same jobs with qu, gu and j:

PT que, qui (hard k) → IT che, chi · PT gue, gui (hard g) → IT ghe, ghi · PT ça, ço is not cia, cio (see the z rule in Chapter 3) · PT ja, jo (soft zh) → IT gia, gio (soft dj)

So queijo hides the Italian pattern of che: both are ke. And Guilherme hides ghi: both are gi. You already use the trick; Italian just spells it with h.

Reflex 2: softening ti and di

Most of Brazil says tia as TCHI-a and dia as DJI-a. Italian never does this. Dieci is DYE-tchi with a hard d; tipo is TI-po; dire is DI-re; notte ends in a clean te. The confusion is doubly bad because Italian has real tch and dj sounds, spelled ci and gi, so tipo said with the Brazilian reflex sounds like a different, nonexistent word cipo. Keep t and d as the hard dental stops you use in tapa and dado, and let only c and g make the soft sounds.

Reflex 3: the Brazilian r and l

Brazilian rua and carro use a sound in the throat, close to an English h. Italian has no such sound anywhere. Every Italian r is made with the tip of the tongue: a single tap when written once (caro, Roma, parlare), a longer trill when written twice (carro, terra). Roma is RO-ma with the r of caro, never HO-ma. The syllable-final r in parlare and per is also a tap, never the h or retroflex of Brazilian porta.

The l works the same way. Brazilian turns a final l into w: Brasil is bra-ZIW, sal is saw. Italian l is always a clear tongue-tip l: il is il, alto is AL-to, sale is SA-le, and Brasile is bra-ZI-le.

Stress: mostly where you'd expect

Italian stress falls on the second-to-last syllable by default, exactly like Portuguese, and the two languages agree on most words: a-MI-co, par-LA-re, fi-NES-tra, im-por-TAN-te. Italian marks the stress in writing only when it falls on the last syllable, with a grave accent: città, caffè, perché, però, più, università. Every word ending in a stressed vowel carries the mark, and no other word carries one. That is the whole Italian accent system: no ã, no ç, no ê in the middle of words, nothing on the penultimate syllable.

The consequence is that words stressed on the third-to-last syllable are not marked, and you have to know them. Italian has far more of these than Portuguese, and it is where your intuition will fail most. Portuguese marks them all (médico, telefone is penultimate but távola, número, câmera); Italian leaves them bare: medico, tavolo, numero, camera, telefono, difficile, facile, giovane, ultimo, subito, abitare. The good news is that where a Portuguese cognate exists, the stress usually matches it, accent mark or not. Médico tells you MEdico; número tells you NUmero; possível tells you posSIbile.

Three stress rules that override your intuition

1. Third-person plural verbs keep the stress of the singular. PARla → PARlano, not parLAno. Abita → Abitano. TElefona → TElefonano. Portuguese shifts the stress forward (fala → falam is the same syllable, but falavam, falaram are not); Italian never does.

2. Most -ere infinitives are stressed on the stem. Portuguese vender, escrever, perder stress the ending. Italian VENdere, SCRIvere, PERdere, PRENdere, LEGgere, CREdere, CHIEdere, METtere, VIvere, coNOscere pull it back. The exceptions stress the e like Portuguese does and are mostly modal or perception verbs: aVEre, doVEre, poTEre, saPEre, voLEre, veDEre, caDEre, piaCEre, teNEre, rimaNEre, paRE-re, seDEre, vaLEre, teMEre.

3. Greek-origin words in -ia stress the i. farmaCIa, poliZIa, democraZIa, filosoFIa, anatoMIa. Portuguese agrees on some (filosofia) and disagrees on others (farmácia, polícia). The exceptions, with stress on the preceding syllable, are words that were in Latin already: ITalia, GRAzia, noTIzia, maLIzia.

A note on the accent marks themselves, since Italians care. The grave accent (à è ì ò ù) is the normal one. The acute (é) appears only on a final closed e: perché, poiché, né, sé, tré as in ventitré. The word for "is" is è with a grave, open; the word for "and" is e with no accent, closed. A few one-syllable words use the accent to tell them apart: è is / e and; là there / la the; dà gives / da from; sì yes / si oneself; né nor / ne of it; sé himself / se if. Writing e for è is the Italian equivalent of writing esta for está: everyone does it in text messages and nobody does it in anything they want taken seriously.

Elision and truncation

Italian hates two vowels bumping into each other across a word boundary and hates certain consonants ending a word, and it fixes both with an apostrophe or by dropping a vowel. Portuguese does almost none of this in writing, so the shapes look odd until you see the pattern.

Elision drops a final vowel before a word starting with a vowel and marks the spot with an apostrophe: lo amico → l'amico, la amica → l'amica, una amica → un'amica, dove è → dov'è, ci è → c'è, come è → com'è, quello uomo → quell'uomo, mi ha detto → m'ha detto. It is mandatory with the articles lo, la, una and with quello and bello; it is optional and increasingly rare with pronouns. Portuguese does the same thing in speech (a água comes out as one word) but never writes it, which is why l'acqua looks foreign and a água doesn't. The plural le never elides: le amiche, not l'amiche.

Truncation drops the final vowel of a word before another word, with no apostrophe, when the word ends in -le, -re, -ne, -no after a vowel. It is mandatory for a handful of very common words and optional elsewhere: uno → un (un libro), buono → buon (buon giorno), bello → bel (bel tempo), grande → gran (gran parte), signore → signor (signor Rossi), dottore → dottor, professore → professor, quale → qual (qual è, with no apostrophe, a famous spelling trap for Italians themselves). You will also hear infinitives lose their -e in speech: voler dire, far vedere, andar via, aver fatto. Portuguese has one truncation, grande → grão in a few fixed phrases; Italian does it all day.

Rhythm and intonation

Brazilian Portuguese already has a fairly even, syllable-by-syllable rhythm, so the Italian beat will feel familiar. The difference is that Italian lengthens stressed vowels noticeably when they are followed by a single consonant: casa is KAA-za, vero is VEE-ro. Before a double consonant the vowel is short and the consonant takes the time instead: cassa is KAS-sa. This give and take between vowel length and consonant length is most of what makes Italian sound Italian. If your Italian sounds flat, it is almost always because you are giving every syllable the same length.

Questions work as in Portuguese: no word order change, no auxiliary, just a rising tune at the end. Parli italiano? is the same words as Parli italiano. The Italian rise is sharper and comes later than the Brazilian one, right on the final syllable. Statements fall at the end more firmly than in Brazilian Portuguese, which tends to trail off; an Italian sentence lands. European Portuguese speakers will find the intonation closer to their own.

One last regional honesty. Everything above describes the standard pronunciation taught in schools and used on national television. Real Italians vary: in the north, s between vowels is always voiced and double consonants soften; in Rome and the south, double consonants grow and some singles double up; in Tuscany, c between vowels turns into a breath. None of this affects you yet. Learn the standard, and Chapter 32 explains what you are hearing when a Neapolitan speaks.

3. The Great Swap List

Thirty sound-change rules that turn a Portuguese word into its Italian cousin. Learn them and you can guess ten thousand words.

Portuguese and Italian each took Latin and bent it in a consistent way. Because the bending was consistent, the relationship between a Portuguese word and its Italian cousin is also consistent, and you can learn the relationship once instead of learning the words one at a time. This is how Portuguese speakers end up reading Italian within a week: not because they memorized vocabulary but because they absorbed, half-consciously, that -ção is -zione and -dade is -tà and lh is gli. This chapter makes the half-conscious knowledge explicit, adds the rules you would never have found on your own, and tells you how often each one holds.

The hit rates are honest estimates, not measurements. "Almost always" means you can bet on it; "often" means guess it and listen for the correction; "sometimes" means it is a hint, not a rule. In every case the swap applies only when the two words actually share a root. The last part of the chapter deals with the awkward truth that the most common words are often the ones that don't.

A swap rule gives you a guess. Reading Italian turns the guess into knowledge. Use the rules to read earlier and faster, and let reading do the memorizing.

Part B: the consonant shifts

Inside words, the two languages applied different sound laws to the same Latin consonants. These are less obvious than the suffixes, but once you see them you cannot unsee them, and they explain thousands of pairs that look unrelated on the page.

Rule 1: drop the Portuguese e- before s + consonant

Latin words beginning with s plus a consonant got an e- stuck on the front in Portuguese. Italian left them alone. Take the e off and you have the Italian word.

escola scuola · estação stazione · estudar studiare · escrever scrivere · estrada strada · especial speciale · espaço spazio · estudante studente · estado stato · estrela stella · estômago stomaco · Espanha Spagna · espírito spirito · estranho strano · escuro scuro · estreito stretto · escada scala · espelho specchio · escolher scegliere · esporte sport · esposa sposa (bride; a wife is moglie).

Hit rate: almost always. The same goes for the silent h-: história is storia, hospital is ospedale, homem is uomo, hora is ora, humano is umano, hoje is oggi, hotel is hotel. Italian has no word-initial h except in ho, hai, ha, hanno and borrowed words.

Watch for esperar: it splits into two Italian verbs. To hope is sperare; to wait is aspettare. Esquerda is sinistra.

Rule 2: Portuguese ch, pr, br, cl, fl → Italian chi, pi, bi, fi

Latin began many words with pl-, cl-, fl- and bl-. Portuguese turned the common ones into ch- and the learned ones into pr-, cr-, fr-, br-. Italian kept the first consonant and turned the l into an i glide.

chuva pioggia · cheio pieno · chave chiave · chamar chiamare · chorar piangere · chama fiamma · chumbo piombo · prato piatto · praça piazza · praia spiaggia · prazer piacere · branco bianco · claro chiaro · flor fiore · flauta flauto (learned, so Italian kept it too) · igreja chiesa · plano piano · pleno pieno.

Hit rate: almost always when the Latin cluster is there. The same shift happens inside words: olho occhio, orelha orecchio, joelho ginocchio, velho vecchio, espelho specchio, coelho coniglio, where Latin -cl- became Portuguese -lh- and Italian -cchi-.

Watch for chão: its cousin piano means a floor of a building (il primo piano) or "flat, quiet, slowly" (piano piano), not the ground. The ground is il pavimento indoors and la terra or per terra outdoors.

Rule 3: lh → gli, nh → gn

Same sounds, different spelling. Portuguese lh from Latin -li- is Italian gli; Portuguese nh from Latin -gn- or -ni- is Italian gn.

filho figlio · folha foglia · alho aglio · melhor migliore · mulher moglie (wife; a woman is donna) · família famiglia · maravilha meraviglia · batalha battaglia · detalhe dettaglio · toalha tovaglia (tablecloth; a towel is asciugamano) · palha paglia.

sonho sogno · banho bagno · senhor signore · ganhar guadagnare (earn; to win is vincere) · unha unghia · aranha ragno · castanha castagna · montanha montagna · junho giugno · lenha legna · cunhado cognato · punho pugno · Espanha Spagna.

Hit rate: often. The failures are the -inho words, where nh is a diminutive and goes to -ino: vinho vino, vizinho vicino, caminho cammino, pinho pino. And trabalho is lavoro, milhão is milione, estranho is strano, tamanho is dimensione or taglia.

Latin -ct- and -pt- became -it- in Portuguese and a doubled -tt- in Italian. This one rule explains dozens of everyday words that look nothing alike.

Rule 5: Portuguese x → Italian ss, s or sc

Latin x survived as a letter in Portuguese and was simplified in Italian. Before a vowel it became ss; before a consonant, plain s; and where Portuguese says it as sh, Italian often has sc.

Hit rate: often. Italian has no x in native words at all, so when you see one in your Portuguese word, something has to change.

Rule 6: put back the l or n that Portuguese lost

This is the strangest and most useful rule. Portuguese deleted every single l and n that stood between two vowels. The n usually left a nasal vowel behind as a ghost; the l left nothing, just two vowels in contact. Italian kept both consonants. So wherever a Portuguese word has a nasal vowel, a hiatus, or a stressed vowel at the end with nothing after it, suspect a missing l or n.

Hit rate: often, and when it works it feels like magic. The grammar consequence is large: Portuguese -am, -em, -ão verb endings are Italian -ano, -ono, -anno with the consonant restored: falam parlano, têm hanno, são sono, vão vanno, dão danno, estão stanno.

Between vowels, Portuguese turned Latin p, t, c into b, d, g. Italian kept the hard consonant. So a Portuguese b, d or g inside a word is often an Italian p, t or c.

b → p: saber sapere · cabelo capelli · aberto aperto · sobre sopra · abril aprile · sabor sapore · cabra capra · cobrir coprire · lobo lupo · obra opera · cabeça capo (head of something; the body part is testa) · pobre povero.

Hit rate: often. It fails when Portuguese dropped the consonant entirely: pé piede, fé fede, nó nodo, cru crudo, ver vedere, crer credere, cair cadere, rir ridere, ouvir udire, suar sudare. Run it the other way: a Portuguese one-syllable verb is almost always an Italian verb with a d in the middle. And it runs backwards for two verbs where Italian dropped and Portuguese kept: dizer dire, fazer fare.

Rule 10: final -z → -ce; a final consonant grows an -e

Italian words end in vowels. A Portuguese word ending in a consonant has to acquire one, and the consonant usually changes with it.

-z → -ce: voz voce · luz luce · paz pace · noz noce · cruz croce · feliz felice · capaz capace · raiz radice · juiz giudice · dez dieci · diz dice · fez fece.

-r → -re, -l → -le: mar mare · sal sale · mal male · sol sole · mel miele · Brasil Brasile · Portugal Portogallo · every infinitive, every -or and -al noun.

-u → -lo, -llo, -eo: céu cielo · chapéu cappello · grau grado · museu museo · europeu europeo · judeu giudeo.

Hit rate: almost always for the shape. Vez is volta, nariz is naso, rapaz is ragazzo, azul is azzurro or blu, papel is carta.

Rule 11: the vowels — ou → o, ei → e, open é/ó → ie/uo

Portuguese grew diphthongs where Italian kept a single vowel, and Italian grew diphthongs where Portuguese kept a single open vowel. The two patterns are mirror images.

ou → o: ouro oro · pouco poco · touro toro · tesouro tesoro · coisa cosa · sou sono · couro cuoio. (Outro is altro: the ou there came from al, which Portuguese also turned into ou.)

Hit rate: sometimes for the diphthongs, often for ou and ei. Use them to recognize, not to produce.

Here is the catch in all of the above. The swap rules work beautifully on the educated layer of the language, the Latinate words that both languages borrowed from books. 4. Articles & Contractions

Portuguese has four definite articles. Italian has seven, chosen by the sound that follows, and they fuse with five prepositions into a table you will meet in every sentence you ever read.

Articles come first among the grammar chapters because you cannot read a line of Italian without them, and because they are the first place where Italian makes a choice Portuguese never asked you to make. In Portuguese the article depends on the noun alone: masculine or feminine, one or many, and you are done. O, a, os, as. In Italian the article also depends on the first sound of the next word, which is why the masculine has three singular forms and two plural forms. Once you see that the extra forms exist only to make the words flow, the system is small, and the contraction table that sits on top of it turns out to be your old friends do, no and ao wearing Italian clothes.

Il and i are the defaults. L' and gli handle vowels. And lo and gli handle a specific list of awkward consonant clusters, the ones that would be hard to say after a bare l. Try saying il studente fast and you will feel why Italian says lo studente instead: the l and the st collide. The o is there to separate them.

The lo test: would the Portuguese word start with es-?

You already know which Italian words take lo, because they are the words Portuguese fixed in a different way. Rule 1 of the Swap List said Portuguese adds e- in front of s + consonant; Italian instead adds an o to the article. So:

Add the words beginning with z (lo zucchero, lo zaino, lo zio, lo zero), the few in gn and ps (lo gnocco, lo psicologo), and the foreign ones in y (lo yogurt), and the list is complete. Everything else masculine is il. In the plural, lo words and vowel words both take gli: gli studenti, gli zii, gli amici.

Reflex: il before everything

The reflex is to treat il as o and use it for every masculine noun. Il studente, il zio, il amico are the three most common article mistakes a Romance speaker makes, and Italians notice them the way you notice o água. Say lo studente, lo zio, l'amico. The plural mistake is i amici for gli amici, and it sounds just as wrong.

The feminine reflex runs the other way: Portuguese never elides, so a amiga and a água keep their article whole. Italian must elide: l'amica, l'acqua, l'ora, l'idea. La amica is as wrong as il amico.

The indefinite follows the same logic as the definite: uno goes exactly where lo goes, and un everywhere else in the masculine. The one detail Italians themselves get wrong in writing is the apostrophe. Un amico has none because un is a truncation of uno, and truncations take no apostrophe. There is no plural indefinite article; uns and umas are handled by the partitive below or by alcuni, alcune.

Where Italian uses the article and Portuguese doesn't

The two languages agree on most uses of the article: generic plurals (os italianos / gli italiani), body parts with reflexives (lavo as mãos / mi lavo le mani), todos os dias / tutti i giorni, professions after "to be" without an article (sou médico / sono medico). The places they disagree are few and they are frequent, which makes them worth a list.

Italian adds an article where Brazilian Portuguese drops one

Possessives. Il mio libro, la tua casa, i nostri amici. Brazilian meu livro drops it; European o meu livro keeps it, and the European habit is the Italian one. The single exception: a singular, unmodified family member takes no article. Mio padre, mia madre, mio fratello, mia sorella, mia moglie. Add a plural, an adjective or a diminutive and the article returns: i miei fratelli, la mia cara madre, il mio fratellino, la mia mamma. (Chapter 21.)

Clock times. Sono le tre, è l'una, alle nove, dalle due alle quattro. Portuguese são três horas, é uma hora, às nove, das duas às quatro has the article only when it is fused. Italian always has it, and the verb agrees with the number of hours: sono le tre plural, è l'una singular, è mezzogiorno singular.

Dates and years. Il cinque maggio, il primo gennaio, nel 2020, negli anni Ottanta. Portuguese cinco de maio has a de and no article; Italian has an article and no di. Em 2020 is nel 2020, with the article fused in.

Languages as nouns. Studio l'italiano, l'italiano è facile, traduco dal portoghese. Portuguese estudo italiano goes bare. Italian drops the article only directly after parlare and after in: parlo italiano, in italiano.

Percentages. Il venti per cento, il 3%. Portuguese vinte por cento is bare.

Days of the week, habitual. La domenica vado in chiesa means aos domingos, every Sunday. Domenica vado in chiesa, no article, means no domingo, this coming one. Portuguese says both with no domingo and lets context decide; Italian decides with the article.

Titles in the third person. Il signor Rossi è arrivato, la dottoressa Bianchi, il professor Conti. Portuguese does this too (o senhor Rossi chegou); the difference is only that Italian truncates the title (signor, dottor, professor before a name) and drops the article when speaking to the person: Buongiorno, signor Rossi.

Italian drops the article where Portuguese keeps one

Countries after in. L'Italia è bella, amo il Brasile, but vivo in Italia, vado in Brasile, in Francia, in Toscana. Portuguese na Itália, no Brasil keeps the article inside the contraction; Italian drops it after in for singular, unmodified countries, regions and large islands. The article comes back for plurals and for modified names: negli Stati Uniti, nei Paesi Bassi, nell'Italia del Sud. Cities take a with no article in both languages: a Roma, em Roma.

Everyday places after in and a. This is the big one. A long list of common destinations takes a bare preposition in Italian where Portuguese fuses in an article: in centro (no centro), in ufficio (no escritório), in banca (no banco), in chiesa (na igreja), in città (na cidade), in montagna (na montanha), in campagna (no campo), in piscina, in palestra (na academia), in biblioteca, in farmacia, in macchina (no carro), in treno, in aereo, in cucina, in camera, in bagno, in giardino, in vacanza (de férias), in strada; and a casa (em casa, same), a scuola (na escola), a letto (na cama), a tavola (à mesa), a teatro (no teatro), a cena, a pranzo. The article returns the moment the noun is specified: nella banca di fronte, nel centro di Roma, alla scuola di mio figlio. A few places keep the article always: al mare, al cinema, al ristorante, al bar, allo stadio, all'università, in aeroporto or all'aeroporto.

Portuguese has this pattern in a few phrases, em casa, na cama versus de cama, a pé; Italian has it in dozens. Reading will give you the list faster than memorizing it; the rule of thumb is that a familiar institution with no specifics goes bare.

Reflex: nella Itàlia, nel centro, a tre

Three sentences that mark a Portuguese speaker instantly: vivo nell'Italia for moro na Itália (say vivo in Italia); lavoro nel centro for trabalho no centro (say lavoro in centro); ci vediamo a tre for a gente se vê às três (say ci vediamo alle tre). In each case the Portuguese contraction tempted you to produce an Italian one, or stopped you from producing one you needed.

The partitive: del pane, dei libri

Portuguese says quero pão and comprei livros with no word in front of the noun. Italian can do that too, but it more often puts di + article in front, meaning "some": voglio del pane, ho comprato dei libri, c'è della gente, conosco degli italiani. This is the partitive article, and it is the closest Italian comes to a plural of un. In the singular it means "some of" a mass (del vino, dello zucchero, dell'acqua, della pasta); in the plural it means "some, a few" (dei ragazzi, degli amici, delle idee) and matches Portuguese uns, umas, alguns.

Comprei pão, queijo e umas maçãs.

Ho comprato del pane, del formaggio e delle mele.

I bought bread, cheese and some apples.

Tem gente na praça.

C'è della gente in piazza.

There are people in the square.

Three working rules. First, the partitive is optional in positive sentences, so voglio pane is fine and sounds slightly more clipped; when in doubt, use it. Second, it is dropped in negatives: non ho soldi, non c'è pane, non ho amici qui, not non ho dei soldi. Third, it is usually avoided after a preposition, because con dei and di dei sound clumsy: sono uscito con amici, un piatto di pasta. For "a little" use un po' di, exactly like um pouco de: un po' di zucchero, un po' di tempo. For "a few" you can also say alcuni, alcune or qualche + singular, which Chapter 22 explains.
