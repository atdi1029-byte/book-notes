# The Little Book of Market Wizards — Notes
## Jack D. Schwager (2014, 182 pages)

## FOREWORD (Peter Brandt)
- N001 [SHOULD KNOW] Peter Brandt reads Market Wizards every Christmas break alongside The Bourne Trilogy — MW for mental preparation, Bourne for entertainment. Calls it his annual tradition since 1981.
- N002 [MUST KNOW] Brandt's core belief: consistently profitable traders share exactly TWO things — (1) an approach reflecting their unique personality, and (2) aggressive risk management. Everything else is secondary.
- N003 [SHOULD KNOW] Brandt dismisses "how-to" trading books that promise 70-80% win rate systems. Says the secret is NOT in entry signals — clever marketers (most of whom aren't successful traders) feed this false belief.
- N004 [SHOULD KNOW] Market Wizards series compared to Edwin Lefèvre's Reminiscences of a Stock Operator — Brandt predicts it will remain just as timely 80 years from now.

## PREFACE (Schwager)
- N005 [MUST KNOW] Book distills 25 years of interviews with 59 of the world's best traders across four Market Wizards books spanning a quarter century. Not a replacement for the series — a "pithy introduction" to the major insights.
- N006 [SHOULD KNOW] Not a how-to book on trading techniques. No formulas or recommendations. Too many aspiring traders seek formulaic treatment while missing concepts essential to success regardless of methodology.
- N007 [MUST KNOW] Schwager's insight: the principles of trading success apply broadly to success in ANY endeavor. A minister once told him the trading success principles were also critical to building a congregation. Schwager suspects these are universal principles of success discovered through the lens of trading.
- N008 [SHOULD KNOW] Different readers consistently cite different interviews as their personal favorites — suggesting the lessons are filtered through individual personality and experience.

## CHAPTER 1: Failure Is Not Predictive

### The Story of Bob Gibson
- N009 [MUST KNOW] Bob Gibson's disastrous MLB debut (April 15, 1959): gave up a home run to the very first batter he faced (only 65 pitchers in history suffered this ignominy), gave up another HR next inning, hit hard as relief pitcher next evening, gave up a double with 2 outs and 2 runners on two nights later, sat on bench a week, sent back to minors. "Hard to imagine a more demoralizing beginning."
- N010 [MUST KNOW] Despite this catastrophic start, Gibson became one of the top 20 pitchers of all time: 17 seasons, 251 wins, 3,117 strikeouts, 2.91 ERA, 1.12 ERA in 1968 (lowest since 1914), 2 Cy Young awards, 2 World Series MVPs, 9 All-Star teams, first-ballot Hall of Fame. LESSON: Initial failure tells you nothing about ultimate potential.

### If at First You Fail — Michael Marcus
- N011 [MUST KNOW] Michael Marcus's early trading disasters: enticed into futures as a college junior, hired a clueless adviser ("John") for $30/week, lost money on every trade. John's "can't-lose" pork belly spread trade nearly wiped out his account — August pork bellies weren't deliverable against February contract, something neither of them knew.
- N012 [MUST KNOW] Marcus's escalating failures: lost another $500, then cashed in $3,000 life insurance from his father (who died when Marcus was 15). Made some winning grain trades. In 1970, bought corn based on a newsletter — by sheer luck, the year of the corn blight. Turned $3,000 into $30,000.
- N013 [MUST KNOW] The devastating corn blight loss: Spring 1971, Marcus believed blight would return. Borrowed $20,000 from his mother, combined with his $30,000, used entire $50,000 to buy maximum corn and wheat contracts on margin. Market held steady on blight fears but didn't go higher. Then headline: "More Blight on the Floor of the Chicago Board of Trade Than in Midwest Cornfields." Market locked limit down. Marcus stood paralyzed. Lost entire $30,000 plus $12,000 of his mother's $20,000.
- N014 [MUST KNOW] Marcus's resilience quote (referencing Fiddler on the Roof): "I would look up and say, 'Am I really that stupid?' And I seemed to hear a clear answer saying, 'No, you are not stupid. You just have to keep at it.' So I did."
- N015 [MUST KNOW] Marcus's eventual triumph: took a job at Commodities Corporation, initially funded with $30,000, later added $100,000. In ~10 years, turned that into $80 MILLION. The man who lost his dead father's life insurance and his mother's savings became one of the most successful traders in history.

### "One-Lot" Persists — Tony Saliba
- N016 [MUST KNOW] Tony Saliba's devastating start: as a clerk on the floor, staked $50,000 by another trader. Went long volatility spreads (options that gain if market volatility increases). First 2 weeks ran account to $75,000 — thought he was a genius. Didn't realize he was buying options at inflated premiums after a highly volatile period. Market went sideways, volatility and premiums collapsed. In 6 weeks, account down to $15,000.
- N017 [MUST KNOW] Saliba's despair: "I was feeling suicidal. Do you remember the big DC-10 crash at O'Hare in May 1979? ... I would have exchanged places with one of those people in that plane on that day. I felt that bad." The depth of emotional devastation from early trading failure.
- N018 [MUST KNOW] Saliba's recovery: sought advice from experienced brokers who taught discipline, homework, and goal of consistent moderate profitability over get-rich-quick. Switched from volatile Teledyne options to narrow-range Boeing options. When he returned to Teledyne, his conservative order size earned ridicule — nicknamed "One-Lot." He persisted despite the ribbing. Result: a streak of 70 consecutive months with profits exceeding $100,000.

### Two Key Lessons (Chapter 1 Summary)
- N019 [MUST KNOW] Lesson 1: Failure is not predictive. Even great traders often encounter failure — and even REPEATED failures — early in their careers. Failure at the start is the norm, not the exception, even for future Market Wizards. Implication: novice traders should start with small amounts of cash because they might as well pay less for their market education.
- N020 [MUST KNOW] Lesson 2: Persistence is instrumental to success. Most people faced with the types of failures described would have given up. Were it not for relentless persistence, many Market Wizards would never have discovered their ultimate potential.
- N021 [NICE TO KNOW] Footnote on "limit down": In futures markets, daily price changes are restricted by a specified limit. When the equilibrium price lies below the limit-down price, the market "locks" limit down — trading virtually ceases because there are sellers but no willing buyers at the constrained price.

## CHAPTER 2: What Is Not Important

### Core Thesis
- N022 [MUST KNOW] Before considering what IS important to trading success, start with what is NOT important: the specific methodology. Many would-be traders believe success is about finding the one secret formula or system. This belief is misguided — there is no single correct methodology.

### Jim Rogers
- N023 [MUST KNOW] Jim Rogers: phenomenally successful, insists on calling himself an investor (not trader) due to long-term positions. Co-founded Quantum Fund with George Soros in 1973 — one of the most successful hedge funds ever. Left Quantum in 1980 to manage his own money ("retired").
- N024 [MUST KNOW] Rogers's prescient calls: In 1988, predicted gold's bear market would continue another decade (it did — 11 more years). Predicted Japanese stock market would collapse 80-90% — seemed preposterous at the time. A year later, Nikkei peaked and lost ~80% over next 14 years.
- N025 [MUST KNOW] Rogers's derisive attitude toward technical analysis: "I haven't met a rich technician, excluding, of course, technicians who sell their technical services and make a lot of money." When Schwager tried to explain the term "reversal," Rogers cut him off: "Don't tell me. It might mess up my mind. I don't know about those things, and I don't want to know."

### Marty Schwartz
- N026 [MUST KNOW] Marty Schwartz: ran a $40,000 account into over $20 million while never realizing a drawdown of more than 3% (based on month-end data). His two worst months — losses of 3% and 2% — occurred when his children were born and he was distracted. Entered 10 public trading contests: nine 4-month contests averaging 210% return (nonannualized!), one 1-year contest scoring 781% return.
- N027 [MUST KNOW] Schwartz's retort to Rogers: "Absolutely. I always laugh at people who say, 'I've never met a rich technician.' I love that! It is such an arrogant, nonsensical response. I used fundamentals for nine years and got rich as a technician." He had been a securities analyst for nearly a decade before switching to full-time technical analysis trading.

### Reconciling the Divergent Views
- N028 [MUST KNOW] The Rogers/Schwartz dichotomy proves there is no single correct way to trade. Rogers succeeds purely on fundamentals and views TA as snake oil. Schwartz lost money using fundamentals and got rich using TA. Both view each other's methods with complete disdain, yet BOTH have succeeded spectacularly.
- N029 [MUST KNOW] Key quote: "There is no single market secret to discover, no single correct way to trade the markets. Those seeking the one true answer to the markets haven't even gotten as far as asking the right question, let alone getting the right answer."
- N030 [SHOULD KNOW] There are a million ways to make money in the markets — unfortunately all very difficult to find. But there are many, many ways to succeed. Some hold positions for months/years, others for minutes. Market success is finding the methodology that is right for YOU — not finding the "one true" methodology.

## CHAPTER 3: Trading Your Own Personality

### Core Principle
- N031 [MUST KNOW] "Successful traders find a methodology that fits their personality." If you get nothing else from the book, this principle alone makes it worthwhile. Every successful trader Schwager ever interviewed had developed a trading style consistent with their personality and beliefs.
- N032 [MUST KNOW] Schwartz's personal example: spent nearly a decade trying to trade using fundamental analysis — an approach poorly attuned to his personality. It tied his ego to his fundamentally derived opinions. "Although I steadily earned good salaries, I was still almost broke because I consistently lost money in the market." Only became successful when he switched to technical analysis, which let him exit losing trades quickly: "By living the philosophy that my winners are always in front of me, it was not so painful to take a loss."

### Paul Tudor Jones
- N033 [MUST KNOW] Paul Tudor Jones: one of the great futures traders of our time. Interviewed 6 months after Oct 1987 crash — that month he had an incredible 62% return while it was catastrophic for many. Had nearly achieved 5 consecutive years of triple-digit returns (one year was "only" 99%).
- N034 [SHOULD KNOW] Jones's frenetic trading style: scheduled interview during market hours. Kept eyes on quote monitors across the room, intermittently shouting orders to the exchange floor "in a particularly frenetic style, the trading equivalent of a professional tennis player aggressively returning a volley: 'Buy 300 December crude at even! Go, go, go! Are we in? Speak to me!'" Simultaneously taking phone calls and speaking to staff.

### Gil Blake
- N035 [MUST KNOW] Gil Blake: became involved in trading to DISPROVE that markets had patterns — wanted to show a colleague that market timing was a waste of time. Was a CFO. His colleague showed research on switching in/out of municipal bond funds. Blake was skeptical: "Have you ever read A Random Walk Down Wall Street? Get more data." When he got more data, he found decisive nonrandom persistence in fund prices.
- N036 [MUST KNOW] Blake's track record: quit his job, spent months at the local library extracting years of data on ~100 mutual funds from microfilm machines. Took out multiple second mortgages on his home to increase trading stake. 12 years later: averaged 45% return per year, worst year was 24% gain, all positive months except 5 in entire 12 years. Had a streak of 65 consecutive winning months. Traded from the bedroom of his house, turned down money management offers.

### Comparing Jones and Blake
- N037 [MUST KNOW] Jones and Blake represent personality extremes: Can you imagine Jones spending months in a library going through prices on microfilm and trading once a day from his bedroom? Or Blake in Jones's chaotic, shouting-orders-at-the-floor environment? Both succeeded spectacularly because their methodologies suit their personalities. If they had swapped approaches, the results would probably have been very different.

### Colm O'Shea Quote
- N038 [MUST KNOW] Colm O'Shea (global macro manager) on whether trading can be taught: "If I try to teach you what I do, you will fail because you are not me. If you hang around me, you will observe what I do, and you may pick up some good habits. But there are a lot of things you will want to do differently." His friend who sat next to him for years became successful — but "what he learned was not to become me. He became something else. He became him."

### Personality and Trading Systems
- N039 [MUST KNOW] Why most people who buy trading systems lose money: Even if >50% of systems sold would be profitable if applied as instructed, Schwager estimates >90% of buyers would still lose money. Reason: every system hits bad periods. If you buy a system that doesn't reflect your personality/beliefs, you won't understand what drives its signals, won't have confidence to stick with it during drawdowns, will stop trading it during the bad period, and won't be there when it recovers.

## CHAPTER 4: The Need for an Edge

### Money Management Is Not Enough
- N040 [MUST KNOW] Wall Street adage: "Even a poor trading system could make money with good money management." Schwager calls this "one of the stupidest things that has ever been said about trading." Roulette proof: if you have a negative edge, the optimal money management strategy is to bet it ALL at once on a single spin — that gives you the highest probability of being a winner (47.37%). The more you play, the greater the probability of losing. With enough play, it's a mathematical certainty you'll lose.
- N041 [MUST KNOW] Money management cannot save you if you don't have an edge. It is helpful in mitigating losses and preserving capital ONLY if you do have an edge. You need BOTH: an edge (method) AND money management.

### An Edge Is Not Enough
- N042 [MUST KNOW] Monroe Trout (one of the best return/risk records ever): "Make sure you have the edge. Know what your edge is. Have rigid risk control rules... To make money, you need to have an edge and employ good money management. Good money management alone isn't going to increase your edge at all. If your system isn't any good, you're still going to lose money, no matter how effective your money management rules are. But if you have an approach that makes money, then money management can make the difference between success and failure."
- N043 [MUST KNOW] Two self-test questions: (1) "What exactly is your methodology?" — if you can't answer, you're not ready to risk money. (2) "Does your trading method provide an edge?" — if unsure, again not ready. No Market Wizard ever said "I look at the screen, and if bonds look good, I'll buy some." They ALL had a specific methodology they could describe.

## CHAPTER 5: The Importance of Hard Work

### Marty Schwartz's Work Ethic
- N044 [MUST KNOW] Schwager interviewed Schwartz in the evening after a long trading day. Schwartz was visibly tired after a lengthy interview, but wasn't about to call it a day — he still had to complete his daily market analysis routine. Quote: "My attitude is that I always want to be better prepared than someone I'm competing against. The way I prepare myself is by doing my work each night."
- N045 [SHOULD KNOW] Schwager was "amazed to find that so many of the great traders I interviewed were workaholics."

### David Shaw
- N046 [MUST KNOW] David Shaw: founder of D.E. Shaw, one of the most successful quantitative trading firms. Assembled scores of brilliant mathematicians, physicists, and computer scientists. Trading strategy exceedingly complex — trading thousands of financial instruments across all global markets. Also incubated/spun off multiple companies (Juno Online Services, a financial technology company sold to Merrill Lynch, an online brokerage, a market-making operation). Also deeply involved in computational biochemistry, served on President Clinton's Committee of Advisors on Science and Technology. When asked about vacation: "Not much. When I take a vacation, I find I need a few hours of work each day just to keep myself sane."

### John Bender
- N047 [MUST KNOW] John Bender: brilliant options trader, managed money for Soros's Quantum Fund and his own fund. Average annual compounded return of 33% with max drawdown of only 6%. In 2000 (last year of fund), registered 269% return — option trades positioned for a major stock market top proved immensely profitable. Closed fund in 2000 due to brain aneurysm. Spent next decade buying rainforest acreage in Costa Rica. Suffered from bipolar disorder; died in 2010.
- N048 [SHOULD KNOW] Bender's extreme work ethic: most active in Japanese options market, would stay up to trade European options markets, then extend his day into U.S. trading session. Normal for him to spend 20 hours a day trading. Not a recommendation for how to live — but an illustration of the extremes to which some Market Wizards carried hard work.

### The Paradox
- N049 [MUST KNOW] Why so many people are attracted to trading: because it SEEMS like an easy way to make a lot of money. But people who are really successful in trading are tremendously hard workers. The brain surgery analogy: no sane person would read "Techniques of Brain Surgery" over a weekend and walk into a hospital Monday morning ready to operate. Yet many people think they can read "How I Made $1,000,000 in the Stock Market Last Year" and beat professionals on Monday.
- N050 [MUST KNOW] Why this paradox exists: Trading is probably the world's only profession where a rank amateur has a 50-50 chance of being right in the beginning (you can only buy or sell). As a matter of probability, a significant percentage will be right >50% of the time initially — by pure luck. Unlike surgery/violin/any other profession where the untrained beginner's odds of short-term success are zero. This fools people into believing trading is easier than it is.

## CHAPTER 6: Good Trading Should Be Effortless

### Core Paradox
- N051 [MUST KNOW] Apparent contradiction with Chapter 5: "The hard work in trading comes in the PREPARATION. The actual process of trading, however, should be effortless." Marathon runner analogy: the out-of-shape person is doing more hard work running one mile, but the world-class runner is more successful — his hard work came in years of training. When he's performing successfully, the actual process is effortless. Same applies to writers, musicians, and traders.
- N052 [MUST KNOW] If trading is going well, it will seem effortless. If it's not going well, you can't force it right by working harder. During a bad period when nearly every decision seems wrong, trying harder won't help — it will probably only make matters worse. You can work harder at research and diagnosing problems, but you can't work harder at the actual trading itself.

### Zen and the Art of Trading
- N053 [MUST KNOW] An unnamed trader (whose full interview was killed by his business manager) referenced Zen and the Art of Archery: "The essence of the idea is that you have to learn to let the arrow shoot itself... In trading, just as in archery, whenever there is effort, force, straining, struggling, or trying, it's wrong... The perfect trade is one that requires no effort."
- N054 [SHOULD KNOW] The interview backstory: trader had gone into currency hedging business, hired a business manager who read the interview about dreams, Zen, and trading — decided it wasn't conducive to the desired corporate image. "No way," said the business manager. Schwager salvaged just the Zen section as a 2-page anonymous chapter in The New Market Wizards.

## CHAPTER 7: The Worst of Times, the Best of Times

### When Everything Is Going Wrong
- N055 [MUST KNOW] Two recommendations from Market Wizards for handling losing periods:
  1. **Reduce your trading size.** Paul Tudor Jones: "When I am trading poorly, I keep reducing my position size. That way, I will be trading my smallest position size when my trading is worst." Ed Seykota (pioneer in systematic futures trading): "Keep reducing risks during equity drawdowns. That way you will approach your safe money asymptotically and have a gentle financial and emotional touchdown."
  2. **Stop trading entirely.** Michael Marcus: "I think that, in the end, losing begets losing. When you start losing, it touches off negative elements in your psychology; it leads to pessimism... When I have had a bad losing streak, I have been able to say to myself, 'You just can't trade anymore.'" Richard Dennis ($400 stake → ~$200 million): "When you are getting beat to death, get your head out of the mixer."
- N056 [MUST KNOW] Marty Schwartz cuts trading size to 1/5th or even 1/10th of normal after confidence-shaking losses. "After a devastating loss, I always play very small and try to get black ink, black ink... And it works." After a $600,000 hit on Nov 4, 1982, he drastically reduced size, pieced together small gains, finished the month with only a $57,000 loss.
- N057 [MUST KNOW] Randy McKay ($2,000 → tens of millions): even more extreme in reducing size during losing streaks. "I'll keep on reducing my trading size as long as I'm losing. I've gone from trading as many as 3,000 contracts per trade to as few as 10 when I was cold, and then back again." Considered this drastic variation a key element in his trading success.
- N058 [SHOULD KNOW] Best remedy for losing streak is to stop trading entirely. Take a break/vacation, liquidate all positions or protect with stops. Physical break interrupts the downward spiral and loss of confidence. When you return, ease back in starting small, gradually increasing if trading has again become effortless.
- N059 [SHOULD KNOW] Marcus's equity curve advice: plot your equity daily. "If the trend in your equity is down, that is a sign to cut back and reevaluate." Traders may be slow to realize the dimensions of a drawdown — they allow losses to mount and are shocked by the magnitude.

### When Everything Is Great
- N060 [MUST KNOW] Counter-intuitive: winning periods are ALSO times to consider playing smaller. Marty Schwartz reduces trading size after strong profits, just as after bad losses, because "My biggest losses have always followed my largest profits." Worst drawdowns often follow the best performance — because (1) winning streaks lead to complacency which leads to sloppy trading, and (2) periods of excellent performance tend to coincide with particularly high exposure.
- N061 [MUST KNOW] Key warning: "If your portfolio is sailing to new highs almost daily and virtually all your trades are working, watch out! These are the times to guard against complacency and to be extra cautious."

## CHAPTER 8: Risk Management

### Opening
- N062 [MUST KNOW] Paul Tudor Jones's most important advice to the average trader: "Don't focus on making money; focus on protecting what you have." Market Wizards generally agreed that money management (risk control) was MORE IMPORTANT to trading success than the trade selection methodology. You can do quite well with a mediocre entry methodology and good money management, but you're likely to go broke with a superior entry methodology and poor money management. "The amount of attention most beginning traders devote to money management is inversely proportional to its importance."

### Uncle Point and Kovner's Dictum
- N063 [MUST KNOW] Marty Schwartz's "uncle point": "Know your uncle point" — before putting on a position, know the price point at which the pain will be too great and you'll give up. Like kids fighting where one demands the other "say uncle" to surrender. You must know in advance where you'll cry uncle.
- N064 [MUST KNOW] Bruce Kovner (founder of Caxton Associates, 87% average annualized compounded return over 10 years): Before entering ANY position, he predetermined his exit point based on where the market should NOT go if his idea was right. "That is the only way I can sleep. I know where I'm getting out before I get in." Reason: before entering, you have complete objectivity. Once in the trade, you lose objectivity and it becomes easier to rationalize a losing position. By making the exit decision BEFORE entry, you remove emotionalism from money management.
- N065 [MUST KNOW] Schwager's personal application of Kovner's dictum: his pivotal trade that changed him from net loser to net winner was actually a LOSING trade. He went long the deutsche mark within a prolonged trading range, placed a good-till-canceled stop just below the consolidation low. When the market fell, he was stopped out at a small loss — then the decline accelerated sharply. Previously this type of trade would have wiped out his account. "If I were asked to provide what I thought was the most important trading advice and restricted to using only 10 words, my reply would be: Know where you will get out before you get in."

### How Not to Place Your Stops
- N066 [MUST KNOW] Colm O'Shea's stop-placement lesson: As a new trader at Citigroup, he correctly predicted UK interest rates wouldn't rise. Three months later he was proven exactly right — rates hadn't risen and short-term interest rate futures were up 100 points. Yet he LOST money. Why? He had a longer-term idea but traded with short-term risk constraints, getting continually stopped out by insignificant adverse moves because he was too afraid of losing money.
- N067 [MUST KNOW] O'Shea's correct stop-placement methodology: "First, you need to decide where you are wrong. That determines where the stop level should be. Then you work out how much you are willing to lose on the idea. Last, you divide the amount you're willing to lose by the per-contract loss to the stop point, and that determines your position size." The most common error: "People start with position size. Then they know their pain threshold, and that determines where they place their stop." The market doesn't care about your pain threshold — place stops where your trade premise is disproved.

### An Option to Stops
- N068 [SHOULD KNOW] Options as alternative to stops: disadvantage of stops is the market can reverse after triggering your stop, leaving you with a loss on a position that would have been a winner. Example: stock at $24, willing to risk $2 → buy $22 call for $3. If stock falls below $22, max loss is $3 (the premium). But if stock falls below $22 and then rebounds to $30, the option trader profits $5 while the stop trader lost $2. Trade-off: if stop isn't hit, the stop trader is $1/share better off (the extra premium cost). In-the-money options also require much lower cash outlay.

### Risk Management at the Portfolio Level
- N069 [MUST KNOW] BlueCrest (Michael Platt): multimanager fund achieving >12% annual returns (after fees) over 13 years with peak-to-valley drawdown under 5%. How? Each manager allowed to lose only 3% before allocation is cut by 50%. If they lose another 3% on remaining assets, entire allocation withdrawn for the year. Max loss per manager: ~5%. But the 3% risk applies only to STARTING stake — as managers build profit cushion, they can take progressively greater risk. This structure assures capital preservation while keeping upside open-ended.

### Quick Exits When Wrong
- N070 [MUST KNOW] Steve Cohen (SAC Capital): shorted a stock at $169, earnings came out — "a complete blowout!" — got out sharply higher in after-hours trading at $187. Next day stock opened $197. "Thank God I covered that night." When asked if he could always turn on a dime: "You better be able to do that. This is not a perfect game. My best trader makes money only 63% of the time. Most [SAC] traders make money only in the 50 to 55 percent range. That means you're going to be wrong a lot. If that's the case, you better make sure your losses are as small as they can be, and that your winners are bigger."

### The Trader's Dilemma — Partial Liquidation
- N071 [MUST KNOW] Steve Cohen's advice for when a position is going against you but you still believe in the trade: "If the market is moving against you, and you don't know why, take in half. You can always put it on again. If you do that twice, you've taken in three-quarters of your position. Then what's left is no longer a big deal." Partial liquidation is much easier psychologically than full liquidation, and provides a way to ACT rather than freeze. Most traders resist it because it "guarantees you'll be partially wrong" — but by trying to be 100% right, many end up being 100% wrong.
- N072 [MUST KNOW] Michael Marcus on clarity: "When in doubt, get out and get a good night's sleep. I've done that lots of times and the next day everything was clear... While you are in [the position], you can't think. When you get out, then you can think clearly again."

### Underappreciated Reason for Avoiding Large Losses
- N073 [MUST KNOW] Michael Platt (BlueCrest) on the HIDDEN cost of large losses: beyond the direct financial damage, large losses mentally impede the trader and cause missed winning opportunities. "You feel like an idiot, and you're not in the mood to put on anything else. Then the elephant walks past you while your gun's not loaded. It's amazing how annoyingly often that happens. In this game, you want to be there when the great trade comes along. It's the 80/20 rule of life. In trading, 80 percent of your profits come from 20 percent of your ideas."

### It's Not Rocket Science
- N074 [MUST KNOW] Larry Hite (Mint Investment, one of the largest CTAs of the 1980s): "The very first rule we live by at Mint is: Never risk more than 1 percent of total equity on any trade." By only risking 1%, he becomes "indifferent to any individual trade." Simple rule prevents any single bad trade from doing much damage. You may still lose money trading, but you won't be knocked out of the game by one or a few bad trades accumulating without limit.
- N075 [MUST KNOW] Nothing magical about 1% — could be 0.5% or 2% or whatever suits your strategy. The key point: there is a strict loss limit on EVERY trade. Effective money management is not a matter of complexity — it's a matter of discipline. Simple risk control rules will work fine, as long as you have the discipline to follow them.

## CHAPTER 9: Discipline

### The Most Common Trait
- N076 [MUST KNOW] When Schwager asked Market Wizards what differentiated them from the majority of traders, the most common reply was "discipline." But rules are boring and quickly forgotten; stories capture interest. So Schwager tells the story of Randy McKay.

### McKay's Lapse of Discipline
- N077 [MUST KNOW] Randy McKay's background: flunked out of college in 1968 (six Fs, lack of attendance), drafted by Marines during Vietnam, returned 1970. His brother (a CME broker) got him a runner job. In 1972, CME launched the International Monetary Market (IMM) for currency futures, gave free seats to existing members. McKay's brother didn't need his, let Randy use it. Currency pits were so inactive floor traders played chess and read newspapers. McKay found he had a knack — successful from year one, making more each successive year.
- N078 [MUST KNOW] McKay's discipline in action — the 1978 Carter dollar rescue: Dollar had been sliding all year. Over a November weekend, Carter administration announced a dollar support plan. Monday morning: British pound futures opened LOCKED LIMIT DOWN (600-point decline). McKay was heavily long the pound. He immediately liquidated on the interbank market (which traded freely at equilibrium), taking a 1,800-point loss — equivalent to three consecutive limit-down moves in futures.
- N079 [MUST KNOW] McKay's principle: "When I get hurt in the market, I get the hell out. It doesn't matter at all where the market is trading. I just get out, because I believe that once you are hurt in the markets, your decisions are going to be far less objective than they are when you're doing well." The trade cost him $1.5 million — his largest loss at the time. No regrets. "As long as you're in the position, there's tremendous anxiety. Once you get out, you begin to forget about it. If you can't put it out of your mind, you can't trade."
- N080 [MUST KNOW] McKay's catastrophic lapse: 10 years later, approaching his $50 million trading goal. His "next-to-last trade" — a huge long position in the Canadian dollar (2,000 contracts) after it broke through the psychologically critical 80-cent barrier. One Sunday evening before rushing to catch a flight to Jamaica (supervising house construction), McKay checked quotes: Canadian dollar was down exactly 100 points. "The Canadian dollar rarely moves 20 points in the overnight session, let alone 100 points; it must be a bad quote," he rationalized. Late for his flight, he rushed to the airport.
- N081 [MUST KNOW] The quote was real — a poll had shown the liberal candidate (who supported an independent Québec) had closed the gap in the upcoming election. By the time McKay (whose new Jamaica house had no phones yet — pre-mobile era) drove to the nearest hotel and called his floor clerk from a pay phone, his position was down $3 million. He got out only 20% of his position. The Canadian dollar continued plunging. A few days later: down $7 million. "Get me out of everything!" The same man who had shown perfect discipline during the 1978 crisis committed a momentary lapse — rationalizing a bad quote because he was late for a flight — and it cost him $7 million.
- N082 [MUST KNOW] Lesson: "The market will not let traders get away with even a momentary lapse of discipline." Next time you're tempted to ease up on discipline and violate your trading/risk control rules, think of McKay.

## CHAPTER 10: Independence

### Core Principle
- N083 [MUST KNOW] Michael Marcus: "You have to follow your own light... As long as you stick to your own style, you get the good and bad in your own approach. When you try to incorporate someone else's style, you often end up with the worst of both styles."

- N084 [MUST KNOW] Schwager's yen trade: had a strong conviction the yen was going lower (sharp downswing followed by tight consolidation = continuation pattern). ## CHAPTER 12: Losing Is Part of the Game

### The Link between Confidence and Taking Losses
- N091 [MUST KNOW] Linda Raschke: "It never bothered me to lose, because I always knew that I would make it right back." Not arrogance — rather: "I have a methodology that I know is going to win in the long run. Along the way there are going to be some losses. If I lose now, I will win subsequently. As long as I stick with my methodology and keep doing what I am doing, I am going to come out ahead."
- N092 [MUST KNOW] Dr. Van Tharp (research psychologist, Market Wizards): top traders share two critical beliefs: (1) it's okay to lose money in the market, and (2) they knew they had won the game before they started. "If you know you have won the game of trading before you start, then there is no problem taking a loss, because you understand that is just part of the way of getting to the ultimate gain."

### The Rationalization of a Losing Trader
- N093 [MUST KNOW] Marty Schwartz on his transition: "What is the ultimate rationalization of a trader in a losing position? 'I'll get out when I am even.' Why is getting out even so important? Because it protects the ego. I was able to become a winning trader when I was able to say, 'To hell with my ego — making money is more important.'" The irony: amateur traders lose money because they try to avoid losing. Professional traders understand taking losses is an integral part of the process. To win at trading, you need to understand that losing is part of the game.

### The Four Types of Trades
- N094 [MUST KNOW] Most traders think there are two types of trades: winning and losing. Actually there are FOUR: winning trades, losing trades, good trades, and bad trades. Don't confuse winning/losing with good/bad. A GOOD trade can lose money. A BAD trade can make money. A good trade follows a process that will be profitable (at acceptable risk) if repeated multiple times. A bad trade is taken as a gamble.
- N095 [MUST KNOW] Coin flip analogy: heads you pay $100, tails you get $200 (fair coin). You flip, it lands heads — you lose $100. Was it a bad bet? No — it was a good bet that happened to lose. Repeated many times, you'd do very well. Similarly, a winning slot machine wager is still a BAD trade because repeated many times it has a high probability of losing money.

### Willing to Lose
- N096 [MUST KNOW] Bruce Kovner: one of the most important things Michael Marcus taught him was that "you have to be willing to make mistakes regularly; there is nothing wrong with it. Michael taught me about making your best judgment, being wrong, making your next best judgment, being wrong, making your third best judgment, and then doubling your money."

## CHAPTER 13: Patience

### Century-Old Wisdom
- N097 [MUST KNOW] Tom Baldwin (largest individual trader in the Treasury bond pit): "They trade too much. They don't pick their spots selectively enough. When they see the market moving, they want to be in on the action. So, they end up forcing the trade rather than waiting patiently. Patience is an important trait many people don't have."
- N098 [MUST KNOW] Reminiscences of a Stock Operator (1923): "There is the plain fool, who does the wrong thing at all times everywhere, but there is the Wall Street fool, who thinks he must trade all the time." "The desire for constant action irrespective of underlying conditions is responsible for many losses on Wall Street even among the professionals."

### A Master of Patience — Ed Seykota
- N099 [MUST KNOW] Ed Seykota: pioneer of systematic futures trading, one of Marcus's biggest influences. One account starting with $5,000 in 1972 had increased by 250,000% by 1988. Marcus learned patience from Seykota: "One time, he was short silver, and the market kept edging down, a half penny a day, a penny a day. Everyone else seemed bullish. Ed said, 'The trend is down, and I'm going to stay short until the trend changes.' I learned patience from him in the way he followed the trend."
- N100 [MUST KNOW] Seykota didn't even have a quote machine on his desk: "Having a quote machine is like having a slot machine on your desk — you end up feeding it all day long. I get my price data after the close each day." His systems gave signals based on daily data; he didn't want to know about intraday gyrations that could only tempt him to trade more frequently than his methodology dictated. Dangers of watching every tick: overtrading AND prematurely liquidating good positions on insignificant adverse moves.

### The Power of Doing Nothing
- N101 [MUST KNOW] Jim Rogers: "One of the best rules anybody can learn about investing is to do nothing, absolutely nothing, unless there is something to do." When asked if he needs everything to line up before taking a position: "What you just described is a very fast way to the poorhouse. I just wait until there is money lying in the corner, and all I have to do is go over there and pick it up." He waits until a trade is so obvious it's like picking money up off the floor.
- N102 [MUST KNOW] Joel Greenblatt (Gotham Capital: 50% average annual compounded return over 10 years, 1985-1994, worst year +28.5%): invoked Warren Buffett's "no called strikes on Wall Street" — you can watch as many pitches as you want and only swing when everything sets up your way. When students asked about companies with unpredictable earnings: "Skip that company and find one you can analyze."
- N103 [MUST KNOW] Kevin Daly (Hedge Fund Market Wizards): long/short equity manager who launched late 1999 (terrible timing for a predominantly long manager). Yet achieved 872% cumulative gross return over 11 years while Russell 2000 was up 68% and S&P 500 was DOWN 9%. Secret: discipline to remain largely in cash during negative market environments, sidestepping large drawdowns during two major bear markets. Largest peak-to-valley drawdown: only 10% (vs. S&P losing nearly half its value twice). The trades he DIDN'T take made all the difference.
- N104 [MUST KNOW] Mark Weinstein's cheetah analogy: "I also don't lose much on my trades because I wait for the exact right moment... Although the cheetah is the fastest animal in the world, it will wait until it is absolutely sure it can catch its prey. It may hide in the bush for a week, waiting for just the right moment. It will wait for a baby antelope, and not just any baby antelope, but preferably one that is sick and lame. Only then, when there is no chance it can lose its prey, does it attack. That, to me, is the epitome of professional trading."
- N105 [SHOULD KNOW] William Eckhardt (trained the Turtles with Richard Dennis) on why patience is so hard: "When behavioral psychologists have compared the relative addictiveness of various reinforcement schedules, they found that intermittent reinforcement — positive and negative dispensed randomly — is the most addictive alternative of all, more addictive than positive reinforcement only." Trading is inherently addictive because of this random reward/punishment structure.

### The Wisdom of Sitting
- N106 [MUST KNOW] Patience applies equally to getting OUT of trades. From Reminiscences: "It never was my thinking that made the big money for me. It always was my sitting. Got that? My sitting tight! ... Men who can both be right and sit tight are uncommon."
- N107 [MUST KNOW] William Eckhardt debunked "You can't go broke taking a profit" — calls it one of the most wrongheaded trading adages. "That's precisely how many traders DO go broke. While amateurs go broke by taking large losses, professionals go broke by taking small profits." Human nature seeks to maximize the CHANCE of gain rather than the gain itself. The desire to maximize winning percentage encourages premature liquidation of good trades, severely reducing total gain. "The success rate of trades is the least important performance statistic and may even be inversely related to performance."
- N108 [MUST KNOW] Marcus: "If you don't stay with your winners, you are not going to be able to pay for the losers." Patience is critical BOTH for entering AND exiting trades.

## CHAPTER 14: No Loyalty

### Core Principle
- N109 [MUST KNOW] Loyalty is a terrible trait for a trader. Loyalty to an opinion or position can be disastrous. The absence of loyalty is FLEXIBILITY — the ability to completely change your opinion when warranted. Marcus: "I am very open-minded. I am willing to take in information that is difficult to accept emotionally... When a market moves counter to my expectations, I have always been able to say, 'I had hoped to make a lot of money in this position, but it isn't working, so I'm getting out.'"

### O'Shea Reverses in 2009
- N110 [MUST KNOW] Colm O'Shea in April 2009: still very pessimistic post-financial-crisis, positioned bearishly. "But the market was telling me I was wrong." So I can't stick with the whole-world-is-terrible thesis." He abandoned his bearish view and reversed — saved what could have been a disastrous year as equities and commodities embarked on a multiyear rally.
- N111 [SHOULD KNOW] O'Shea on George Soros as flexibility paragon: "has the least regret of anyone I have ever met... He has no emotional attachment to an idea. When a trade is wrong, he will just cut it, move on, and do something else." Soros once made $250 million on a huge FX position in one day, was quoted in the press about it, then when the market reversed — "the position just disappeared. It was gone."

### Jones Reverses Course
- N112 [MUST KNOW] Schwager interviewed Paul Tudor Jones on two visits two weeks apart. First visit: very bearish, heavily short S&P 500. Second visit: "This market is sold out" — had not only covered his short but gone long, based on the market's failure to follow through on the downside. A 180-degree shift in two weeks. The market moved sharply higher in the ensuing weeks.
- N113 [MUST KNOW] "Good traders liquidate their positions when they believe they are wrong; GREAT traders REVERSE their positions when they believe they are wrong."

### Caught by a Surprise — Michael Platt
- N114 [MUST KNOW] Michael Platt held a massive long position in European interest rate futures when the ECB unexpectedly hiked rates. He was on a flight from London to South Africa, completely unaware. Upon landing, his assistant called — down $70-80 million. His instinct: "When I am wrong, the only instinct I have is to get out. If I was thinking one way, and now I can see that it was a real mistake, then I am probably not the only person in shock, so I'd better be the first one to sell. I don't care what the price is."

### Surviving the Worst Trading Blunder Ever — Druckenmiller
- N115 [MUST KNOW] Stanley Druckenmiller (Duquesne Capital, ~30% average annual return over 25 years): On Friday Oct 16, 1987, came into the day net short. By afternoon, believed the market had fallen enough to reach support — covered his short AND went heavily long, switching to 130% long (leveraged). Over the weekend, realized his massive error. Monday morning the market opened enormously lower. What did he do? Covered his entire long position in the first hour — AND went net short again. Think about that: he reversed a large position, then reversed it AGAIN the next trading day after the market had moved tremendously against the previously reversed position. Despite this being arguably the worst trading blunder imaginable, October 1987 showed up as only a moderate loss on his track record.

### A Bad Idea Transformed — Jamie Mai
- N116 [MUST KNOW] Jamie Mai (Cornwall Capital, profiled in The Big Short): In 2011, started researching long dry bulk shippers (China transitioning from coal exporter to importer → increased freight demand, stocks at depressed valuations). But deeper research revealed a massive shipbuilding boom had created fleet capacity growing at 20% annually — even optimistic Chinese demand projections couldn't absorb the surplus. So Mai did the REVERSE trade: went short via out-of-the-money puts. What started as a long idea became his highest-conviction short of the year.

### Don't Publicize Your Market Calls
- N117 [MUST KNOW] Don't announce your predictions publicly — if you do, you become emotionally invested in being right. You'll be more reluctant to change your view when price action and facts contradict your forecast. You'll find reasons why you might still be right rather than acknowledging you're wrong. Paul Tudor Jones: "I avoid letting my trading opinions be influenced by comments I may have made on the record about a market."
- N118 [MUST KNOW] Ed Seykota's silver trap: early in his career, told friends he expected silver to keep going up. When silver fell, he kept ignoring market signs, telling himself it was just a temporary correction. "I couldn't afford to be wrong." His subconscious saved him — he kept dreaming of a big silver aircraft going down, headed for a crash. Eventually he got the message, dumped his long position, and even went short. The dreams stopped.

## CHAPTER 15: Size Matters

### The Power of Bet Size — Edward Thorp
- N119 [MUST KNOW] Edward Thorp: Princeton Newport Partners achieved 19.1% annualized gross (15.1% after fees) over 19 years; 227 of 230 months were winning months, worst monthly loss under 1%. Before finance, he devised winning strategies for casino games — built a miniature computer with Claude Shannon to predict roulette outcomes. In blackjack, his insight: by betting more on high-probability hands and less (or nothing) on low-probability hands, you could transform a negative-edge game into a positive-edge one.
- N120 [MUST KNOW] Trading application: varying position size can improve performance. Trade larger on higher-confidence trades and smaller on lower-confidence ones. If degree of confidence serves as a proxy for probability of winning, allocate more risk to high-confidence trades.
- N121 [MUST KNOW] Michael Marcus used this principle explicitly: recognized he did much better when fundamentals, chart pattern, AND market tone all aligned. Admitted he "enjoyed the game too much" to only take such trades, but "the thing that saved me was that when a trade met all my criteria, I would enter five to six times the position size I was doing on the other trades."

### The Danger of Size — Paul Tudor Jones's Cotton Trade
- N122 [MUST KNOW] Jones's most devastating early trade: managing speculative cotton accounts, built a 400-contract long position. When July cotton broke below a trading range then rebounded, Jones thought stops had been taken out and rallied back — in an act of bravado, bid higher for 100 more contracts. The firm with deliverable cotton stocks instantly yelled "Sold!" — Jones knew he was on the wrong side. Instructed his broker to sell as much as possible. Market plunged, locked limit down within 60 seconds. He could only liquidate less than half. Next day: locked limit down again. Finally exited selling some contracts 400 points below where he knew he wanted to be out. His accounts lost 60-70% on a SINGLE trade.
- N123 [MUST KNOW] The cotton trade transformed Jones: "I was totally demoralized. I said, 'I am not cut out for this business; I don't think I can hack it much longer.' ... It was at that point that I said, 'Mr. Stupid, why risk everything on one trade? Why not make your life a pursuit of happiness rather than pain?'" His focus permanently shifted to what he could LOSE, not what he could make.
- N124 [MUST KNOW] Kovner on overtrading: "Undertrade, undertrade, undertrade... Whatever you think your position ought to be, cut it at least in half. My experience with novice traders is that they trade three to five times too big. They are taking 5 to 10 percent risks on a trade when they should be taking 1 to 2 percent risks." He trained ~30 traders; only 5 became good. Key difference: successful ones sized positions correctly. "A greedy trader always blows up."
- N125 [MUST KNOW] Steve Clark: you have to trade within your "emotional capacity." If the position is so large that you're prone to getting out on meaningless corrections, you'll lose money on trades that would have been winners. "One sure way of knowing your position is too large is if you wake up worrying about it."
- N126 [SHOULD KNOW] Howard Seidler (Turtle trader): took a short position, market moved his way, so he doubled the position. Small adverse move → so concerned he liquidated both the add-on AND original position. Two days later the market collapsed as he'd originally anticipated. "You can't win if you're trading at a leverage size that makes you fearful of the market."
- N127 [SHOULD KNOW] Schwartz: "Most people make the mistake of increasing their bets as soon as they start making money. That is a quick way to get wiped out." Wait until you've at least doubled your capital before trading larger.

### Stepping on the Accelerator — Soros and Druckenmiller
- N128 [MUST KNOW] Druckenmiller on what he learned from Soros: "It's not whether you're right or wrong that's important, but how much money you make when you're right and how much you lose when you're wrong." Soros only criticized him when he was right on the market but didn't "maximize the opportunity."
- N129 [MUST KNOW] The $1 billion exchange: Druckenmiller was short dollar vs deutsche mark, position working in his favor, feeling proud. Soros: "How big a position do you have?" "One billion dollars." "You call that a position?" Soros encouraged him to double it. The trade went dramatically further in their favor. Lesson: "when you have tremendous conviction on a trade, you have to go for the jugular. It takes courage to be a pig."
- N130 [MUST KNOW] Plaza Accord 1985: Soros was heavily long the yen. After the agreement to depreciate the dollar, yen opened 800 points higher Monday morning. Other traders at Soros Management started taking profits. Soros came bolting out, telling them to stop selling and that he would assume their positions. "The government had just told him that the dollar was going to go down for the next year, so why shouldn't he be a pig and buy more?" CAVEAT: the lesson is NOT to always trade large — it's to be willing to trade larger WHEN you have very high conviction.

### Volatility and Trading Size
- N131 [MUST KNOW] Position sizing must adjust for changing volatility. In 2008, O'Shea encountered managers who said they'd "cut their risk in half." He'd respond: "Yes, my leverage was four, and it is now two." Then: "Do you realize volatility has gone up five times?" They'd cut leverage by half but volatility had quintupled — their volatility-adjusted risk had actually INCREASED.

### Correlation and Trading Size
- N132 [MUST KNOW] Positions are not independent coin tosses — they can be significantly correlated. When positions are positively correlated, losses tend to occur simultaneously, increasing portfolio risk. Position sizing should be reduced when different positions are positively correlated. Edward Thorp: "We traded a correlation matrix that was used to reduce exposures in correlated markets. If two markets were highly correlated and the technical system went long one and short the other, that was great. But if it wanted to go long both or short both, we would take a smaller position in each."

## CHAPTER 16: Doing the Uncomfortable Thing

### The Outperforming Monkey
- N133 [MUST KNOW] Eckhardt's thesis: natural human tendency to seek comfort leads to WORSE THAN RANDOM decisions in trading. Not just as bad as the dart-throwing monkey — the monkey will do BETTER than most human traders. "What feels good is often the wrong thing to do." Richard Dennis: "If it feels good, don't do it."
- N134 [MUST KNOW] Three examples of "the call of the countertrend": (1) Buying on weakness/selling on strength feels good (you're smarter than everyone who bought higher) but is usually a losing strategy. (2) Cashing in small profits feels good but impedes large gains. (3) Holding losing positions hoping to get back to even feels comforting but leads to disaster. In all cases, the action that feels good is usually wrong.
- N135 [MUST KNOW] Empirical proof: one of Richard Dennis's employees entered a charting contest that required predicting year-end prices for multiple markets. He simply used current prices for all his predictions. He finished in the TOP FIVE among hundreds of contestants. At least 95% (probably 99%) of all entrants' predictions were WORSE THAN RANDOM — because human biases systematically distorted their forecasts.

### The Inadvertent Experiment — Greenblatt's Magic Formula
- N136 [MUST KNOW] Joel Greenblatt created a "Magic Formula" stock-ranking indicator and set up a website where investors could pick stocks from the pre-screened list. Also offered a managed option (equal-dollar amounts in all top-ranked stocks, no selection/timing). Less than 10% chose to manage their own selections. After two years: the managed portfolios outperformed the self-managed portfolios by 25% — even though BOTH were selected from the SAME list of stocks.
- N137 [MUST KNOW] Greenblatt's explanation: self-managing investors "took their exposure down when the market fell. They tended to sell when individual stocks or their portfolio underperformed. They did much worse than random in selecting stocks from our prescreened list, probably because by avoiding the stocks that were particularly painful to own, they missed some of the biggest winners." Human comfort-seeking destroyed all the edge.

### Behavioral Economics and Trading
- N138 [MUST KNOW] Eckhardt: "There is a persistent overall tendency for equity to flow from the many to the few. In the long run, the majority loses. The implication for the trader is that to win you have to act like the minority. If you bring normal human habits and tendencies to trading, you'll gravitate toward the majority and invariably lose."
- N139 [MUST KNOW] Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory experiment: Choice A: sure $3,000 vs. 80% chance of $4,000 — vast majority chose the sure $3,000 (even though expected value of B = $3,200). Choice B: sure loss of $3,000 vs. 80% chance of losing $4,000 — vast majority GAMBLED (even though expected loss of gambling = $3,200). People are risk-averse with gains but risk-seeking with losses. This is exactly why people let losses run and cut profits short. "Let your profits run and cut your losses short" is the exact opposite of what most people naturally do.

### Why Emotions Affect Even Computerized Trading
- N140 [MUST KNOW] Even systematic/computerized trading is not immune to emotional comfort-seeking. When backtesting, people discover drawdown periods and instinctively add rules to smooth them out. Each iteration makes the simulated equity curve smoother. But the more a system is optimized to fit past prices, the LESS likely it is to work in the future — because impressive simulated results are achieved with hindsight knowledge of prices that won't repeat. The human instinct for comfort (smoothing drawdowns) leads to over-optimization even in algorithmic trading.

### Conclusion (Chapter 16)
- N141 [MUST KNOW] Most people lose money in trading not only because they lack skill, but because their inclination to make comfortable choices will lead to worse-than-random results. Awareness of this inherent human handicap is the first step in resisting the temptation to make trading decisions that feel good but are wrong on balance.

## CHAPTER 17: Emotions and Trading

### Alex Honnold Analogy
- N142 [MUST KNOW] Alex Honnold (free solo climber, first to free-solo Half Dome's 2,000-foot northwest face): "There is no adrenaline rush... If I get a rush, it means that something has gone horribly wrong... The whole thing should be pretty slow and controlled." Same applies to expert trading — the Hollywood image of adrenaline-filled, high-risk trading makes for good visuals but has nothing to do with successful trading.

### Expensive Excitement
- N143 [MUST KNOW] Larry Hite's friend (who had gone broke trading futures) asked: "How can you trade the way you do? Isn't it boring?" Hite: "I don't trade for excitement; I trade to win." Charles Faulkner (trading coach) had a client with a successful system who couldn't follow it. Faulkner taught him emotional detachment techniques — they worked, the trader was profitable. Then one day, up $7,000 in the first few hours, the trader said in a monotone: "This is boring." He eventually blew up. "He knew how to go into an emotionally detached state, but he didn't like to be there." The markets are an expensive place to look for excitement.

### You Can't Win If You Have to Win
- N144 [MUST KNOW] Druckenmiller's desperate trade: In 1981, his only income source (Drysdale Securities consulting contract, $10,000/month) abruptly went away. His $7 million fund generated $70,000/year in fees against $180,000 overhead, with only $50,000 cash on hand. Absolutely convinced interest rates had peaked, he took the firm's entire $50,000 and margined a highly leveraged long position in T-bill futures. In four days, he lost everything — then one week later, rates hit their cycle high and never came close again. He had timed a major bottom within a week — and still lost all his money. The emotionalism of a Hail Mary attempt doomed a trade that was analytically correct. "The market will seldom reward the carelessness of trades born of desperation."

### Impulsive Trades
- N145 [MUST KNOW] Kovner's "going-bust trade" (1977 soybeans): had a brilliantly conceived long July/short November soybean spread — old crop supplies tightening, spread soaring via limit-up moves. At new highs, his broker called excitedly: "You are a fool to stay short the November contracts. Let me lift your November shorts." Kovner agreed — a spur-of-the-moment decision he called "a moment of insanity." Fifteen minutes later the broker called back: "the market is limit down! I don't know if I can get you out." Between lifting the protective short and liquidating the long, Kovner's account equity was HALVED. "What bothered me so much was the realization that I had lost a process of rationality that I thought I had."
- N146 [MUST KNOW] Marcus's soybean regret: long soybeans during the great 1973 bull market, saw prices triple their previous record highs. As the rally developed, he impulsively took profits on his entire position — "trying to be fancy instead of staying with the trend." Ed Seykota, at the same firm, stayed with his position. Soybeans then went limit up for 12 consecutive days. Marcus dreaded going to work each day knowing soybeans would be bid limit up again while he was out and Seykota was still in.
- N147 [MUST KNOW] Schwartz on impulsive recovery attempts: "Whenever you are hit, you are very upset emotionally. Most traders try to make it back immediately; they try to play bigger. Whenever you try to get all your losses back at once, you are most often doomed to fail." Schwager: probably no class of trades has a higher failure rate than impulsive trades.

### Don't Confuse Intuition with Impulse
- N148 [MUST KNOW] Impulsive trades ≠ intuitive trades. Impulsive trades are almost invariably bad. Intuitive trades can be high-probability for experienced traders. Intuition is simply subconscious experience — a subconscious recognition of similar past situations. "The trick is to differentiate between what you WANT to happen and what you KNOW will happen."
- N149 [SHOULD KNOW] Emotional influences that cloud objectivity: (1) A long trader will dismiss bearish evidence. (2) A trader who procrastinated on a buy will ignore signs the market is moving higher (entering now would confirm the earlier mistake). (3) A trader on the record with a forecast will be reluctant to accept contradictory evidence. The subconscious mind is not inhibited by these constraints — which is why intuition can be more objective than conscious analysis.

## CHAPTER 18: Dynamic versus Static Trading

### The Need to Adapt
- N150 [MUST KNOW] Colm O'Shea: "I use risk guidelines, but I don't believe in rules that way. Traders who are successful over the long run adapt. If they do use rules, and you meet them 10 years later, they will have broken those rules. Why? Because the world has changed. Rules are only applicable to a market at a specific time. Traders who fail may have great rules that work, but then stop working. They stick to the rules because the rules USED to work, and they are quite annoyed that they are losing even though they are still doing what they used to do. They don't realize that the world has moved on without them."
- N151 [MUST KNOW] Edward Thorp's adaptation: pioneered statistical arbitrage ("MUD" strategy — Most Up, most Down). Initial version: market-neutral. When returns deteriorated, he adapted to also be sector-neutral. When that version weakened, he switched to neutralizing various mathematically defined factors. Each time the original version would have lost its edge, but by continually adapting, he maintained superior return/risk performance.

### Scaling versus Single-Price Entry and Exit
- N152 [MUST KNOW] You don't have to enter or exit a position all at once. Most traders pick a single entry/exit price — but scaling in and out is often better. Scale-down buying: buy a partial position now, then seek to add on pullbacks. Ensures you have at least a partial position if the market keeps going, without assuming full risk after a significant advance. Also reduces average entry price, mitigating the chances of abandoning a good long-term trade due to an initial adverse move.
- N153 [MUST KNOW] Scale-out selling works analogously: if you get out entirely and the advance continues, you miss a large portion of the move. If you hold on entirely and the market reverses, you give back a large portion of gains. Scaling out ensures you still have a partial position if the move continues while taking partial profits. Bill Lipschutz (former head of global FX trading at Salomon Brothers): scaling out "has enabled me to stay with long-term winners much longer than I've seen most traders stay with their positions."
- N154 [SHOULD KNOW] Scaling avoids the temptation of wanting to be completely right: "By shunning all-or-nothing decisions and instead scaling in and scaling out of positions, you will never get the best outcome, but you will never get the worst one, either."

### Trading around Positions — Jimmy Balodimas
- N155 [MUST KNOW] Jimmy Balodimas (First New York Securities): "breaks all the rules" — sells into sharp rallies, buys into plunges, adds to losers, cuts winners. Normally financial suicide, but one element of his style is valuable: he ALWAYS takes some money off the table when the market is in his favor. "That saves me a lot of money, because when the market rallies, I have a smaller position." This dynamic approach — reducing size on favorable moves, rebuilding on corrections — can generate profit even when on the wrong side of the trend.
- N156 [MUST KNOW] Dynamic trading in practice: reduce position size on profitable moves, rebuild on subsequent corrections. Any time a position was lightened and the market retraces to the reentry point, a profit is generated that otherwise wouldn't exist. A trade can even show a loss from original entry to final exit yet still be profitable through trading around the position. Additional benefit: reducing exposure on favorable moves lessens the chance of being knocked out of a good trade on a correction.

## CHAPTER 19: Market Response

### Core Principle
- N157 [MUST KNOW] Marty Schwartz (learned from friend Bob Zoellner): "When the market gets good news and goes down, it means the market is very weak; when it gets bad news and goes up, it means the market is healthy." A counter-to-anticipated response to news may be more meaningful than the news itself. Many Market Wizards echoed this theme.

### Gold and the First Iraq War
- N158 [MUST KNOW] Randy McKay's approach to fundamentals: "I don't think, 'Supply is too large and the market is going down.' Rather, I watch how the market responds to fundamental information." Example: eve of the Gulf War (Jan 1991), gold was just below $400. When U.S. planes started bombing, gold rallied to $410 in Asian markets — then retreated to $390, BELOW where it was before the supposedly bullish news. McKay saw gold's failure to hold its war-rally gains as very bearish. Gold opened sharply lower the next morning and continued declining for months.

### McKay Gets Interested in Stocks
- N159 [SHOULD KNOW] In 1982, McKay (a futures trader who had never traded stocks) became so convinced the stock market was going higher that he opened a stock account. Why? "The market was going up almost every day without any particular supporting news. In fact, the news was actually quite negative: Inflation, interest rates, and unemployment were all still very high." The market's ability to advance despite bearish fundamentals was the crucial clue.

### Dalio Is Surprised
- N160 [MUST KNOW] Ray Dalio, two formative experiences: (1) August 1971, Nixon took US off the gold standard — Dalio expected it to be bearish, but the market rallied. (2) 1982, with unemployment at 11%, Mexico defaulted on its debt. Dalio expected disaster for stocks — instead, the default marked the exact bottom and the beginning of an 18-year bull market. Dalio's lesson: "In both the abandonment of the gold standard in 1971 and the Mexico default in 1982, I learned that a crisis development that leads to central banks easing and coming to the rescue can swamp the impact of the crisis itself."
- N161 [MUST KNOW] Why markets respond counterintuitively to news: (1) Markets often anticipate and discount impending events before they occur — so the event's occurrence REMOVES the concern, leading to a counter-to-expected price response. (2) Significant bearish events can trigger bullish responses (central bank intervention). Understanding this is crucial — investors are often baffled by counterintuitive market reactions.

### A Most Bullish Report — Marcus's Soybean Story
- N162 [MUST KNOW] Marcus: "Always ask yourself, 'How many people are left to act on this particular idea?' You have to consider whether the market has already discounted your idea." Marcus was long soybeans in the late 1970s, weekly export reports kept driving prices higher. Then the most bullish export report yet — everyone expected limit up for three consecutive days. The market opened limit up but then prices eased off the limit. Marcus thought: "Soybeans were supposed to be limit up for three days, and they can't even hold limit up for the first morning." He immediately called his broker with sell orders, ended up going significantly net short — and bought back at much lower prices. "It was the only time I made a lot of money on an error."
- N163 [SHOULD KNOW] Schwager's analogous experience: during the largest cotton bull market of the 20th century (prices near $1/pound, highest since the Civil War), the weekly export report showed sales of a half million bales to China — by far the most bullish report ever. Instead of locking limit up (200 points), cotton opened only 150 points higher and started trading down. That opening proved to be the exact market top — a high not seen again for well over 30 years.

### Druckenmiller and the Deutsche Mark
- N164 [MUST KNOW] After the Berlin Wall fell, Druckenmiller was long deutsche marks (expansionary fiscal + tight monetary policy = bullish). At the start of the first Iraq war, he was still heavily long — a bad position to hold. But rather than waiting to get hurt, he read the market's response to news: a story that Hussein would capitulate should have caused the dollar to sell off sharply vs. the deutsche mark, but it declined only slightly. "I smelled a rat." He sold $3.5 billion worth of deutsche marks in one day, largely avoiding the ensuing losses.

### The Invincible Position — Platt's Yield Curve Trade
- N165 [SHOULD KNOW] Michael Platt (2009): placed a large trade to benefit from a widening yield curve. Each time detrimental news came out, he thought he'd get crushed — but nothing happened. After this repeated several times, he concluded the yield curve "just couldn't get any flatter no matter what news came out." He quadrupled his position. The yield curve went from 25 points to 210 points — his biggest winning trade of the year.

### The Submerged Volleyball — Scott Ramsey
- N166 [MUST KNOW] Scott Ramsey (Denali Asset Management, 15% avg annual compounded return, 11% vol, 13-year track record): compares market resilience to a volleyball being pushed underwater. "That is your crisis event. Then you let go — the event dissipates — and the ball goes popping out of the water." Markets that are strongest during a crisis are the markets that will lead when the pressure lifts.

### Buy the Strongest, Sell the Weakest
- N167 [MUST KNOW] Ramsey: relative strength during a crisis predicts leadership afterward. "Measuring which markets were the strongest during a crisis can tell you which markets are likely to be the leaders when the pressure is off." He always wants to be long the strongest and short the weakest. Marcus agreed: "You absolutely want to put down a bet when a market acts terribly relative to everything else. When the news is wonderful and a market can't go up, then you want to be sure you are short."
- N168 [SHOULD KNOW] Example: during an inflationary period in the 1970s, all commodities were limit up on one extreme day — except cotton, which opened limit up but then sold off. Marcus: "That was the market peak." Cotton never recovered. Most novice traders buy laggards thinking they have the best potential; Marcus and Ramsey say do the opposite.

### Correlation as a Clue
- N169 [SHOULD KNOW] Scott Ramsey: when previously correlated markets break from their pattern, it provides a trading clue. In September 2011, post-2008 correlations between commodities and equities (the "risk-on/risk-off" pattern) broke down: equities rebounded to the top of a two-month range but copper was near its low for the year. Ramsey took this as a sign commodities were vulnerable — a decline subsequently occurred.

## CHAPTER 20: The Value of Mistakes

### Ray Dalio's Philosophy
- N170 [MUST KNOW] Ray Dalio "loves" mistakes because they provide learning experiences that lead to improvement. This philosophy permeates Bridgewater's corporate culture. Dalio quote: "I learned that there is an incredible beauty in mistakes because embedded in each mistake is a puzzle and a gem that I could get if I solved it... While most others seem to believe that mistakes are bad things, I believe mistakes are good things because I believe that the most learning comes via making mistakes and reflecting on them."
- N171 [SHOULD KNOW] Dalio's Principles — a 111-page document, required reading for all Bridgewater employees. Sample rules: "Create a culture in which it is okay to fail but unacceptable not to identify, analyze, and learn from mistakes." "If you don't mind being wrong on the way to being right, you will learn a lot."
- N172 [MUST KNOW] Marty Schwartz: "Most people, in most careers, are busy trying to cover up their mistakes. As a trader, you are forced to confront your mistakes because the numbers don't lie."

### Analyzing Your Trades
- N173 [MUST KNOW] Steve Clark: dissect your P&L to see what is working and what isn't. Traders often don't know where their profits are coming from. "Do more of what works and less of what doesn't." Sounds obvious, but surprisingly few traders follow this simple rule.
- N174 [MUST KNOW] Ray Dalio: "Beginning around 1980, I developed a discipline that whenever I put on a trade, I would write down the reasons on a pad. When I liquidated the trade, I would look at what actually happened and compare it with my reasoning and expectations when I put on the trade."

### The Trader's Log
- N175 [MUST KNOW] Randy McKay attributed his early success to rigorously analyzing every trade: "Every day, I made copies of my cards and reviewed them at home. Every trader is going to have tons of winners and losers. You need to determine why the winners are winners and the losers are losers. Once you can figure that out, you can become more selective in your trading and avoid those trades that are more likely to be losers."
- N176 [MUST KNOW] Each mistake, if recognized and acted upon, provides an opportunity for improving a trading approach. Most traders would benefit by writing down each mistake, the implied lesson, and the intended change. A trading log can be periodically reviewed for reinforcement. "Trading mistakes cannot be avoided, but repeating the same mistakes can be, and doing so is often the difference between success and failure."

## CHAPTER 21: Implementation versus Idea

### A Post-Bubble Trade — O'Shea
- N177 [MUST KNOW] How a trade is IMPLEMENTED can be more important than the trade idea itself. Colm O'Shea viewed the NASDAQ in 1999-2000 as a bubble. After the March 2000 break, he was sure equities would surrender their gains. But he NEVER considered going short equities. Why? "While the price rise during a bubble can be quite smooth, the price decline after a bubble bursts is typically interspersed by treacherous bear market rallies." Instead, he reasoned: NASDAQ bust → economy slows → interest rates decline → go long bonds. Both trends materialized — stocks declined erratically (with a +40% bear market rally in summer 2000 that would have stopped out any short), while the bond rise was relatively smooth.
- N178 [MUST KNOW] Key insight: "The trade was highly successful, not because the underlying premise was correct, which it was, but rather because of the way the trade was implemented." If O'Shea had shorted stocks, he would have been correct but most likely would have lost money being stopped out during the massive bear market rally.

### A Better Option — Greenblatt's Wells Fargo LEAPS
- N179 [MUST KNOW] Joel Greenblatt's Wells Fargo trade (early 1990s): Wells Fargo had an excellent fee-generating business but was under pressure from California commercial real estate concentration during a deep recession. Binary outcome: if Wells Fargo went bust → stock down $80; if it survived → stock up $80. A 1:1 risk/reward on the stock. But by buying LEAPS (options with 2+ years to expiration), Greenblatt transformed it into 1:5 risk/reward — if the bank survived, he'd make 5x his money; if it failed, he'd only lose the option premium. "The odds were much better than 50-50 that the bank would survive, so the stock was a buy. But in terms of risk/reward, the options were an even better buy." The stock more than doubled before the options expired.

## CHAPTER 22: Off the Hook

### A Unique Observation — Schwartz's Dictum
- N180 [MUST KNOW] Marty Schwartz: "If you're ever very nervous about a position overnight, and especially over a weekend, and you're able to get out at a much better price than you thought possible when the market trades, you're usually better off staying with the position." If the market lets you off the hook easily, DON'T get out.
- N181 [MUST KNOW] Why this works: if you're really worried about a position (especially over a weekend), it's because something dramatic has happened — bearish news for a long, or a breakout against a short. Whatever the news or development, you're hardly the only one who knows about it. Everyone will be aware. If, despite developments that suggest the market should move strongly against you, it instead barely moves against you or goes your way — it implies very strong hands are positioned in the same direction as you. The lesson: if the market lets you off the hook easily, don't get out.

### On the Hook — Bill Lipschutz
- N182 [MUST KNOW] Bill Lipschutz (Salomon Brothers, fall 1988): short $3 billion of dollars vs. the deutsche mark. On a Friday afternoon, Gorbachev announced Soviet troop reductions at the UN → market interpreted this as bullish for the dollar (less defense spending → deficit reduction). Dollar strengthened. Lipschutz was trapped — couldn't liquidate in thin Friday afternoon liquidity. Tried to push the dollar down by selling another $300 million DM. The market absorbed his order "like a sponge" — not even a hint of weakness. He knew he was in deep trouble. Down $70-90 million.
- N183 [MUST KNOW] Tokyo opened Sunday night with the dollar actually moving LOWER — letting Lipschutz off the hook. Instead of covering (as most traders would have done, relieved), Lipschutz abandoned his plan to cover half on the Tokyo open. He waited. The dollar kept sliding. He eventually covered his entire position in the European session with an $18 million loss — after having been down nearly five times that amount. "The reason I didn't get out on the Tokyo opening was that it was the wrong trading decision." He applied Schwartz's principle: the market was letting him off the hook, so he didn't take the exit.

### Schwartz Saves Me Money
- N184 [SHOULD KNOW] Schwager's personal application: in 2011, was heavily short NASDAQ when a devastating unemployment report came out (so bearish that commentators couldn't find any silver lining). Market sold off sharply, then reversed and erased 75% of losses by the close — a strong weekly close near multiyear highs. Schwager expected to be forced to cover. But Sunday night, the market traded down 15 points in the first 10 minutes. Recalling Schwartz's dictum (the market was letting him off the hook), he covered only a token 10%. Monday: the market was much lower and continued declining sharply. Following Schwartz's advice saved him a lot of money.

## CHAPTER 23: Love of the Endeavor

### The Common Thread
- N185 [MUST KNOW] Market Wizards all describe trading in game-like terms: Kovner: "Market analysis is like a tremendous multidimensional chess board." Rogers: "one big, three-dimensional puzzle... The picture is always changing." David Ryan: "a giant treasure hunt." Steve Clark: "I thought I was playing a video game, and I couldn't believe I was getting paid to do it." Monroe Trout: "I like to trade... Now I get to play a very fun game, and I'm paid handsomely for it."
- N186 [MUST KNOW] Colm O'Shea on what determines who will succeed: "Frankly, if you don't love it, there are much better things to do with your life... No one who trades for the money is going to be any good. If successful traders were only motivated by the money, they would just stop after five years and enjoy the material things." Trading is something they love to do — an endeavor pursued for the fun of the challenge, not for getting rich. Love of trading may not guarantee success, but its absence will likely lead to failure.
