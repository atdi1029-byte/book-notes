If You Only Remember 10 Things

  1. There is no single correct way to trade. Find the methodology that fits YOUR personality, not the “one true” methodology.
  2. Before entering any trade, know exactly where you will get out if you are wrong. Decide on the exit before the entry.
  3. Risk management is more important than the entry methodology. You can succeed with a mediocre system and good risk management, but you will eventually blow up with a great system and poor risk management.
  4. When you are in a losing streak, reduce your position size or stop trading entirely. Never try to trade your way out of a drawdown by increasing size.
  5. A good trade can lose money and a bad trade can make money. Judge trades by process, not outcome.
  6. Never risk more than 1–2% of equity on any single trade. This simple rule will keep you in the game.
  7. The human instinct to seek comfort leads to worse-than-random trading decisions. What feels good is usually the wrong thing to do.
  8. When the market gets good news and goes down, it is very weak. When it gets bad news and goes up, it is healthy. Market response matters more than the news itself.
  9. Patience applies to both entries and exits: wait for the obvious setup, and once in a winner, sit tight. Most profits come from a small number of trades.
  10. Love of trading is the one trait that every Market Wizard shares. If you trade only for money, you will not succeed.

Foreword & Preface

Peter Brandt, a trader who has lived off trading profits since 1981, writes the foreword. He reads the Market Wizards series every Christmas break alongside The Bourne Trilogy—Bourne for entertainment, Market Wizards for mental preparation for the coming year of market combat. Brandt compares the series to Edwin Lefèvre’s Reminiscences of a Stock Operator, predicting it will remain just as timely eighty years from now. His core belief, distilled from decades of trading and reinforced by every reading of these interviews: consistently profitable traders share exactly two things—an approach that reflects their unique personality, and aggressive risk management. Everything else is secondary. Brandt dismisses the trading system marketers who promise seventy to eighty percent win rates, noting that most of them are not successful traders themselves. The secret to profitable trading is not in the entry signals.

In the preface, Schwager frames the book as a distillation of twenty-five years of interviews with fifty-nine of the world’s best traders across four Market Wizards books spanning a quarter century. He is clear about what the book is not: it is not a how-to manual, not a collection of formulas or recommendations. Too many aspiring traders seek a formulaic treatment while missing concepts essential to success regardless of methodology. Different readers consistently cite different interviews as their personal favorites, suggesting that the lessons are filtered through individual personality and experience.

Perhaps the most surprising insight in the preface is Schwager’s claim that the principles of trading success apply broadly to success in any endeavor. He recalls giving a talk on trading success when a minister in the audience approached him afterward: “I am a minister, and I was fascinated by how many of the points you made were also critical to my success in building a congregation.” Schwager suspects these are common principles of success that he simply discovered through the lens of trading.

Chapter 1: Failure Is Not Predictive

Schwager opens with a story that has nothing to do with markets. On April 15, 1959, Bob Gibson played his first major league game as a relief pitcher for the Cardinals. He gave up a home run to the very first batter he faced—an ignominy suffered by only 65 pitchers in the entire history of baseball. In the next inning, he gave up another home run. The following evening he was hit hard again. Two nights later he gave up a double with two outs and runners on. He was benched for a week and sent back to the minors.

Despite this catastrophic beginning, Gibson became one of the twenty greatest pitchers of all time: seventeen seasons, 251 wins, 3,117 strikeouts, a 2.91 career ERA, an unbelievable 1.12 ERA in 1968 (the lowest since 1914), two Cy Young awards, two World Series MVPs, nine All-Star selections, and first-ballot Hall of Fame induction. The lesson is simple and powerful: initial failure tells you nothing about ultimate potential.

The same pattern holds in trading. Michael Marcus was enticed into futures as a college junior. He hired an adviser named John for thirty dollars a week who lost money on every trade. John’s brilliant idea—a “can’t-lose” pork belly spread—nearly wiped out Marcus’s account because August pork bellies were not deliverable against the February contract, something neither of them knew. Marcus scraped together another five hundred dollars and lost that too. Then he cashed in the three thousand dollars of life insurance left by his father, who had died when Marcus was fifteen.

By sheer luck, 1970 was the year of the corn blight. Marcus turned three thousand into thirty thousand on a newsletter recommendation. Emboldened, he borrowed twenty thousand from his mother, combined it with his profits, and used the entire fifty thousand dollars to buy the maximum number of corn and wheat contracts on margin. The blight theory proved wrong. The corn market locked limit down and Marcus stood paralyzed, losing his entire thirty thousand plus twelve thousand of his mother’s money. His response was to keep going.

I would look up and say, “Am I really that stupid?” And I seemed to hear a clear answer saying, “No, you are not stupid. You just have to keep at it.” So I did. — Michael Marcus

Marcus eventually took a job at Commodities Corporation, which funded his account with thirty thousand dollars and later added another hundred thousand. In roughly ten years, he turned those modest allocations into eighty million dollars—with the firm withdrawing as much as thirty percent of his profits in many years to cover expenses.

Tony Saliba provides perhaps the most emotionally devastating version of this pattern. Staked with fifty thousand dollars as a clerk on the options floor, Saliba went long volatility spreads and ran the account to seventy-five thousand in two weeks. He thought he was a genius. What he did not realize was that he was buying options at inflated premiums following a period of extreme volatility. When the market went sideways, volatility collapsed and his account dropped to fifteen thousand. Saliba described the despair in terms that went beyond financial loss: he would have traded places with the victims of the 1979 DC-10 crash at O’Hare. But he persisted, sought advice from experienced brokers who taught him discipline and conservative position sizing, and eventually put together a streak of seventy consecutive months with profits exceeding one hundred thousand dollars.

Saliba’s recovery method is as instructive as his failure. The experienced brokers who mentored him taught him to abandon the get-rich-quick mindset and pursue consistent moderate profitability instead. He switched from trading the wildly volatile Teledyne options to the narrow-range Boeing options, where the swings were manageable and the lessons came cheaper. When he eventually returned to Teledyne, his conservative order sizes earned him a nickname from the other floor traders: “One-Lot.” They mocked his tiny positions. He persisted despite the ribbing, building his skill and confidence one small trade at a time until the streak of seventy consecutive profitable months made the mockery irrelevant.

Schwager draws two lessons. First, failure is not predictive—even repeated failure at the start is the norm for future Market Wizards. Second, persistence is instrumental. Most people facing losses like Marcus’s or Saliba’s would have quit. Were it not for relentless persistence, many of these traders would never have discovered their ultimate potential. The practical implication is that novice traders should start with small amounts of capital, since they are essentially paying for their market education.

Chapter 2: What Is Not Important

Before considering what is important to trading success, Schwager starts with what is not: the specific methodology. Many aspiring traders believe success lies in discovering some secret formula or system that explains and predicts price moves. This belief is fundamentally misguided. There is no single correct methodology. Schwager proves this by contrasting two traders with diametrically opposed approaches.

Jim Rogers co-founded the Quantum Fund with George Soros in 1973, building one of the most successful hedge funds in history. Rogers is a pure fundamentalist with a genius for anticipating major macro trends. In 1988, with gold declining for eight years, Rogers predicted the bear market would continue for another decade—it lasted eleven more years. He predicted the Japanese stock market would collapse eighty to ninety percent, a forecast that seemed preposterous at the time but proved almost exactly right as the Nikkei lost roughly eighty percent over the next fourteen years. When Schwager asked Rogers about technical analysis, his contempt was absolute: “I haven’t met a rich technician.” When Schwager tried to explain the term “reversal,” Rogers cut him off: “Don’t tell me. It might mess up my mind.”

Marty Schwartz, on the other hand, ran a forty-thousand-dollar account into over twenty million dollars while never realizing a drawdown of more than three percent on a monthly basis. He entered ten public trading contests and won nine of the four-month contests with an average nonannualized return of 210 percent. In his single one-year contest, he scored 781 percent. Schwartz had been a securities analyst for nearly a decade before switching to full-time technical analysis trading. His response to Rogers was withering: “Absolutely. I always laugh at people who say, ‘I’ve never met a rich technician.’ I love that! It is such an arrogant, nonsensical response. I used fundamentals for nine years and got rich as a technician.”

There is no single market secret to discover, no single correct way to trade the markets. Those seeking the one true answer to the markets haven’t even gotten as far as asking the right question, let alone getting the right answer. — Jack Schwager

Both Rogers and Schwartz succeeded spectacularly, and both view each other’s methods with complete disdain. The dichotomy proves that market success is not about finding the “one true” methodology. Rogers bases his trading decisions solely on fundamental analysis and considers technical analysis to be on the same plane as snake oil. Schwartz consistently lost money using fundamental analysis but achieved incredible performance using technical analysis. Yet both men have generated extraordinary wealth from the markets.

The reconciliation is that there are a million ways to make money in the markets—unfortunately, they are all very difficult to find. But there are many, many ways to succeed. Some traders succeed using only fundamental analysis, others using only technical analysis, and still others using a combination of the two. Some traders hold positions for months or even years, while others operate on a time scale measured in minutes. The critical distinction is not which methodology you choose but whether it is the right methodology for you.

Schwager emphasizes that the question itself is wrong. Aspiring traders who ask “What is the best trading methodology?” have not even gotten as far as asking the right question, let alone getting the right answer. The correct question is: “What is the best trading methodology for me?” And the answer will be different for every individual, depending on their personality, skills, beliefs, time horizon, and tolerance for risk.

Schwager notes that there are a million ways to make money in the markets—unfortunately, all of them are very difficult to find. The word “many” should not be confused with “easy.” The existence of multiple viable approaches does not mean that any random approach will work. Each successful methodology represents years of development, testing, and refinement by a trader whose personality aligned with that specific way of reading and responding to markets. The diversity of successful approaches is encouraging, but the difficulty of developing any single one of them is sobering.

This chapter’s message is liberating for those who have spent years searching for the one correct approach. The search is futile because no such thing exists. But it is also demanding, because it places the burden squarely on the individual trader to discover—through experimentation, self-knowledge, and honest self-assessment—which approach fits them. That process of discovery is itself part of becoming a successful trader.

Chapter 3: Trading Your Own Personality

If there is no single correct methodology, then the essential task is finding one that fits your personality. Schwager considers this the single most important principle in the book. Every successful trader he has ever interviewed developed a trading style that was consistent with their personality and beliefs. The problem is that many traders spend years forcing themselves into approaches that do not suit them. Schwartz spent nearly a decade trying to trade using fundamental analysis, an approach poorly attuned to his personality because it tied his ego to his market opinions. He was almost broke despite earning good salaries. Only when he switched to technical analysis, which allowed him to exit losing trades quickly and move on, did he succeed.

Schwager illustrates the personality principle by contrasting Paul Tudor Jones and Gil Blake. Jones is one of the great futures traders of our time—he earned a 62 percent return during the October 1987 crash and nearly achieved five consecutive years of triple-digit returns. When Schwager arrived to interview him during market hours, Jones was shouting orders into speakerphones connected to exchange floors while simultaneously answering phone calls and fielding questions from staff. The scene was pure frenetic energy. Blake, by contrast, became involved in trading to disprove that markets had patterns. He was a CFO. After discovering decisive nonrandom persistence in mutual fund prices, he quit his job, spent months at the local library extracting data from microfilm machines, and took out multiple second mortgages to increase his trading stake. He averaged forty-five percent annual returns over twelve years, with only five negative months out of 144, and traded from his bedroom. Despite his enormous success, Blake had no desire to create a money management business or grow beyond a one-man operation. He turned down offers to manage outside money, with the exception of a few friends and family accounts.

Schwartz’s personal journey is particularly instructive because it shows how long a trader can persist with the wrong methodology. For nearly a decade, he tried to adapt fundamental analysis to trading markets. The approach was poorly suited to his personality because it led him to tie his ego to his fundamentally derived market opinions. Speaking of this early period, Schwartz said: “Although I steadily earned good salaries, I was still almost broke because I consistently lost money in the market.” It was not until he immersed himself in technical analysis that he became successful. Technical analysis gave him a methodology where getting out of a losing trade quickly was natural: “By living the philosophy that my winners are always in front of me, it was not so painful to take a loss. If I make a mistake, so what?” The point is not that technical analysis is superior to fundamental analysis, but that it was the better methodology for Schwartz.

You would be surprised by the number of people who waste time and money trying to fit their personality into a trading method that is not suited for them. There are traders with innate skills in creating computerized trading systems who then feel a compulsion to intervene with discretionary trades, often sabotaging their own systems. There are traders naturally attuned to ascertaining long-term market trends who get bored staying with a position for a long time and then make short-term trades that lose money.

Colm O’Shea, one of the global macro managers Schwager interviewed, expressed this concept lucidly: “If I try to teach you what I do, you will fail because you are not me. If you hang around me, you will observe what I do, and you may pick up some good habits. But there are a lot of things you will want to do differently.” O’Shea described a close friend who sat next to him for several years, learned from him, and went on to manage a large sum at another hedge fund very successfully. But what the friend learned was not to become O’Shea —he became something else. He became himself.

Can you imagine Jones spending months in a library, painstakingly going through prices on microfilm and trading once a day from his bedroom? Or Blake in Jones’s chaotic, shouting-orders-at-the-floor environment? There is something jarring about these images. They just do not fit. Both men have succeeded spectacularly because their methodologies suit their personalities. Had they swapped approaches, the results would probably have been very different.

The contrast between Jones and Blake could not be more stark. Can you imagine Jones spending months in a library painstakingly extracting prices from microfilm and then trading once a day from his bedroom? Or Blake on the floor of a futures exchange, shouting orders at brokers while simultaneously fielding phone calls and monitoring half a dozen quote screens? The images are jarring because each man’s approach is an extension of who he is. Had they swapped methodologies, the results would almost certainly have been very different—not because either approach is inherently inferior, but because personality and methodology must be matched for the system to survive the inevitable drawdowns.

This principle also explains why most people who buy trading systems lose money. Schwager estimates that even if more than fifty percent of systems sold to the public were profitable when applied as instructed, over ninety percent of buyers would still lose money. The reason is that every trading system, regardless of the strategy, will hit periods when it does poorly. If you buy a system that has nothing to do with your personality or beliefs, you will not understand what drives its signals. Consequently, the first time the system hits a bad period, you will not have the confidence to stay with it, and you will stop trading it. You will not be there when the system recovers. The system must grow from your own understanding.

Chapter 4: The Need for an Edge

There is a Wall Street adage that says a poor trading system can make money with good money management. Schwager calls this one of the stupidest things ever said about trading. To prove the point, he uses roulette: if you have a negative edge, the optimal money management strategy is to bet everything on a single spin. That gives you the highest probability of winning (47.37 percent on a double-zero wheel). The more you play, the more certain your loss becomes. Money management cannot create an edge where none exists.

The roulette proof is worth sitting with. If you asked a hundred mathematicians for the optimal strategy to bet a thousand dollars in roulette, all one hundred would give the same answer: place the entire thousand on a single spin of red or black, and then win or lose, walk away. That gives you the highest probability of being a winner—47.37 percent, to be exact, for a wheel with a double zero. The more times you play, the greater the probability that you will lose. Play long enough and it is a mathematical certainty. So the optimal money management strategy for a negative-edge game is the epitome of bad money management: bet it all at once. Money management is helpful in mitigating losses and preserving capital only if you actually have an edge.

But the reverse is also true: an edge without money management will eventually blow up. Monroe Trout, who achieved one of the best long-term return/risk records ever recorded, summarized it perfectly: “Make sure you have the edge. Know what your edge is. Have rigid risk control rules. To make money, you need to have an edge and employ good money management. Good money management alone isn’t going to increase your edge at all. If your system isn’t any good, you’re still going to lose money, no matter how effective your money management rules are. But if you have an approach that makes money, then money management can make the difference between success and failure.”

No trader Schwager ever interviewed for any of the Market Wizards books gave a response like “I look at the screen, and if bonds look good, I’ll buy some.” None of them approached trading with a cavalier, shoot-from-the-hip attitude. They all had a specific methodology. Some could describe their approach in very specific, almost step-by-step terms. Others described their approach in more general terms. But it was clear they all had a specific methodology.

Schwager poses two self-test questions that every trader must be able to answer before risking money in the markets. First: what exactly is your methodology? If you cannot answer that question clearly, you are not ready to be risking money. Second: does your trading method provide an edge? If you are unsure about the answer, again, you are not ready. Successful traders are confident that their methodology provides an edge. That confidence does not come from hope or optimism —it comes from experience, testing, and deep understanding of why the approach works.

The BlueCrest model illustrates how edge and money management can be institutionalized at the portfolio level. Each of Platt’s managers was allowed to lose only three percent of their starting allocation before their capital was cut by half. If they lost another three percent on the remaining assets, the entire allocation was withdrawn for the year. Maximum loss per manager: approximately five percent. But the three-percent risk limit applied only to starting capital— as managers built a profit cushion, they could take progressively greater risk. This asymmetric structure assured capital preservation while keeping the upside open-ended. The result was more than twelve percent annual returns after fees over thirteen years with a peak-to-valley drawdown under five percent.

Trout’s distinction is worth restating because it is so frequently confused. Money management does not improve your edge—it cannot make a losing strategy profitable. What it does is allow a winning strategy to compound over time without being wiped out by an outsized loss. The edge tells you what to trade and when to trade it. Money management tells you how much to trade. Both are necessary conditions for long-term success, and neither can substitute for the other. A trader with a genuine edge and no risk controls is a time bomb. A trader with impeccable risk controls and no edge is a slow bleed.

The chapter’s central insight is that you need both an edge and money management. They are the two legs of the trading stool. An edge without money management is a trading account waiting to blow up. Money management without an edge is slow-motion losing. You need both, and the combination is what separates the Market Wizards from the vast majority of market participants who lose money over time.

Chapter 5: The Importance of Hard Work

Schwager interviewed Marty Schwartz in the evening after a long trading day. Schwartz was visibly tired after a lengthy interview, but he was not about to call it a day—he still had to complete his daily market analysis routine. “My attitude is that I always want to be better prepared than someone I’m competing against. The way I prepare myself is by doing my work each night.” Schwager was amazed to find that so many of the great traders were workaholics.

David Shaw assembled scores of brilliant mathematicians, physicists, and computer scientists at D.E. Shaw to develop multiple computer models that in combination could extract consistent profits from the markets by exploiting pricing discrepancies among different securities. The entire trading strategy was exceedingly complex, trading thousands of financial instruments including equities, warrants, options, and convertible bonds on all the major global markets. You would think that heading up this massive trading operation and directing and supervising ongoing research of a large team of brilliant quantitative scientists would be more than enough work for any individual. But for David Shaw, apparently, it was not enough.

Over the years, Shaw’s firm incubated and spun off a number of other companies, including Juno Online Services, a financial technology company sold to Merrill Lynch, an online brokerage firm, and a market-making operation. In addition, Shaw became heavily involved in computational biochemistry, keeping current on developing research and providing venture capital to several firms in the field. He eventually turned over the management of D.E. Shaw to a management team so that he could devote full time to biochemistry research. On top of all that, Shaw served on President Clinton’s Committee of Advisors on Science and Technology. When Schwager asked about vacations: “Not much. When I take a vacation, I find I need a few hours of work each day just to keep myself sane.”

John Bender was a brilliant options trader who managed money for George Soros’s Quantum Fund and also traded his own fund. By 1999, his fund had achieved an average annual compounded return of 33 percent with a maximum drawdown of only 6 percent. In 2000, the last year of his fund, he registered an astounding 269 percent return as his option trades positioned in anticipation of a major stock market top proved immensely profitable. While Bender was trading, he was probably most active in the Japanese options market. He would then stay up to trade the European options markets and typically extend his day into the U.S. trading session. It would be normal for Bender to spend as much as twenty hours a day trading. Schwager mentions this not as a recommendation for how to live, but as an illustration of the extremes to which some Market Wizards carried hard work.

Here is the irony. Why are so many people attracted to trading? Because it seems like an easy way to make a lot of money. But the people who are really successful in trading are tremendously hard workers. This dichotomy between perception and reality leads to what Schwager calls The Paradox. No sane person would walk into a bookstore, buy a book titled Techniques of Brain Surgery, study it over the weekend, and walk into a hospital operating room Monday morning believing he was ready to perform brain surgery. Yet many people think there is absolutely nothing unusual about buying a book called How I Made $1,000,000 in the Stock Market Last Year, reading it over the weekend, and then Monday morning believing they can beat the market professionals at their own game.

The line of reasoning in both examples is quite similar, but while it is obvious that the thinking in the brain surgery scenario is deranged, many people see nothing odd about the trading version. The explanation is that trading is probably the world’s only profession in which a rank amateur has a fifty- fifty chance of being right at the beginning. There are only two things you can do in trading: buy or sell. As a matter of probability, a significant percentage of beginners will be right more than fifty percent of the time simply by chance. If you have 1,000 people toss ten coins each, nearly 30 percent will toss sixty percent or more heads. In the coin toss experiment, those people will realize their results are pure luck. But in trading, the amateurs who happen to be right more than fifty percent of the time will attribute their success to superior decision-making rather than chance. This fools people into believing that trading is a lot easier than it is.

The same misperception cannot happen in any other profession. If you have never trained as a surgeon, the odds of performing a successful surgery are zero. If you have never played the violin, the odds of performing a successful solo with the New York Philharmonic are zero. It is just a quirk of trading that you could be successful for the short term without knowing anything, and that possibility fools people.

Bender’s story adds a poignant dimension to the work ethic theme. He closed his fund in 2000 after suffering a brain aneurysm, spent the next decade buying rainforest acreage in Costa Rica, and died in 2010. He had also struggled with bipolar disorder throughout his life. Schwager includes this detail not as a recommendation but as an honest acknowledgment that the extremes of hard work these traders exhibited came at a real cost. Twenty-hour trading days across Japanese, European, and American markets are not sustainable indefinitely, and Bender’s life illustrates the difference between admiring the dedication of Market Wizards and attempting to replicate it without regard for personal limits.

Chapter 6: Good Trading Should Be Effortless

This chapter seems to contradict the previous one, but the distinction is crucial: the hard work comes in the preparation, while the actual process of trading should be effortless. Schwager uses the analogy of a marathon runner. An out-of-shape person is doing more hard work struggling through a single mile than a world-class runner gliding effortlessly at sub-five-minute pace. But the elite runner trained for years to reach that point. When he is performing at his best, the execution feels natural. Writers achieve their best work when the writing comes effortlessly; musicians perform best when their playing comes effortlessly. The same applies to trading.

If trading is going well, it will seem effortless. If it is not going well, you cannot force it by working harder at the trading itself. You can work harder at research and diagnosis, but not at execution. An unnamed trader whose interview was killed by his business manager captured this perfectly through the lens of Zen and the Art of Archery: “The essence of the idea is that you have to learn to let the arrow shoot itself. In trading, just as in archery, whenever there is effort, force, straining, struggling, or trying, it’s wrong. The perfect trade is one that requires no effort.”

The backstory of this quote is itself remarkable. People often wonder how Schwager gets traders to agree to be interviewed. He assures them they will review the finished chapter before it goes to the publisher, and that he will not use the chapter without their approval. This promise helps traders be open and candid. But it can also backfire. For one interview in The New Market Wizards, Schwager did a fairly eclectic interview covering topics like dreams and trading, precognition and trading, and Zen and trading. He spent weeks honing the two-hundred-page raw transcript into a polished chapter. Then the trader called to say he was starting a new business and his newly hired business manager had decided the material about dreams and Zen was not conducive to the desired corporate image. The interview was killed.

Schwager salvaged a single section—the Zen passage—as a two-page anonymous chapter. The trader asked Schwager if he had ever read Zen and the Art of Archery. He had not. The trader explained that in archery, as in trading, the essence is learning to let the arrow shoot itself. Whenever there is effort, force, straining, or trying, something is wrong. The perfection lies in the absence of effort.

There is no contradiction between this chapter and the previous one. The hard work in trading comes in the preparation. The actual process of trading, however, should be effortless. If you are in a particularly bad trading period, when nearly every decision you make seems to be wrong, trying harder will not help. It will probably only make matters worse. You can work harder in doing more research. You can work harder in trying to figure out what is going wrong. But you cannot work harder at the actual trading. If you are out of sync with the markets, trying harder is often likely to make matters even worse. So if trying harder is not the solution for handling a losing streak, what is? That is the question addressed in the next chapter.

The distinction between preparation and execution is one of the most practically useful ideas in the book. It tells you where to direct your energy when things go wrong. Double down on research, analysis, and self-examination. But do not try to force trades or override your methodology with frantic activity. The former is productive; the latter is destructive.

If you are a trader, you will recognize the truth in the Zen trader’s words. The best trading days feel almost boring—you follow your plan, the trades work, and there is no drama. The worst days feel intense and effortful, full of second-guessing and forced decisions. The chapter is essentially telling you that if trading feels hard in the moment, something about your approach or your state of mind needs to change.

If trying harder is not the solution for handling a losing streak, what is? Schwager uses this question as the bridge to the next chapter. But the answer is already implicit in the Zen metaphor. When the archer cannot release the arrow naturally, she does not grip harder—she steps back from the target, returns to fundamentals, and practices until the motion becomes unconscious again. For a trader, this means stepping away from the screen, reviewing your methodology, and returning only when the preparation is complete enough that execution can flow without strain. The hard work is always in the preparation; the performance is where the preparation pays off or reveals its gaps.

Chapter 7: The Worst of Times, the Best of Times

Even great traders experience demoralizing losing periods. The Market Wizards offered two consistent recommendations for handling them. The first is to reduce your trading size. Paul Tudor Jones: “When I am trading poorly, I keep reducing my position size. That way, I will be trading my smallest position size when my trading is worst.” Ed Seykota advised keeping “reducing risks during equity drawdowns. That way you will approach your safe money asymptotically and have a gentle financial and emotional touchdown.” Schwartz would cut his trading size to one-fifth or even one-tenth of normal after a confidence-shaking loss. Randy McKay went even further, scaling from three thousand contracts down to ten contracts when he was cold.

The second recommendation is to stop trading entirely. Marcus: “Losing begets losing. When you start losing, it touches off negative elements in your psychology; it leads to pessimism.” Richard Dennis, who turned a four-hundred-dollar stake into roughly two hundred million: “When you are getting beat to death, get your head out of the mixer.” Take a break. Liquidate all positions. A physical break interrupts the downward spiral of lost confidence. When you return, start small.

Schwartz’s recovery after a devastating loss illustrates the power of this approach in practice. After losing $600,000 on November 4, 1982, he drastically reduced his trading size and focused on piecing together small gains. By the end of the month, his total loss was only $57,000—meaning he had recovered more than ninety percent of the damage by trading tiny positions consistently. The discipline of going small after a hit, rather than trying to make it all back at once, preserved both his capital and his psychological equilibrium.

McKay was even more extreme in his willingness to shrink. He would go from trading as many as three thousand contracts per trade down to as few as ten when he was cold—then scale back up when the winning returned. Most traders find this kind of dramatic reduction humiliating. After trading thousands of contracts, placing an order for ten feels like an admission of defeat. But McKay considered this drastic variation a key element of his trading success. The small positions served a dual purpose: they limited financial damage during the losing period, and they provided a low-risk laboratory for rebuilding confidence and regaining the feel of winning.

The best remedy for an extended losing streak, according to several Market Wizards, is to stop trading entirely and take a physical break—a vacation, a few days away from the screens. Liquidate all positions or protect them with stops. The physical separation interrupts the downward spiral of loss breeding more loss. When you return, ease back in by starting with positions that are a fraction of your normal size. Gradually increase only as trading has once again become effortless. Forcing a comeback never works; allowing it to happen naturally almost always does.

Counter-intuitively, winning periods are also times to play smaller. Schwartz reduces size after particularly strong runs because “my biggest losses have always followed my largest profits.” The mechanism is twofold. First, winning streaks breed complacency, and complacency leads to sloppy trading —skipping the nightly analysis, loosening stop discipline, taking trades that do not fully meet your criteria. Second, periods of excellent performance tend to coincide with particularly high exposure, so when the inevitable reversal comes, the magnitude of the hit is amplified.

If your portfolio is sailing to new highs almost daily and virtually every trade is working, that is not the time to celebrate—it is the moment to guard against overconfidence and be extra cautious. Schwager notes that this advice is perhaps the hardest to follow in the entire book because it requires you to pull back precisely when everything feels best. Human nature screams to press the advantage, to increase size, to ride the hot streak. But the data from dozens of Market Wizards is unambiguous: the worst drawdowns tend to follow the best performance.

Marcus offered a practical tool for catching losing periods early: plot your equity curve daily. Traders are often slow to recognize the dimensions of a drawdown because the losses accumulate gradually. A trader who loses a little here and a little there over several weeks may be shocked to discover that the cumulative damage is far worse than he realized. Plotting equity daily makes the trend visible before it becomes catastrophic. “If the trend in your equity is down,” Marcus said, “that is a sign to cut back and reevaluate.” Treat your equity curve with the same analytical rigor you apply to the price charts of the markets you trade.

Chapter 8: Risk Management

Jones offered his most important advice to the average trader in six words: “Don’t focus on making money; focus on protecting what you have.” The Market Wizards generally agreed that money management was more important to trading success than the entry methodology. You can do quite well with a mediocre entry approach and good money management, but you are likely to go broke with a superior methodology and poor risk control. Schwager observes that the amount of attention beginning traders devote to money management is inversely proportional to its importance.

Schwartz provided the most vivid framework: “Know your uncle point.” Before putting on any position, know the price at which the pain becomes too great and you will give up—like a kid in a fight who cries “uncle.” Bruce Kovner, who averaged 87 percent annualized compounded returns over ten years at Caxton Associates, took this further: before entering any position, he predetermined his exit point based on where the market should not go if his idea was right. “I know where I’m getting out before I get in.” Before the trade, you have complete objectivity. Once in the trade, you lose it. By deciding on the exit in advance, you remove emotionalism from the process.

Schwager himself credits Kovner’s dictum as the pivotal principle that turned him from a net losing trader into a net winning one. His best trade ever was actually a losing trade: he went long the deutsche mark within a trading range, placed a stop just below the consolidation low, and was stopped out at a small loss when the market fell. The decline then accelerated sharply. Previously, that type of trade would have wiped out his account.

Colm O’Shea taught the critical lesson of proper stop placement. As a new trader at Citigroup, O’Shea correctly predicted UK interest rates would not rise. Three months later he was proven exactly right—yet he had lost money because his stops were too tight. The correct method: first decide where you are wrong (that determines the stop level), then decide how much you are willing to lose, then divide that amount by the per-contract loss to the stop to determine position size. Most traders do it backwards—they start with position size, know their pain threshold, and let that determine the stop. The market does not care about your pain threshold.

Steve Cohen’s SAC Capital data revealed that even his best trader made money only 63 percent of the time. Most traders at SAC were in the 50 to 55 percent range. “You’re going to be wrong a lot. Make sure your losses are as small as they can be, and that your winners are bigger.” Cohen also advocated partial liquidation when uncertain: cut half, and if the market keeps going against you, cut half again. After two rounds you have taken off three-quarters of the position, and what remains is no longer a big deal.

Larry Hite of Mint Investment summarized the simplicity of effective risk management: “Never risk more than 1 percent of total equity on any trade.” There is nothing magical about one percent—it could be half a percent or two percent. The key is having a strict loss limit on every trade and the discipline to follow it. Simple rules work fine.

Michael Platt of BlueCrest identified an underappreciated reason for avoiding large losses that goes beyond the direct financial damage. A large loss mentally impedes the trader and causes missed winning opportunities. “You feel like an idiot, and you’re not in the mood to put on anything else. Then the elephant walks past you while your gun’s not loaded. It’s amazing how annoyingly often that happens.” In trading, eighty percent of profits come from twenty percent of ideas. If a large prior loss has put you on the sidelines or destroyed your confidence at the moment one of those twenty-percent ideas appears, the indirect cost of the loss far exceeds the direct financial hit.

Schwager also notes that options can serve as an alternative to stops. One disadvantage of a stop is that the market can reverse after triggering it, leaving you with a loss on a trade that would have been a winner. For example, if you want to buy a stock at twenty-four dollars with a maximum risk of two dollars, you could instead buy a twenty-two-dollar call option for three dollars. If the stock falls below twenty-two, your maximum loss is three dollars regardless of how far it drops. But if the stock falls to twenty-one, triggers what would have been your stop, and then rallies to thirty, the option trader earns five dollars per share while the stop trader is sitting with a two-dollar loss. In-the-money options also require significantly less capital than outright positions.

Chapter 9: Discipline

When Schwager asked Market Wizards what differentiated them from ordinary traders, the most common answer was discipline. But rules are boring and quickly forgotten. Stories stick. So Schwager tells the story of Randy McKay, who flunked out of college (six Fs from lack of attendance), was drafted by the Marines during Vietnam, returned in 1970, and stumbled into trading when his brother gave him a free seat on the newly launched International Monetary Market. The currency pits were so inactive that floor traders played chess. McKay found he had a knack for trading and was successful from the start.

McKay demonstrated extraordinary discipline during the November 1978 Carter dollar rescue plan. He was heavily long the British pound when the surprise announcement caused pound futures to open locked limit down. Rather than wait for futures to trade freely, McKay immediately liquidated on the interbank market at an 1,800-point loss—equivalent to three consecutive limit-down moves. It cost him $1.5 million. He had no regrets: “When I get hurt in the market, I get the hell out. It doesn’t matter at all where the market is trading. Once you are hurt, your decisions are going to be far less objective.”

Ten years later, approaching his fifty-million-dollar trading goal, McKay built a two-thousand-contract long position in the Canadian dollar after it broke through the critical eighty-cent level. One Sunday evening, rushing to catch a flight to Jamaica to supervise house construction, he checked the quote screen at the airport: the Canadian dollar was down exactly one hundred points. He was late for his flight. The Canadian dollar rarely moved twenty points overnight, let alone a hundred. It had to be a bad quote, McKay rationalized. He rushed to the airport.

The quote was real. A poll had shown the separatist candidate—who supported an independent Québec—had closed the gap in Canada’s upcoming election. By the time McKay drove from his new Jamaica house (which had no phones installed yet—this was the pre-mobile era) to the nearest hotel to use a pay phone, his position was down three million dollars. He got out only twenty percent. The Canadian dollar continued to plunge. A few days later he was down seven million. “Get me out of everything!” he finally told his floor clerk.

The juxtaposition of the two episodes is devastating. In 1978, McKay acted with textbook discipline: he recognized instantly that the surprise announcement changed the fundamental picture, he did not waste time hoping the market would recover, and he accepted a large loss without hesitation because he knew that staying in the trade would only make things worse. He absorbed a $1.5 million hit and moved on. Yet ten years later, the same trader committed the cardinal sin of rationalization—convincing himself that an alarming signal was merely a data error because acting on it was inconvenient.

What made the Canadian dollar lapse so instructive was how small the initial failure of discipline was. McKay did not decide to ignore the market or override his methodology. He simply rationalized a single data point in a moment when he was distracted and rushed. That was all it took. The market does not grade on a curve or offer partial credit. A momentary lapse can produce the same catastrophic result as deliberate recklessness. As Schwager puts it: the market will not let traders get away with even a momentary lapse of discipline.

McKay’s principle from the 1978 episode remains one of the clearest articulations of trading discipline in the entire series: “When I get hurt in the market, I get the hell out. It doesn’t matter at all where the market is trading. I just get out, because I believe that once you are hurt in the markets, your decisions are going to be far less objective than they are when you are doing well.” The reasoning is psychological as much as financial. Pain clouds judgment. A trader nursing a large loss is more likely to double down, move stops, and rationalize holding—all of which compound the damage.

The chapter’s lesson is that discipline is not a general quality you either possess or lack. It is something that must be exercised on every trade, in every moment. McKay proved he had extraordinary discipline—the 1978 episode leaves no doubt. But discipline is not a permanent achievement; it is a practice that requires constant vigilance. The next time you are tempted to ease up on your rules —to skip the stop, to hold through a news event, to rationalize a warning signal because checking on it would be inconvenient—think of McKay standing in a Jamaican hotel lobby seven million dollars poorer because he was late for a flight.

Chapter 10: Independence

Marcus offered the essential principle: “You have to follow your own light. As long as you stick to your own style, you get the good and bad in your own approach. When you try to incorporate someone else’s style, you often end up with the worst of both styles.”

Schwager illustrates this with a personal story. He had a strong conviction that the yen was heading lower based on a sharp downswing followed by a tight consolidation—a continuation pattern. Then a Market Wizard he had interviewed called to discuss markets. The trader gave fifty-eight reasons why the yen was oversold and heading up. Against his better judgment, Schwager liquidated his short position. When he returned from traveling a few days later, the yen had fallen several hundred points. The trader called again. Asked about the yen, the trader exclaimed: “Long? I am short!” He had been a very short-term trader looking for an intraday bounce. When the bounce did not materialize, he reversed and made two hundred points—while Schwager, who was right all along, made nothing.

The yen story illustrates a subtle but devastating trap. Schwager was not listening to a random pundit on television—he was listening to one of the best traders in the world. The advice was not even wrong in the short term; the trader was looking for an intraday bounce that might well have materialized. The problem was that Schwager operated on a completely different time frame. A long-term trade for Schwager was two weeks; a long-term trade for his friend was a single day. Their views were not contradictory at all—both could have been right simultaneously on their respective time horizons. But by listening to the other trader’s opinion, Schwager abandoned a position he had no reason to doubt.

Marcus articulated the broader principle with characteristic bluntness. Following your own light does not mean you will always be right. It means that when you are wrong, you will be wrong for your own reasons, and you can learn from those reasons and improve. When you follow someone else’s opinion and it goes wrong, you learn nothing— because you were never operating from your own analysis in the first place. And when you follow someone else’s opinion and it goes right, you learn something even worse: that you can succeed by outsourcing your thinking.

Independence also means filtering out the constant noise of financial media and market commentary. Every trading day produces a flood of opinions from analysts, commentators, and fellow traders. Each opinion sounds plausible in the moment. The danger is cumulative: even if you dismiss any single opinion, the steady drip of contrary views erodes your conviction over time. The Market Wizards protected themselves from this erosion by limiting their exposure to outside opinions, relying instead on their own analysis and the price action itself.

There is also a social dimension to independence that Schwager hints at throughout the book. Trading is an inherently solitary activity, and the best traders tend to be comfortable with that solitude. Gil Blake traded from his bedroom. Seykota worked from a home office without even a quote screen. McKay operated independently for decades. The willingness to stand alone, to hold a position when everyone around you disagrees, requires a psychological constitution that not everyone possesses. Independence is not just an intellectual choice—it is a temperamental one.

The practical takeaway is simple but difficult to implement. If you find yourself wanting to discuss your positions with other traders, wanting confirmation from a newsletter, or feeling anxious about a trade until a commentator on television validates your view, you are not yet trading independently. The cure is not to avoid all market discussion, but to make your trading decisions before you hear anyone else’s opinion. Once the decision is made and the position is on, outside views should be irrelevant to your execution.

Schwager adds a caveat that independence does not mean inflexibility. The independent trader changes his mind based on new evidence from the market itself—price action, volume, market response to news. What he does not do is change his mind because someone else told him to. There is a world of difference between reversing a position because the market is telling you that you are wrong and reversing it because another trader’s confidence intimidated you into abandoning your own analysis. The first is adaptation; the second is capitulation.

Chapter 11: Confidence

Jones kept eighty-five percent of his net worth in his own funds because he considered it the safest place in the world for his money. Trout kept ninety-five percent of his money in his own funds. Blake took out four successive second mortgages over three years to increase his trading stake. These traders did not view massive self-investment as high risk—they considered it the safest investment they could make.

The numbers are worth pausing on. A futures trader who views his own fund as the safest investment he can make is displaying a level of confidence that borders on the irrational to outsiders. Most financial advisors would recoil at the idea of putting eighty-five percent of one’s net worth into a single fund—let alone one that trades volatile futures markets. But Jones was not being reckless. He knew his methodology, he knew his risk controls, and he had years of evidence that his approach worked. His confidence was not blind faith but the byproduct of deep competence.

Blake’s case is even more striking. He stopped telling people about the second mortgages because their reactions were so uniformly negative. Friends and family could not fathom why a financially comfortable CFO would bet his home—multiple times over—on a trading strategy built from library microfilm data. But Blake had spent months verifying the statistical persistence in mutual fund prices. He had the evidence. The mortgages were not gambles to him; they were rational capital allocation based on a proven edge. The gap between how Blake perceived the risk and how everyone around him perceived it illustrates the isolation that genuine confidence can create.

Schwager believes both cause-and-effect directions are true: success produces confidence, and confidence produces success. The relationship is self-reinforcing. Early trading success builds the belief that your methodology works, which makes you more willing to stick with it during drawdowns, which in turn produces better long-term results and deepens the confidence further. Conversely, a lack of confidence becomes its own obstacle because hesitation and second-guessing erode the discipline needed to execute any strategy consistently.

Confidence is one of the most consistent traits Schwager observed across all the traders he interviewed. It was not arrogance or bravado— several of the most confident traders were quiet, unassuming people. It was a deep, settled certainty that their approach would produce results over time. This kind of confidence cannot be manufactured or faked. It can only be earned through preparation, testing, and the accumulated experience of seeing your methodology work across many market environments.

One sure sign that you lack the necessary confidence is seeking the advice of others. If you feel the need to check your market views against a friend, a newsletter, or a television commentator before placing a trade, you are telling yourself that you do not trust your own methodology enough to act on it independently. This does not mean traders should never discuss markets—it means the final decision must come from internal conviction, not external validation.

Schwager draws a practical distinction between the confident trader and the recklessly bold one. The confident trader sizes positions according to risk parameters and stays the course because she trusts the underlying process. The recklessly bold trader puts on oversized positions out of ego and doubles down when wrong because admitting error feels intolerable. Confidence is quiet; recklessness is loud. The difference shows up in the equity curve: the confident trader compounds steadily while the bold one blows up spectacularly.

The chapter is brief—Schwager admits that confidence is difficult to discuss at length because it is more of a byproduct than a technique. You cannot simply decide to be confident. But you can build confidence systematically by developing a methodology you understand deeply, testing it thoroughly, starting with small positions to build a track record of execution, and gradually increasing size as results confirm your edge. The process is slow and unglamorous, but it is the only reliable path to the kind of unshakeable conviction that defines a Market Wizard.

Chapter 12: Losing Is Part of the Game

Linda Raschke said it never bothered her to lose because she always knew she would make it right back. That sounds arrogant, but what she was really saying is: “I have a methodology that I know is going to win in the long run. Along the way there will be losses. As long as I stick with my methodology, I am going to come out ahead.”

Dr. Van Tharp, a research psychologist who studied winning versus losing traders, found two critical beliefs that top traders shared: it was okay to lose money in the market, and they knew they had won the game before they started. If you know you have already won, taking a loss is simply part of the path to the ultimate gain.

Schwartz described the pivotal shift in his own career: “What is the ultimate rationalization of a trader in a losing position? ‘I’ll get out when I am even.’ Why is getting out even so important? Because it protects the ego. I was able to become a winning trader when I was able to say, ‘To hell with my ego—making money is more important.’” Amateur traders lose money because they try to avoid losing. Professional traders understand that taking losses is an integral part of the process.

Most traders think there are two types of trades: winning and losing. Actually there are four: winning trades, losing trades, good trades, and bad trades. A good trade follows a positive-expectancy process and can still lose money on any individual occurrence. A bad trade is a gamble and can still make money. Coin flip analogy: heads you pay a hundred dollars, tails you get two hundred. You flip and lose a hundred. Was it a bad bet? No—repeated many times, you would do very well. Conversely, a winning slot machine wager is still a bad trade because the process has a negative expectancy. Repeated many times, you will lose.

The four-trade framework is liberating once you internalize it. If you can separate the quality of your decision from the outcome of any single trade, you stop punishing yourself for unavoidable losses and stop rewarding yourself for lucky wins. This psychological shift is not just abstract philosophy —it directly affects how you trade. A trader who beats himself up over every loss will eventually start avoiding losses by holding too long, moving stops, or cutting winners prematurely. A trader who judges by process will execute his methodology cleanly because he knows that any individual result is statistically meaningless.

Kovner learned this willingness to lose from Marcus early in his career. Marcus taught him that trading is an iterative process of making your best judgment, being wrong, making your next best judgment, being wrong again, making your third best judgment, and then doubling your money. The sequence matters: the profitable trade came only after multiple losses. If Kovner had given up after the first loss or the second, the winning trade would never have materialized. Willingness to accept a string of small losses is the price of admission for large eventual gains.

You have to be willing to make mistakes regularly; there is nothing wrong with it. Michael taught me about making your best judgment, being wrong, making your next best judgment, being wrong, making your third best judgment, and then doubling your money. — Bruce Kovner, on what he learned from Michael Marcus

The irony Schwartz identified cuts to the heart of why amateur traders fail. They lose money precisely because they are trying to avoid losing. They hold losing positions hoping to get back to breakeven. They refuse to take a small loss because it feels like admitting failure. Meanwhile, the loss grows from small to large to catastrophic. Professional traders understand that small, controlled losses are not failures—they are operating expenses. Taking them quickly and cleanly is what allows the winners to compound. The willingness to lose small is what enables you to win big.

Raschke’s attitude is the emotional endstate that every developing trader should aspire to reach. She did not simply tolerate losses; she was genuinely unbothered by them because her confidence in the long-term positive expectancy of her methodology was absolute. This kind of equanimity cannot be faked or willed into existence. It grows naturally from experience: after years of following a methodology and watching it produce profits despite regular individual losses, the trader develops an unshakeable conviction that the process works. The individual loss becomes as emotionally insignificant as a single hand in a card game that you know you will win over the course of an evening.

Chapter 13: Patience

Tom Baldwin, who in the pre-electronic era was the largest individual trader in the Treasury bond pit, identified the average trader’s biggest mistake: “They trade too much. They don’t pick their spots selectively enough.” From Reminiscences of a Stock Operator, published in 1923 and still remarkably pertinent: “There is the plain fool, who does the wrong thing at all times everywhere, but there is the Wall Street fool, who thinks he must trade all the time.”

Marcus identified Ed Seykota as the most influential person in transforming him into a successful trader. Seykota was one of the pioneers of systematic futures trading—one account starting with five thousand dollars in 1972 had increased by 250,000 percent by 1988. Marcus learned patience from watching Seykota sit in a short silver position while the market edged down a half penny a day, a penny a day, with everyone around him screaming that silver was too cheap. Seykota did not even have a quote machine on his desk: “Having a quote machine is like having a slot machine on your desk—you end up feeding it all day long.”

Rogers stressed the importance of trading only with very strong convictions: “One of the best rules anybody can learn about investing is to do nothing, absolutely nothing, unless there is something to do.” When asked if he needed everything to line up before taking a position, Rogers answered: “What you just described is a very fast way to the poorhouse. I just wait until there is money lying in the corner, and all I have to do is go over there and pick it up.”

Mark Weinstein used the cheetah analogy: the fastest animal in the world will wait in the bush for a week until it finds a sick, lame baby antelope. Only when there is no chance it can lose its prey does it attack. “That, to me, is the epitome of professional trading.”

Joel Greenblatt of Gotham Capital—who averaged fifty percent annual compounded returns over ten years with a worst year of plus 28.5 percent—invoked Warren Buffett’s observation that there are no called strikes on Wall Street. In baseball, you must swing after three pitches or you are out. In investing, you can watch as many pitches as you want and only swing when everything lines up perfectly. When students asked Greenblatt about companies with unpredictable earnings, his answer was simple: “Skip that company and find one you can analyze.” The best trade is often the one you do not take.

Kevin Daly built one of the most remarkable long-term records in hedge fund history largely through the trades he did not take. He launched his fund in late 1999—terrible timing for a predominantly long manager—yet achieved 872 percent cumulative gross returns over eleven years while the S&P 500 was actually down nine percent over the same period. His secret was the discipline to remain largely in cash during negative market environments, sidestepping the massive drawdowns that devastated other managers during two major bear markets. His largest peak-to-valley drawdown was only ten percent while the S&P lost nearly half its value twice. The patience to do nothing was his edge.

William Eckhardt explained why patience is so psychologically difficult. Behavioral psychologists have found that intermittent reinforcement—rewards and punishments dispensed randomly—is the most addictive reinforcement schedule of all, more addictive than consistent positive reinforcement. Trading is inherently structured this way: wins and losses arrive unpredictably, creating the same neurological hook that makes slot machines irresistible. The result is a constant urge to trade, to pull the lever one more time, even when no legitimate setup exists. Overcoming this urge is one of the hardest things a trader must learn to do.

Patience applies equally to exits. From Reminiscences: “It never was my thinking that made the big money for me. It always was my sitting. Got that? My sitting tight! Men who can both be right and sit tight are uncommon.” Eckhardt debunked the adage “you can’t go broke taking a profit”—he called it one of the most wrongheaded sayings in trading and added that it describes precisely how many traders do go broke. While amateurs go broke by taking large losses, professionals go broke by taking small profits. Human nature seeks to maximize the chance of gain rather than the gain itself, leading to premature liquidation of winning trades that severely impairs total returns. Eckhardt went further: “The success rate of trades is the least important performance statistic and may even be inversely related to performance.” Marcus agreed: “If you don’t stay with your winners, you are not going to be able to pay for the losers.”

Chapter 14: No Loyalty

Loyalty is a good trait in family and friendship, but a terrible one in trading. The absence of loyalty is flexibility—the ability to completely change your opinion when the evidence warrants it. Marcus: “I am very open-minded. I am willing to take in information that is difficult to accept emotionally. When a market moves counter to my expectations, I have always been able to say, ‘I had hoped to make a lot of money in this position, but it isn’t working, so I’m getting out.’”

In April 2009, O’Shea was positioned bearishly in the aftermath of the financial crisis. But the market was telling him he was wrong. His thought process was revealing: “China is turning around, metal prices are turning higher, and the Australian dollar is moving up. What is that telling me? There is a recovery somewhere in the world. So I can’t stick with the whole-world-is-terrible thesis.” He abandoned his bearish view and reversed— saving what could have been a disastrous year as equities and commodities embarked on a multiyear rally. O’Shea cites Soros as the paragon of flexibility: “He has the least regret of anyone I have ever met. He has no emotional attachment to an idea.” Soros once made $250 million on a huge foreign exchange position in a single day, was quoted in the press about it, and when the market reversed —the position simply disappeared. No agonizing, no ego, no attempt to prove himself right.

Michael Platt demonstrated the same instinct under extreme pressure. He held a massive long position in European interest rate futures when the European Central Bank unexpectedly hiked rates. Platt was completely unaware—he was on a flight from London to South Africa. As soon as his plane landed, his assistant called with the news. “How much are we down?” “About $70 million to $80 million.” Platt instantly reasoned that if the ECB had started raising rates, more hikes were likely and the loss could balloon to $250 million within a week. “Dump everything!” he instructed. “When I am wrong, the only instinct I have is to get out. If I was thinking one way, and now I can see that it was a real mistake, then I am probably not the only person in shock, so I’d better be the first one to sell. I don’t care what the price is.”

Schwager witnessed Jones’s flexibility firsthand during two interview visits separated by only two weeks. On the first visit, Jones was very bearish and heavily short the S&P 500. On the second visit, his view had completely reversed: “This market is sold out,” he declared. He had not only covered his short but gone long, based entirely on the market’s failure to follow through on the downside. A 180-degree shift in fourteen days. The market moved sharply higher in the ensuing weeks. Jones did not agonize over the reversal or worry about looking foolish. He simply read the market, changed his mind, and acted.

The most dramatic example of flexibility belongs to Stanley Druckenmiller. On Friday, October 16, 1987, he came into the day net short the S&P 500. By afternoon, believing the market had reached support, he not only covered his short but went 130 percent long—a leveraged position. Over the weekend he realized his catastrophic error. Monday morning the market opened enormously lower. Druckenmiller covered his entire long position in the first hour and went net short again. He reversed a large position, then reversed it again the next trading day with the market having moved tremendously against his previous reversal. Despite this being arguably the worst trading blunder imaginable, October 1987 showed up as only a moderate loss on his record. “Good traders liquidate their positions when they believe they are wrong; great traders reverse their positions when they believe they are wrong.”

Schwager warns against publicizing market calls: announcing your prediction makes you emotionally invested in being right. Jones specifically addressed this: “I avoid letting my trading opinions be influenced by comments I may have made on the record about a market.” Seykota learned this the hard way early in his career. After telling friends he expected silver to keep going up, he found he could not afford to be wrong when silver started falling. He kept ignoring the market’s signals, telling himself the decline was just a temporary correction. His subconscious finally intervened through recurring dreams of a big silver aircraft going down, headed for a crash. Eventually he got the message, dumped his long position, and even went short. The dreams stopped. The episode illustrates how public commitments to a market view create a psychological trap that even experienced traders struggle to escape.

The chapter’s collective message is that flexibility is not the same as indecision or lack of conviction. The Market Wizards described here held strong views and traded with enormous conviction. But their conviction was attached to the present assessment of evidence, not to the position itself. When the evidence changed, they changed. When the market told them they were wrong, they listened. The difference between stubbornness and conviction is that stubbornness persists in the face of contradictory evidence, while conviction updates itself based on new information. Good traders liquidate when wrong; great traders reverse.

Flexibility also applies to trade ideas before they are implemented. Jamie Mai of Cornwall Capital—profiled in Michael Lewis’s The Big Short as one of the big winners on the short side of subprime mortgages—demonstrated this in 2011. Mai noticed that China had transitioned from a net coal exporter to a net importer, with imports accelerating rapidly. Dry bulk shippers were trading at depressed cash flow multiples. Going long seemed like a perfect trade. But Mai, who comes from a private equity background and researches every idea exhaustively, dug deeper and discovered that high freight rates from rising commodity demand had sparked a massive shipbuilding boom. New freighters were coming onstream with fleet capacity growing at twenty percent annually. Even the most optimistic projections for Chinese demand could not absorb the coming surplus. So Mai did the exact opposite of his original idea: he went short dry bulk shippers via out-of-the-money put options—making it his highest-conviction short trade of the year.

Chapter 15: Size Matters

Edward Thorp’s insight from blackjack applies directly to trading: by betting more on high-probability hands and less on low-probability ones, you can transform a negative-edge game into a positive-edge one. In trading, varying position size based on conviction can significantly improve performance. Marcus explicitly used this principle: when fundamentals, chart pattern, and market tone all aligned, he entered five to six times the position size of his other trades. Those high-conviction trades saved him, despite his admission that he took too many marginal trades out of enjoyment.

But size is a double-edged sword. Jones’s most devastating early trade involved a 400-contract long position in cotton. When the market broke below a trading range and then rebounded, Jones bid higher for another 100 contracts in an act of bravado. The firm with deliverable stocks instantly yelled “Sold!”—Jones knew he was trapped. The market locked limit down within sixty seconds. His accounts lost sixty to seventy percent on a single trade. The experience transformed him permanently: his focus shifted from what he could make to what he could lose.

Kovner believed most novice traders trade three to five times too big: “Whatever you think your position ought to be, cut it at least in half. A greedy trader always blows up.” Steve Clark said you must trade within your “emotional capacity”—one sure sign your position is too large is if you wake up worrying about it.

The counterpoint is that there are times to step on the accelerator. Druckenmiller learned from Soros that “it’s not whether you’re right or wrong that’s important, but how much money you make when you’re right.” When Druckenmiller had a one-billion-dollar position working in his favor, Soros asked: “You call that a position?” and encouraged him to double it. After the 1985 Plaza Accord, while other traders at Soros Management took profits on their yen positions, Soros came bolting out telling them to stop selling—the government had just told them the dollar would decline for the next year. The lesson is not to always trade large; it is to be willing to trade larger when conviction is exceptionally high.

Steve Clark introduced the concept of trading within your “emotional capacity.” If a position is so large that you are prone to getting out on meaningless corrections, you will lose money on trades that would have been winners with proper sizing. One sure way of knowing your position is too large: you wake up worrying about it. Howard Seidler, one of the original Turtle traders, provided a vivid illustration. He took a short position, the market moved in his favor, so he doubled his size. Then a small adverse move made him so anxious that he liquidated both the add-on and the original position. Two days later the market collapsed exactly as he had anticipated. He had been right about the direction but wrong about the size, and the oversizing cost him the entire trade.

Schwartz warned against the most common sizing mistake: increasing bets as soon as you start making money. “That is a quick way to get wiped out.” He recommended waiting until you have at least doubled your capital before trading larger. The logic is straightforward: early gains may reflect favorable market conditions or luck rather than genuine skill. Increasing size before your edge has been validated across multiple market environments exposes you to the risk of discovering that your methodology does not work just as you have maximized your exposure.

Position sizing must also account for volatility and correlation. In 2008, O’Shea encountered managers who said they had “cut their risk in half” by reducing leverage from four to two. His response: “Do you realize volatility has gone up five times?” They had cut leverage by half while volatility had quintupled, meaning their volatility-adjusted risk had actually increased dramatically. Edward Thorp addressed the correlation problem by trading a correlation matrix that reduced exposures in correlated markets. If two markets were highly correlated and his system wanted to go long both, he would take a smaller position in each. Positions are not independent coin tosses—when they are positively correlated, losses tend to occur simultaneously, and position sizing must reflect that reality.

Chapter 16: Doing the Uncomfortable Thing

Eckhardt’s thesis is that human comfort-seeking leads to decisions that are not just random but worse than random. The dart-throwing monkey will outperform most human traders because humans systematically buy on weakness (it feels like a bargain), sell on strength (it feels like selling dear), take profits too quickly (locking in feels good), and hold losers too long (hoping to get back to even protects the ego). Richard Dennis: “If it feels good, don’t do it.”

Greenblatt’s “Magic Formula” website provided an inadvertent controlled experiment. Investors could either pick their own stocks from the pre-screened list or let the system manage the portfolio automatically. After two years, the managed portfolios outperformed the self-managed ones by twenty-five percent—even though both drew from the same list. The self-managing investors reduced exposure when the market fell, sold underperformers, and avoided the stocks that felt painful to own—missing the biggest winners. A monkey throwing darts at the list would have done better.

Kahneman and Tversky’s prospect theory confirmed the mechanism. In the famous experiment, people were offered a choice between a sure three thousand dollars and an eighty percent chance of four thousand. The vast majority chose the certain gain, even though the expected value of the gamble was higher at thirty-two hundred. Then the same people were offered a choice between a sure loss of three thousand and an eighty percent chance of losing four thousand. The vast majority chose to gamble, even though the expected loss was greater. People are risk-averse with gains but risk-seeking with losses.

This asymmetry explains precisely why most traders let losses run and cut profits short. When a trade is profitable, the certainty of locking in the gain is psychologically irresistible—even if the expected value of holding is higher. When a trade is losing, the hope of avoiding the certain loss drives the trader to gamble by holding on. The old cliché to “let your profits run and cut your losses short” is the exact opposite of what most people naturally do. Following this advice requires you to actively resist your own instincts on every single trade.

Eckhardt drew the sweeping conclusion that applies to anyone who risks money in markets: “There is a persistent overall tendency for equity to flow from the many to the few. In the long run, the majority loses. The implication for the trader is that to win you have to act like the minority. If you bring normal human habits and tendencies to trading, you’ll gravitate toward the majority and invariably lose.” This is not cynicism—it is a mathematical observation about how human behavioral biases create a systematic transfer of wealth from the majority to the disciplined minority.

Even computerized and systematic trading is not immune to human comfort-seeking. When backtesting a trading system, the developer discovers drawdown periods and instinctively adds rules to smooth them out. Each iteration makes the simulated equity curve look better. But the more a system is optimized to fit historical prices, the less likely it is to work in the future, because the impressive simulated results were achieved with the hindsight knowledge of prices that will never repeat in exactly the same sequence. The human desire for comfort—the urge to eliminate the discomfort of drawdowns—corrupts even the most rational, data-driven approach to trading.

Richard Dennis provided the most memorable empirical proof of how human biases systematically destroy forecasting ability. One of his employees entered a charting contest that required predicting year-end prices for multiple markets. Rather than attempting any analysis, the employee simply submitted current prices as his predictions for every single market. He finished in the top five out of hundreds of contestants. At least ninety-five percent of participants—people who had spent time and effort crafting their forecasts—produced predictions that were worse than the lazy assumption that prices would not change at all.

The chapter’s conclusion is sobering. Most people lose money in trading not only because they lack skill but because their natural inclination to make comfortable choices produces results that are systematically worse than random. Awareness of this inherent human handicap is the first step in resisting the temptation to make decisions that feel good but are wrong on balance. The uncomfortable trade— buying into fear, selling into euphoria, holding a winner when every instinct screams to take the profit, cutting a loser when hope whispers that it will come back—is almost always the right trade. That is what makes trading so psychologically demanding and why so few people succeed at it.

Chapter 17: Emotions and Trading

Alex Honnold, the greatest free solo climber in history, was asked on 60 Minutes whether he feels an adrenaline rush while climbing two thousand feet without ropes. “If I get a rush, it means that something has gone horribly wrong. The whole thing should be pretty slow and controlled.” The same applies to expert trading. The Hollywood image of adrenaline-filled risk-taking has nothing to do with successful trading.

Hite’s friend, who had gone broke trading, asked how he could follow a computerized system without getting bored. “I don’t trade for excitement; I trade to win.” Charles Faulkner, a trading coach, had a client with a winning system who learned to enter an emotionally detached state—but one day said flatly: “This is boring.” The trader eventually blew up. He knew how to detach emotionally but did not like being there. The markets are an expensive place to look for excitement.

Druckenmiller’s desperate T-bill trade in 1981 shows what happens when you MUST win. His only income source—a consulting contract with Drysdale Securities worth ten thousand dollars a month— abruptly disappeared. His seven-million-dollar fund generated only seventy thousand a year in fees against one hundred eighty thousand in overhead, and he had just fifty thousand dollars in cash. Absolutely convinced that interest rates had peaked, he took the firm’s entire fifty thousand and margined a highly leveraged long position in T-bill futures. His analysis was exactly right—rates hit their cycle high within a week and never came close again. But he lost everything in four days because the desperation that drove the trade made the position sizing suicidal. The market will seldom reward the carelessness of trades born of desperation.

Marcus captured the flip side of emotional trading when he recalled impulsively exiting his entire soybean position during the great 1973 bull market. Prices had tripled their previous record highs, and rather than staying with the trend, Marcus tried to be fancy and took profits on everything. Seykota, sitting at the same firm, stayed with his position. Soybeans then went limit up for twelve consecutive days. Marcus dreaded going to work each morning, knowing that soybeans would be bid limit up again while he was flat and Seykota was still in. The pain of watching someone else profit from the trade you abandoned is one of the most corrosive emotions in trading, and it often leads to even worse impulsive decisions as the trader scrambles to get back in.

Kovner’s “going-bust trade” in 1977 soybeans remains Schwager’s favorite cautionary tale about impulsive trading. Kovner had a brilliantly conceived spread—long old-crop July, short new-crop November—that was soaring on limit-up moves. At new highs, his broker called excitedly, urging him to lift the short side and go naked long. In what Kovner called “a moment of insanity,” he agreed. Fifteen minutes later the market was limit down. Between lifting the protective short and liquidating the long, his account equity was halved. Marcus experienced a similar agony after impulsively exiting his entire soybean position during the 1973 bull market: Seykota, who sat in the same office, stayed with his position and watched soybeans go limit up for twelve consecutive days.

Schwartz identified a particularly dangerous pattern that follows losses: the impulse to recover immediately by trading bigger. “Whenever you are hit, you are very upset emotionally. Most traders try to make it back immediately; they try to play bigger. Whenever you try to get all your losses back at once, you are most often doomed to fail.” Schwager estimates that probably no class of trades has a higher failure rate than impulsive trades born of the desperation to recoup losses. The emotional state that produces these trades—anger, frustration, wounded pride—is precisely the state least likely to generate clear analytical thinking.

Several emotional influences cloud a trader’s objectivity without being recognized as emotions at all. A trader who is long will unconsciously dismiss bearish evidence because accepting it threatens the position. A trader who procrastinated on a buy will ignore signs that the market is moving higher because entering now would confirm the earlier mistake of waiting. A trader who has publicly forecast a direction will resist contradictory evidence because admitting error in front of others feels intolerable. In each case, the trader believes he is being analytical when he is actually being emotional. The subconscious mind, free from these ego-driven constraints, can sometimes be more objective than conscious analysis—which is why intuition, properly understood, has value.

Impulsive trades should not be confused with intuitive trades. Impulse is almost invariably destructive, while intuition—the subconscious recognition of patterns from past experience—can be highly valuable for experienced traders. Intuition is simply accumulated market experience operating below the threshold of conscious awareness. A veteran trader who gets a “feeling” about a market is not being irrational—his subconscious is recognizing a pattern it has seen hundreds of times before. “The trick is to differentiate between what you want to happen and what you know will happen.” If the feeling is driven by hope, fear, or ego, it is impulse. If it arrives unbidden and without emotional charge, it may be genuine intuition worth heeding.

Chapter 18: Dynamic versus Static Trading

O’Shea does not believe in fixed trading rules: “Traders who are successful over the long run adapt. If they do use rules, and you meet them 10 years later, they will have broken those rules. Because the world has changed.” Thorp demonstrated this through three successive versions of his statistical arbitrage strategy, each adapted as the previous version lost its edge. If he had clung to the original system, profitability would have evaporated.

Scaling in and out of positions, rather than making all-or-nothing decisions, provides flexibility that most traders ignore. Scale-down buying ensures you have at least a partial position if the market keeps running, without assuming full risk after a significant advance. It also reduces the average entry price, which mitigates the risk of abandoning a good long-term trade due to an initial adverse move that would have been tolerable with a better cost basis.

Bill Lipschutz, former head of global foreign exchange trading at Salomon Brothers, attributed his ability to stay with long-term winners to his practice of scaling out. When you sell your entire position and the market continues higher, you are left watching a move you identified correctly but failed to capture. When you hold your entire position and the market reverses sharply, you give back a disproportionate amount of gains. Scaling out ensures you still participate if the move continues while banking partial profits along the way. Lipschutz found that this approach fundamentally changed his relationship with winning trades—he could hold longer because the psychological pressure of watching unrealized gains fluctuate was reduced.

Jimmy Balodimas of First New York Securities takes dynamic trading to its logical extreme. He always takes some money off the table when a trade moves in his favor, then rebuilds the position on corrections. This approach generates profits on the intermediate swings that most traders simply endure passively. A trade can even show a loss from original entry to final exit and still be profitable through the accumulated gains from trading around the core position. As Balodimas put it: “That saves me a lot of money, because when the market rallies, I have a smaller position.”

The dynamic approach carries an additional benefit that is easy to overlook. By reducing exposure on favorable moves, you lessen the chance of being knocked out of a fundamentally sound trade by a sharp correction. Many traders have experienced the frustration of being right on the direction but getting stopped out on a violent pullback. With a reduced position size after partial profit-taking, the same pullback represents a smaller loss and is far less likely to trigger an exit. The trader stays in the game and is positioned to benefit when the original thesis resumes.

Schwager notes that scaling avoids the temptation of wanting to be completely right. All-or-nothing decisions carry enormous psychological weight because every entry and exit feels like a final verdict on your judgment. By shunning these binary choices and instead scaling gradually in and out, you will never achieve the best possible outcome, but you will also never suffer the worst. For most traders, this tradeoff dramatically improves both financial results and emotional well-being.

The chapter’s deeper message is that rigidity in trading is a liability. Markets evolve, edges erode, and conditions change. The traders who survive across decades are those who treat their methodology as a living framework rather than a fixed algorithm. Thorp adapted his statistical arbitrage strategy three times as each version lost its edge. O’Shea broke his own rules when circumstances demanded it. The willingness to modify your approach—whether in how you enter, how you exit, or how you size your positions—is not a sign of weakness but of maturity. Static traders may succeed for a while, but the market eventually finds their blind spots and exploits them relentlessly.

Dynamic trading does not mean trading without a plan. It means having a plan that incorporates flexibility as a core feature. Your methodology defines the framework; the dynamic elements—scaling, adapting, trading around positions—operate within that framework to improve execution. The distinction matters because undisciplined traders often use “flexibility” as an excuse for abandoning their rules whenever the going gets tough. True dynamic trading is structured adaptation, not ad hoc decision-making.

Chapter 19: Market Response

Schwartz credited his friend Bob Zoellner with teaching him to read market action: “When the market gets good news and goes down, it means the market is very weak; when it gets bad news and goes up, it means the market is healthy.” A counter-to- anticipated response to news may be more meaningful than the news itself.

McKay demonstrated this with gold during the Gulf War. On the eve of the first U.S. air strike in January 1991, gold was trading just below four hundred dollars. The night of the attack, gold rallied to four hundred ten in Asian markets—then retreated to three hundred ninety, below where it had been before the supposedly bullish news. McKay saw this failure to hold gains as deeply bearish. Gold opened sharply lower the next morning and continued declining for months.

Ray Dalio learned this lesson through two formative shocks. When Nixon took the U.S. off the gold standard in 1971, Dalio expected a bearish reaction—the market rallied. When Mexico defaulted on its debt in 1982 with unemployment at eleven percent, Dalio expected disaster for stocks—the default marked the exact bottom and the beginning of an eighteen-year bull market. Dalio’s conclusion: “A crisis development that leads to central banks easing and coming to the rescue can swamp the impact of the crisis itself.”

Marcus provided the most actionable version of this principle. During a soybean bull market, the most bullish export report imaginable came out. Everyone expected limit up for three consecutive days. The market opened limit up but could not hold the level for the first morning. Marcus immediately sold everything and went short: “Soybeans were supposed to be limit up for three days, and they can’t even hold limit up for the first morning. It was the only time I made a lot of money on an error.” Schwager recalls a parallel from his own career: during the largest cotton bull market of the twentieth century, the weekly export report showed sales of half a million bales to China—by far the most bullish report ever. Instead of locking limit up at two hundred points, cotton opened only 150 points higher and started trading down. That opening proved to be the exact market top, a high not seen again for over thirty years.

Platt experienced a variation of this principle in 2009 when he placed a large trade to benefit from a widening yield curve. Each time detrimental news came out, he braced for losses—but nothing happened. After this scenario repeated several times, Platt concluded that the yield curve simply could not get any flatter no matter what news emerged. He quadrupled his position. The yield curve moved from 25 points to 210 points, making it his biggest winning trade of the year. When a position refuses to be hurt by adverse developments, it may be signaling exceptional strength.

Scott Ramsey of Denali Asset Management—who averaged fifteen percent annual compounded returns with eleven percent volatility over thirteen years—used the “submerged volleyball” metaphor: push a volleyball underwater (the crisis event), then let go and the ball pops back out of the water. The markets that are strongest during a crisis will be the leaders when the pressure lifts. Measuring which markets were strongest during a crisis can tell you which markets are likely to lead when the pressure is off.

Ramsey and Marcus agreed on the practical application: always be long the strongest market and short the weakest. Marcus recalled a day during an inflationary period in the 1970s when all commodities were locked limit up—except cotton, which opened limit up but then sold off during the session. “That was the market peak,” he said. Cotton never recovered. When everything around a market is screaming higher and it alone cannot hold its gains, the weakness is telling you something that the headlines are not. Most novice traders do the opposite, buying laggards because they seem to have more upside potential—a reliably losing approach born of the same comfort-seeking impulse that Eckhardt warned about.

Schwager identifies two reasons why markets often respond counterintuitively to news. First, markets frequently anticipate and discount impending events before they occur. By the time a widely expected event actually happens, its impact has already been priced in, and the event’s occurrence actually removes the uncertainty—leading to a price reaction opposite to what the news logically implies. Second, significant bearish events can trigger bullish policy responses—central bank easing, government intervention, emergency spending— that swamp the direct impact of the crisis itself. Dalio saw this twice and never forgot it. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for any trader who has been baffled by a market that rallied on terrible news or collapsed on good news.

Chapter 20: The Value of Mistakes

Dalio views mistakes with near-reverence: “I learned that there is an incredible beauty in mistakes because embedded in each mistake is a puzzle and a gem that I could get if I solved it.” This philosophy permeates Bridgewater’s culture, where it is acceptable to fail but unacceptable not to identify, analyze, and learn from mistakes. Schwartz drew the key contrast between trading and other careers: “Most people, in most careers, are busy trying to cover up their mistakes. As a trader, you are forced to confront your mistakes because the numbers don’t lie.”

Clark’s advice is deceptively simple: “Do more of what works and less of what doesn’t.” It sounds obvious, but surprisingly few traders actually follow this rule. Most have never systematically dissected their profit-and-loss statements to identify which types of trades, which markets, or which setups consistently generate their returns versus which ones drain them. Without this analysis, traders unconsciously repeat their worst habits while neglecting their best instincts.

Dalio traced the origin of the entire Bridgewater system to his personal discipline of writing down the reasons for every trade at the moment of entry and then comparing the actual outcome to his expectations when the trade was closed. Beginning around 1980, he maintained this practice religiously. Over time, the gap between his predictions and reality revealed systematic biases in his thinking that he could correct. This simple habit—a trader’s journal with reasons, expectations, and post-mortem analysis—evolved into the foundation for the world’s largest hedge fund.

McKay took a similarly disciplined approach to self-examination. Every single day, he made copies of his trading cards and reviewed them at home that evening. He looked for patterns: why were the winners winning and the losers losing? Over months of nightly review, he began to recognize which types of trades were consistently profitable and which were consistently unprofitable. This allowed him to become increasingly selective, avoiding the setups that were more likely to produce losses. McKay attributed much of his early and sustained success directly to this rigorous after-hours analysis.

Schwager draws a sharp distinction between trading and most other careers when it comes to mistakes. Schwartz put it plainly: in most professions, people spend their energy trying to cover up their mistakes. A bad decision gets buried, rationalized, or blamed on someone else. Trading offers no such luxury. The numbers on your screen are brutally transparent. Every mistake shows up in the equity curve, and no amount of rationalization can make a losing trade profitable in retrospect. This forced accountability is painful, but it is also what makes trading one of the most effective learning environments available.

Dalio’s 111-page Principles document, required reading for every Bridgewater employee, codified this philosophy into a corporate culture. Among its rules: “Create a culture in which it is okay to fail but unacceptable not to identify, analyze, and learn from mistakes.” And: “If you don’t mind being wrong on the way to being right, you will learn a lot.” Bridgewater’s extraordinary long-term track record suggests that this mistake-embracing culture was not just philosophical window dressing but a genuine competitive advantage.

Each mistake, if recognized and acted upon, provides an opportunity for improving a trading approach. Most traders would benefit enormously by writing down each mistake, the lesson it implies, and the specific change they intend to make. A trading log reviewed periodically serves as both a diagnostic tool and a reinforcement mechanism. Schwager concludes with a principle that separates the professionals from the perpetual amateurs: trading mistakes cannot be avoided, but repeating the same mistakes can be, and the difference between the two is often the difference between success and failure.

The chapter carries an implicit challenge for every trader reading it. How many of your recent losses repeated a pattern you have seen before? How many times have you violated the same rule, made the same type of impulsive trade, or held a loser past your stop for the same rationalized reason? If the answer is more than once, you are not lacking in market knowledge—you are lacking in the systematic self-examination that Dalio, McKay, and Clark practiced relentlessly. The trading log is not a suggestion; it is the single most underused tool in any trader’s arsenal.

Chapter 21: Implementation versus Idea

How a trade is implemented can be more important than the trade idea itself. O’Shea viewed the NASDAQ in 1999–2000 as a bubble. After the March 2000 break, he was confident equities would surrender their gains. But he never considered shorting stocks. The price decline after a bubble bursts is typically interspersed with treacherous bear market rallies— the NASDAQ witnessed a plus-forty-percent rebound in the summer of 2000 alone. Instead, O’Shea reasoned that a NASDAQ bust would slow the economy, which would cause interest rates to decline, so he went long bonds. Both trends materialized: the stock decline was highly erratic while the bond rise was relatively smooth. The trade succeeded not because the premise was correct, which it was, but because of how it was implemented.

Greenblatt’s Wells Fargo trade in the early 1990s provides another illustration. The bank was under pressure from California commercial real estate exposure during a deep recession. The outcome was binary: bankruptcy would send the stock down eighty dollars, survival would send it up eighty dollars— a one-to-one risk/reward on the stock. But by buying LEAPS (options with more than two years to expiration), Greenblatt transformed it into a one-to-five risk/reward ratio. If the bank survived, he would make five times his money; if it failed, he would lose only the option premium. The odds were much better than fifty-fifty that Wells Fargo would survive, so the stock was already a buy. But in terms of risk-reward, the options were an even better buy. The stock more than doubled before the options expired.

The contrast between the two examples reveals a deeper principle. O’Shea’s insight was about choosing the right market to express a view, while Greenblatt’s was about choosing the right instrument within the same market. Both demonstrate that the implementation decision deserves as much analytical rigor as the original trade thesis. Too many traders stop thinking once they have identified a directional view. The Market Wizards treated the question of how to express a view as a separate and equally important analytical problem.

O’Shea’s decision not to short the NASDAQ directly was rooted in a sophisticated understanding of how bubbles deflate. While the price rise during a bubble can be relatively smooth, the decline after a bubble bursts is invariably punctuated by violent bear market rallies that can easily exceed thirty or forty percent. The NASDAQ’s summer 2000 rally proved the point. Any short seller with reasonable stop-loss discipline would have been forced out during that rally, turning a correct thesis into a realized loss. By expressing the same view through bonds, O’Shea captured the economic consequence of the bubble bursting without exposing himself to the erratic price action of the bubble itself.

The Greenblatt trade illustrates the power of asymmetric payoff structures. When the potential outcomes are binary—the company either survives or it does not—options can transform the risk-reward profile in ways that outright stock positions cannot. The key insight is that the option premium effectively defines your maximum loss regardless of how catastrophic the downside scenario becomes, while your upside remains uncapped if the favorable scenario plays out. This is not a general endorsement of options over stocks; it is a specific observation that certain trade setups benefit enormously from the nonlinear payoff that options provide.

Schwager extracts a practical lesson that applies beyond these specific examples. Before putting on any trade, ask yourself: is there a better way to express this view? Could you use a different market, a different instrument, a different structure, or a different time frame to capture the same directional thesis with a more favorable risk-reward profile? The traders who ask this question systematically gain a persistent advantage over those who simply buy or sell the most obvious instrument.

The chapter also carries an implicit warning about the danger of being right but losing money. In trading, being correct about the direction of a market is necessary but not sufficient. If the path from here to there involves drawdowns that exceed your risk tolerance, you will be stopped out before your thesis is vindicated. Druckenmiller’s T-bill trade from an earlier chapter is the cautionary example: he timed a major interest rate peak within a single week but lost everything because his position sizing and timing made the trade unmanageable. O’Shea and Greenblatt avoided this trap by choosing implementations that could withstand adverse intermediate price action.

The distinction between idea and implementation is one of the most sophisticated concepts in the book, and it separates truly elite traders from merely competent ones. Most traders focus almost exclusively on the what—which direction will the market move? The Market Wizards spend at least as much time on the how—what is the optimal vehicle, structure, and position size to capture that move while controlling risk? Getting the what right is only half the battle; the how determines whether a correct view translates into actual profits.

Chapter 22: Off the Hook

Schwartz offered one of the most unique and actionable observations in the entire Market Wizards canon: “If you’re ever very nervous about a position overnight, and especially over a weekend, and you’re able to get out at a much better price than you thought possible when the market trades, you’re usually better off staying with the position.”

The logic is elegant. If you are worried about a position, it is because something dramatic has happened—bearish news, a breakout against you, a scary close. But you are not the only one who knows. Everyone knows. If, despite the fact that the market “should” move strongly against you at the next opening, it instead barely moves or goes your way—it means very strong hands are positioned in the same direction. When the market lets you off the hook easily, do not get out.

Bill Lipschutz lived this principle in 1988 when he was short three billion dollars against the deutsche mark at Salomon Brothers and Gorbachev’s speech caused the dollar to strengthen—leaving him trapped with a seventy-to- ninety-million-dollar loss by Friday afternoon. When Tokyo opened Sunday night, the dollar was actually moving lower. Most traders would have been so relieved that they would have covered immediately. Instead, Lipschutz abandoned his plan to cover half, waited for the dollar to keep sliding, and eventually exited with an eighteen-million-dollar loss rather than the ninety million he had feared. “The reason I didn’t get out on the Tokyo opening was that it was the wrong trading decision.”

Schwager himself applied Schwartz’s dictum in 2011. He was heavily short the NASDAQ when a devastating unemployment report came out—so bearish that commentators could not find a single silver lining. The market sold off sharply in response, then reversed and erased seventy-five percent of the losses by the close, finishing the week near multiyear highs. Schwager expected to be forced to cover. But on Sunday night, the market traded down fifteen points in the first ten minutes. The market was letting him off the hook. Recalling Schwartz’s advice, he covered only a token ten percent of his short position. Monday was much lower and continued sharply lower thereafter. Following Schwartz’s principle saved him a great deal of money.

What makes Schwartz’s observation so valuable is that it works precisely because it is counterintuitive. Every instinct tells you to take the gift—the market is giving you a chance to escape a trade that was keeping you up at night. Natural human relief overwhelms analytical thinking in this moment. The desire to eliminate anxiety is so powerful that most traders will not even pause to ask why the market is being so generous. But the question is essential: if everyone knows the same bearish information, and the market still cannot go down, something important is happening beneath the surface.

Lipschutz’s decision to stay with his massive short dollar position required extraordinary nerve. He was down seventy to ninety million dollars heading into a weekend with no ability to trade. He tried to move the market by selling an additional three hundred million deutsche marks on Friday afternoon, and the market absorbed his order without even flinching. That told him the buying pressure behind the dollar was immense. Yet when Tokyo opened Sunday night with the dollar actually declining, he recognized the signal: the market was letting him off the hook. Instead of covering in relief, he waited and ultimately exited with a loss of only eighteen million rather than the catastrophic ninety million he had feared.

The principle also works in reverse, though Schwager does not dwell on this. If you are nervous about a position and the market opens aggressively against you—confirming your worst fears—the pain is real but the information is straightforward. The market is doing what everyone expected it to do. That is a normal response to news, not a signal of hidden strength or weakness. The truly informative moments are when the market does the unexpected: when it should punish you and instead gives you a reprieve, or when it should reward you and instead takes away your profits.

Schwager notes that this principle requires judgment and cannot be applied mechanically. Not every favorable opening after a scary weekend means you should hold your position. The trader still needs to assess whether the underlying thesis remains intact, whether the news that caused the anxiety has been genuinely discounted, and whether the favorable opening reflects real institutional accumulation or merely thin overnight liquidity. Schwartz’s dictum is a powerful filter, but like all trading principles, it works best when combined with contextual analysis and experience.

Chapter 23: Love of the Endeavor

The language Market Wizards use to describe trading is uniformly game-like. Kovner: “Market analysis is like a tremendous multidimensional chess board.” Rogers: “one big, three-dimensional puzzle.” David Ryan: “a giant treasure hunt.” Clark: “I thought I was playing a video game, and I couldn’t believe I was getting paid to do it.” Trout: “I like to trade. Now I get to play a very fun game, and I’m paid handsomely for it.” Lipschutz had quote monitors in every room of his home, including one next to his bed and one in the bathroom. “It’s tremendous fun!! I would do this for free.”

O’Shea cut through the sentimentality: “Frankly, if you don’t love it, there are much better things to do with your life. No one who trades for the money is going to be any good. If successful traders were only motivated by the money, they would just stop after five years and enjoy the material things.” But they do not stop. They keep trading for decades because the intellectual challenge is intrinsically rewarding in a way that no amount of accumulated wealth can replace.

The language these traders use is revealing. They do not describe trading as work, as a job, or even as a career. They describe it as a game, a puzzle, a treasure hunt. This framing matters because it reflects a fundamentally different relationship with the activity. A person who views trading as a job will eventually burn out under the relentless pressure of daily gains and losses. A person who views it as an endlessly fascinating puzzle will find the pressure energizing rather than draining. The emotional resilience of the Market Wizards is not separate from their love of trading—it is a direct consequence of it.

Lipschutz’s lifestyle was perhaps the most extreme expression of this love. He had quote monitors in every room of his home, including one next to his bed so he could check prices half-asleep and one at standing height in his bathroom. His entire life was organized around markets. When asked whether trading consumed too much of his existence, his answer was immediate: “It’s tremendous fun!! It’s fascinating as hell because it’s different every day. I’m thirty-six years old, and I almost feel like I have never worked. I would do this for free.”

The distinction between trading for money and trading for love has practical consequences that go beyond motivation. When the primary goal is money, every loss feels like a personal failure and every drawdown triggers anxiety about financial survival. This emotional turbulence leads to exactly the kinds of mistakes the previous chapters warn against: impulsive trades, abandoning methodology during losing streaks, taking profits too early, and letting losers run. When the primary goal is the intellectual satisfaction of solving a complex, ever-changing problem, individual losses are easier to absorb because they do not threaten the trader’s fundamental sense of purpose.

There is also a selection effect at work. Trading is so difficult, so psychologically punishing, and so statistically weighted against the participant that only those who genuinely love the process will persist long enough to develop mastery. The dropout rate in trading is enormous. Most people who try it lose money and quit within a few years. The ones who survive the initial gauntlet and go on to become consistently profitable are almost always people for whom trading was never primarily about the money in the first place. The money was a byproduct of doing something they would have done regardless.

Schwager ends the book with this chapter deliberately. After twenty-two chapters of rules, principles, frameworks, and cautionary tales, the final message is disarmingly simple: none of it will help you if you do not love what you are doing. You can have the right methodology, the right risk management, the right psychology, and the right discipline, but if trading feels like drudgery, you will eventually cut corners, skip the nightly analysis, let a stop slide, or take a week off at exactly the wrong moment. Love of the endeavor is not one more item on the checklist —it is the foundation that makes everything else on the checklist sustainable over a career measured in decades rather than months.

The chapter implicitly challenges every reader to ask a difficult question: do I actually love trading, or do I love the idea of making money from trading? If you find the process of analyzing markets, testing hypotheses, managing risk, and confronting your own psychological weaknesses genuinely fascinating— even on the days when you lose money—you have the right temperament. If you find the process tedious and are only sustained by the occasional thrill of a winning trade, you may be better suited to a different pursuit. The Market Wizards were unanimous on this point, and their collective authority on the subject is difficult to dismiss.

Trading & Investing Lessons

Risk Per Trade Is Your Survival Mechanism

Hite’s one-percent rule is not about maximizing returns—it is about ensuring you survive long enough for your edge to play out. A single catastrophic loss can eliminate you from the game before the law of large numbers has a chance to work in your favor. In practice, this means defining your maximum acceptable loss before entry and sizing the position accordingly.

Stops Must Be Based on Market Structure, Not Pain

O’Shea’s lesson is one of the most practical in the book: determine where your trade thesis is wrong, set the stop there, then calculate position size from how much you can afford to lose divided by the distance to the stop. Most traders reverse this process and place stops based on the dollar amount they are willing to lose, which is irrelevant to whether the trade premise has been invalidated.

Vary Position Size With Conviction

Thorp’s blackjack insight translates directly: bet bigger when the deck is favorable. Marcus traded five to six times his normal size when fundamentals, technicals, and market tone all aligned. The implication is that you should have a clear framework for distinguishing high-conviction from low-conviction setups.

Market Response Is a Leading Indicator

How the market reacts to news is more important than the news itself. A market that cannot rally on good news is weak. A market that refuses to fall on bad news is strong. This principle applies at every time frame and in every asset class.

Reduce Size When Losing AND When Winning

This is perhaps the most counterintuitive lesson. Reducing size during losing streaks prevents psychological spiral and capital destruction. Reducing size during winning streaks prevents the complacency and excessive exposure that typically precede the worst drawdowns.

Implementation Can Matter More Than the Idea

O’Shea’s post-bubble bond trade and Greenblatt’s Wells Fargo LEAPS trade both demonstrate that the vehicle and structure of a trade can determine whether a correct thesis produces profits or losses. Always ask: is there a better way to express this view?

Key Takeaways

There Is No Holy Grail

The search for the one correct methodology is not just futile—it reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of what trading success requires. The methodology must fit your personality, and what works for one trader may be disastrous for another.

Edge + Money Management = Success

Neither alone is sufficient. An edge without risk control will eventually blow up (Jones’s cotton trade, Kovner’s soybean trade). Risk control without an edge is just slow-motion losing (the roulette proof).

Psychology Is the Battlefield

Every chapter in this book is ultimately about psychology: the courage to persist through failure, the discipline to follow rules, the independence to ignore others, the flexibility to reverse positions, the patience to wait, the humility to take losses, the awareness that comfort-seeking is the enemy. Trading is a psychological endeavor that happens to use financial instruments.

The 80/20 Rule of Trading

Platt’s observation that eighty percent of profits come from twenty percent of ideas has profound implications. The primary job of a trader is not to generate profits on most trades, but to survive the eighty percent of trades that do not work so that you are present and properly positioned for the twenty percent that do.

Process Over Outcome

A good trade can lose money. A bad trade can make money. The only thing you can control is the process. If the process has a positive expectancy and you execute it with discipline, the outcomes will take care of themselves over time.

Contrarian Ideas

A Monkey Will Outperform Most Traders

Eckhardt’s claim is not the standard “monkeys do as well as experts” line. He is saying the monkey will do better because human biases systematically push decisions below random. Greenblatt’s Magic Formula experiment confirmed this empirically.

Win Rate Is the Least Important Statistic

Eckhardt argued that the percentage of winning trades may be inversely correlated with overall performance. The desire to maximize win rate encourages premature profit-taking, which destroys the ability to earn large gains that pay for the losers.

Reduce Size After Winning Streaks

Conventional advice says to increase size as your account grows. Multiple Market Wizards (Schwartz, Jones, Seykota) do the opposite, specifically because the worst losses tend to follow the best profits.

The Best Trade Can Be No Trade

Kevin Daly’s extraordinary record was built primarily on the trades he did not take—remaining in cash during bear markets while others suffered massive drawdowns. Doing nothing is an active and often optimal trading decision.

Being Right Is Not Enough

Druckenmiller’s 1982 T-bill trade and O’Shea’s post-bubble strategy both show that being analytically correct is insufficient. The structure, timing, and sizing of the trade must also be right.

Proposals & Frameworks

The Two-Question Self-Test

Before risking money: (1) What exactly is your methodology? (2) Does it provide an edge? If you cannot answer both with confidence, you are not ready to trade.

O’Shea’s Position Sizing Formula

Step 1: Decide where you are wrong (the stop level). Step 2: Decide how much you are willing to lose. Step 3: Divide amount willing to lose by per-contract loss to the stop. That is your position size.

The BlueCrest Risk Model

Each manager may lose up to 3% before capital is cut by 50%. Another 3% loss on remaining assets triggers complete withdrawal for the year. Maximum loss per manager: ~5%. But the 3% limit applies only to starting capital—accumulated profits expand the risk budget, keeping upside open-ended while protecting the base.

The Four Types of Trades

Winning trade, losing trade, good trade, bad trade. Good = positive-expectancy process regardless of single-trade outcome. Bad = gamble regardless of single-trade outcome. Judge yourself on good/bad, not win/lose.

The Losing-Streak Protocol

Step 1: Reduce position size to one-fifth or one-tenth of normal. Step 2: If still losing, stop trading entirely. Step 3: Take a break. Step 4: Return with very small positions. Step 5: Gradually increase as trading becomes effortless again. Step 6: Plot equity daily to catch drawdowns early.

What Didn’t Fit

The Interview That Was Killed

One of Schwager’s most fascinating interview subjects for The New Market Wizards discussed dreams, precognition, and Zen as they relate to trading. After weeks of editing the 200-page raw transcript into a polished chapter, the trader called to say his new business manager had vetoed publication—the material about dreams and Zen was not conducive to the desired corporate image. Schwager salvaged just a two-page anonymous section called “Zen and the Art of Trading.” The trader’s core insight survived: the perfect trade requires no effort.

John Bender’s Tragic End

Bender managed money for Soros’s Quantum Fund and achieved a 33% average annual return with only 6% maximum drawdown. In the final year of his fund (2000), he registered a 269% return by positioning for the dot- com top. He closed the fund after suffering a brain aneurysm, spent a decade buying rainforest acreage in Costa Rica, and died in 2010 from bipolar disorder- related suicide. His twenty-hours-a-day trading schedule serves as both inspiration and cautionary tale.

Steve Cohen and the SAC Controversy

Schwager included a footnote acknowledging that multiple SAC Capital employees pleaded guilty or were convicted of insider trading, and that the firm itself paid $1.8 billion in fines. Schwager noted that even if you cut Cohen’s returns in half, the record would still be exceptional, arguing that Cohen is a genuinely skilled trader regardless of what assumptions one might make about insider trading.

The Charting Contest That Proved Human Bias

One of Richard Dennis’s employees entered a year-end price prediction contest by simply using current prices as his predictions for every market. He finished in the top five out of hundreds of contestants, demonstrating that at least 95 percent of all entrants made predictions worse than simply guessing “prices stay the same.”

About the Author

Jack D. Schwager is one of the most recognized names in the trading world, known primarily for his Market Wizards series of books, which have become required reading for serious traders and investors since the first volume appeared in 1989. Across four books spanning a quarter century, Schwager interviewed 59 of the world’s most successful traders, creating an unparalleled archive of trading wisdom.

Beyond his interview work, Schwager has held positions as a director of futures research, a CTA, and a fund of hedge funds manager. He is also the author of several other books on trading and markets, including Market Sense and Nonsense and the three-volume Schwager on Futures series. His work bridges the gap between academic finance and the practical reality of markets in a way that few other authors have achieved.

Why This Book Matters

The Market Wizards series has shaped the thinking of an entire generation of traders. This volume serves as both an accessible entry point for new readers and a concentrated reference for those who have read the original interviews. Unlike most trading books, which focus on specific technical setups or systems, Schwager identifies the meta-principles that transcend any particular methodology—principles that have remained constant across decades of market evolution.

What makes this book particularly valuable is the sheer weight of evidence behind each principle. These are not theoretical claims or backtested hypotheses. They are lessons extracted from traders who collectively generated tens of billions of dollars in profits across stocks, bonds, currencies, commodities, and options. When dozens of the world’s greatest traders independently converge on the same principles through entirely different methodologies, those principles deserve serious attention from anyone who risks capital in the markets.
