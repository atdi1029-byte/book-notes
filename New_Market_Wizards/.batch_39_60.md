## Chapter: Bill Lipschutz Interview (Early Career & Currency Trading)

### Entry into Foreign Exchange

- **Bill Lipschutz** at Salomon Brothers was the only person in his currency options department with equities background when Philadelphia Stock Exchange introduced currency options contract
- The entire FX department came from fixed-income; he came from equities and knew specialists/market makers on the stock exchange floor
- Unique synergistic experience: Salomon's FX department, Lipschutz as trader, and currency options all started at the same time and grew together
- To enter without contacts: use **Hambros directory** (international FX dealer listings), find D-mark dealer in Hambros, cold call them

### The Nature of Foreign Exchange Trading

[MUST KNOW][N001] **FX trading is fundamentally about relationships and information access.** Not technical analysis or indicators. Lipschutz: "Foreign exchange is all about relationships. Your ability to find good liquidity, your ability to be plugged into the information flow—it all depends on relationships."

- **Concrete example of relationship value**: If you call a bank needing price on $10M mark and say "I need a price," they can delay or refuse. If you have a relationship and say "Hey, Joe, it's Bill, I need a price on the mark," the response is entirely different: "I was just on my way out the door, but for you I'll see what I can do."

- Being "plugged into the information flow" is an operational skill—you must maintain relationships, get calls at 5 P.M. when most are leaving, be the person who gets through when others don't

[SHOULD KNOW][N002] **Specialization in emerging products created competitive edge.** Lipschutz had background in options when it was new to the marketplace. Others assumed "He knows options, he must be a genius" (referencing Black-Scholes model). Senior FX traders wanted to meet him because their customers wanted to do options but they needed to catch up quickly. This created disproportionate access.

[SHOULD KNOW][N003] **Working for prestigious institution provided mystique advantage.** Salomon Brothers at the time generated aura of success—"We don't know what they do, but they make a lot of money." Lipschutz was not a pompous investment banker; he dressed casually and acted like FX traders (who were "go-out-for-pasta-and-dribble-marinara-sauce-all-over-yourself" types). This approachability made him different from stiff investment banker competitors, breaking down barriers.

[NICE TO KNOW][N004] Being first person at Salomon to have home Telerate screen (overnight monitoring of 24-hour FX market) was initially shocking to colleagues ("You want a screen at home? Are you out of your mind?") but demonstrated commitment and provided information advantage.

### Information Flow and Market Sentiment

[MUST KNOW][N005] **Being "plugged into the news" means actively understanding what the market is *currently* focused on, which changes from day to day.** Not about having information no one else has—it's about understanding what market participants are collectively focusing on at a given moment. This determines which direction capital flows and which currencies benefit.

[SHOULD KNOW][N006] **Market interpretation evolves; the consensus you could argue at announcement time is irrelevant.** When Berlin Wall came down, initial consensus was everyone would invest in East Germany, benefiting Deutsche mark. But within weeks, realization set in: rebuilding would be a long haul, East Germans didn't want to work as hard as West Germans, social costs would be enormous. Capital flows reversed—money moved *out* of Deutsche mark. 

- Mechanism: This shift happened through: 1) Kohl's political statements, 2) Baker's comments, 3) Statistics on East German unemployment, 4) East Germans' statements about not matching West German work ethic, 5) State's inability to fund medical bills anymore. Each data point shifted market interpretation.

- **The key insight**: "I don't think many people saw it that way at the time, and even if they did, that's not important. What is important is to assess what the market is focusing on at the given moment."

[NICE TO KNOW][N007] Different FX market focuses on different factors. One day might focus on interest rate differentials; the next day might focus on capital appreciation potential. The same fundamental data can lead to opposite trading conclusions depending on which framework is in focus.

- Example: Interest rate differential focus → invest in highest-yielding currencies. Capital appreciation focus → invest in countries with strongest economic/political outlook (often lower interest rates). These are opposites.

### Early Trading Example: Currency Options (1983)

[SHOULD KNOW][N008] **In specialist system, deep-in-the-money options can create risk-limited profit opportunities.** In 1983, a specialist quoted a particular option 100 points off. Lipschutz bought fifty contracts, immediately sold underlying currency forward, locking in risk-free profit. 

- **Definition for context**: Deep-in-the-money option = just like outright contract, but maximum risk is theoretically limited. If market had extreme move, maximum loss is defined.

[SHOULD KNOW][N009] **Specialists sometimes make arithmetic mistakes in their own pricing models.** Lipschutz's specialist quoted same price repeatedly, then Goldman Sachs entered and bought hundreds of options at that price. Specialist had clearly made computational error in his pricing. Once Goldman and Lipschutz bought 150+ contracts, specialist realized he'd lost his arbitrage profit and backed off.

[SHOULD KNOW][N010] **Willingness to enforce market conventions protects marketplace integrity.** When specialist was clearly wrong, Lipschutz could have kept buying. Instead, he recognized this would put specialist out of business and destroy the fledgling currency options market. He canceled trades to preserve the specialist's viability. This long-term business decision (protecting the marketplace) took precedence over short-term profit (holding him to a disastrous trade).

### Philosophy: Discipline and Rules

[SHOULD KNOW][N011] **Lipschutz's reputation is built on never cutting anyone a break.** "I have a reputation as being one of the most—if not the most—hard-assed players in the market. I never, ever, ever, ever cut anybody a break, because I figured that at Salomon everybody was trying to knock us off."

- Operational manifestation: Traders requesting exceptions for late exercises are always told no. "I tried to get through earlier. I'm only a few minutes late, couldn't you just make an exception?" Answer: No. Never.

- Reasoning: If Salomon allowed exceptions, competitors could exploit goodwill; no one would ever give Salomon a break either; rules apply equally.

[SHOULD KNOW][N012] **Zero-tolerance approach extends to back-office operations.** When working out management details with Merrill Lynch, asked how much budget for back-office errors, Lipschutz said "Zero." They asked in disbelief. He clarified: "We don't have back-office errors. If you put in enough fail-safes, you don't make errors."

[NICE TO KNOW][N013] This discipline caused some frustration among colleagues ("Lipschutz, why do you have to be such a hard-ass about everything?") but was consistent with Lipschutz's view: "These are the rules; that's the way the game is played."

### Market Conventions and Honest Attempts

[SHOULD KNOW][N014] **Interbank market convention: when a dealer quotes a price, there must be an "honest attempt" to execute. If market moves violently and quote is obviously off, protocol exists.** Convention is not "You're stuck with any price you quoted." It's "Are you sure? Please check your price. Do you want to deal or don't you?"

- If dealer responds "Yeah, yeah, I'm sure. Do you want to deal or don't you?" → the quote should stand.

- Lipschutz's approach: "I refused, because I had asked them to verify the price."

[SHOULD KNOW][N015] **Market conventions protect against opportunistic exploitation but also require good faith.** When a dealer quotes 1.9140 and Lipschutz thinks price should be 1.9240, the convention is to say "1.9140. Are you sure? Please check your price" and if they confirm, the trade stands. The convention prevents people from taking every obviously-wrong quote while still allowing honest errors to be exploited if dealer stands by their price after verification.

[NICE TO KNOW][N016] Lipschutz has experienced situations where other institutions wanted to cancel or split the difference after quoting wrong price. He always refused because he had asked for verification.

### The Mismspriced Bond Issue

[MUST KNOW][N017] **Interest rate differentials create currency forward premiums through arbitrage.** When U.K. rates are much lower than U.S. rates, forward sterling trades at huge premium to spot. Interest rate arbitrageurs ensure forward premiums are exactly large enough to offset the interest rate differential. If premium isn't there, arbitrageurs would borrow at low rate, invest at high rate, and buy forward currency at discount to hedge.

- **The resulting guarantee**: Forward premiums for currency with lower rates will be large enough to offset interest rate differential between the two countries.

[SHOULD KNOW][N018] **A bond issue can be mispriced if it doesn't account for this arbitrage relationship.** Lipschutz found a redemption bond (redeemable in sterling or USD) that assumed no premium on the sterling redemption option despite huge premium in the actual forward market. The bond pricing was: spot rate = $1.3470, but could be redeemed in sterling. Forward sterling 7-year was trading ~$1.47, 12-year ~$1.60. 

- **The arbitrage**: Buy the bond at the sterling redemption price ($1.3470 equivalent), sell sterling forward at market premium ($1.47-$1.60), lock in huge profit over the bond's life as forward converges to spot.

### The First Major Trade

- Total outstanding issue: $150 million in four tranches (5, 7, 9, 12-year maturities)

[SHOULD KNOW][N019] **Even when you identify mispricing, getting approval and position size takes time.** Initially couldn't buy issue—only $50M of first $100M tranche sold by the time approval came through. Took 1-2 years in secondary market, but with help of salesperson knowing original placement, accumulated $135M of $150M total.

[NICE TO KNOW][N020] **Hedging creates optionality.** Sold 50% of position in forward sterling market at huge premium ($1.47-$1.60 range vs. $1.3470 spot). This created a straddle-like position: half the position was a call on sterling (bond was) and half was a put (forward sale). Locked-in profit if pounds stayed near $1.3470, even bigger profit if pounds moved above forward price.

[SHOULD KNOW][N021] **Interest rate regime changes can invalidate the entire trade thesis.** U.K. interest rates eventually moved above U.S. rates (reversed from below), causing sterling forward rates to invert from premium to discount to spot. Lipschutz covered the whole position at huge profit.

### Information During G-7 Meeting (September 1985)

- **Context**: G-7 meeting involved "Plaza Accord"—coordinated policy to lower dollar value

[MUST KNOW][N022] **Major policy shifts often happen without clear market awareness beforehand.** G-7 September 1985 was the first coordinated policy statement from seven industrialized nations. But few anticipated it. The initial market reaction (dollar down) was small compared to subsequent decline. Lipschutz was on vacation in Sardinia, completely unaware.

[NICE TO KNOW][N023] **Information access advantage: Lipschutz's London office had better real-time information flow than he did from Sardinia.** Tried reaching his office in NYC with great difficulty. Eventually reached London office. The effort required (international phone lines, 2-hour delay) shows why 24-hour market presence and relationships matter.

[SHOULD KNOW][N024] **Understanding what constitutes a major policy shift requires being part of market conversations.** Lipschutz wasn't even aware the G-7 meeting was happening. The meeting itself was "just a bunch of bureaucrats getting together to talk down the value of the dollar" from his perspective at the time.

### The 1987 Yen Spread Trade (Poker Game)

[MUST KNOW][N025] **In enormous positions with ambiguous hedging, market makers must manage position uncertainty.** Lipschutz had 23,000 Japanese yen 54 calls (long) and 23,000 yen 55 calls (short)—a bull call spread. Position was worth nearly $800 million if both expired in the money. 

- **The structure**: Bought 54 calls (unlimited upside but costs premium), sold 55 calls (income offsets cost, but caps upside at +1 point per contract).

- **Maximum profit**: Only $625/contract ($13M total if market above 55 at expiration), but substantially lower cost than outright call purchase.

[SHOULD KNOW][N026] **With positions this large, hedging at expiration creates massive risk.** Can't liquidate 23,000-lot in final hours without huge market impact. If he assumes market settles above 55 and hedges the long 54 call, but market instead settles between 54-55, he ends up with naked $800M long position over weekend (until Tokyo opens Sunday). If market settles below 54, and he hedges for below-55 scenario, he might end up naked short $800M position.

[NICE TO KNOW][N027] **Fast market conditions on exchanges can create multi-price execution.** Floor traders were bidding higher, but market traded at multiple levels simultaneously. Market moved down 200+ points in minutes, floor traders went for stops below, market continued lower. This left his orders unfilled at higher levels even though market temporarily traded there.

### The Poker Game Dynamics

[MUST KNOW][N028] **Large positions create information asymmetries between counterparties—each tries to deduce the other's hedging status.** Lipschutz didn't know if market maker on opposite side had hedged their short 54 calls or planned to exercise their long 55 calls. Opposite party didn't know if Lipschutz had hedged or planned to exercise. Neither could figure out the other's position until Monday (when they'd get notification of exercises).

[SHOULD KNOW][N029] **Market maker exploited this ambiguity by buying in the cash market.** They tipped their hand by buying yen Friday afternoon. This revealed they hadn't hedged their short 54 call and had no intention of exercising the long 55 call. Lipschutz knew their likely position but couldn't capitalize before market opened Sunday in New Zealand.

[SHOULD KNOW][N030] **Conveying false information can be a trading tactic.** By telling the market maker he'd tipped his hand Friday (buying yen), Lipschutz created impression he'd figured out their position and hedged accordingly (when he actually hadn't). This caused the market maker to call Saturday and say "We're not going to exercise"—letting Lipschutz off the hook.

[SHOULD KNOW][N031] **Information revelation sequencing matters in multilateral markets.** Trading happens Friday in US, Saturday communication between parties, Sunday/Monday in Asia. The sequence of who knows what becomes the trading advantage. Lipschutz's advantage: he could infer their hedging status from Friday actions before they knew he'd figured them out.

[NICE TO KNOW][N032] **The profit came from psychological positioning, not from market movement.** Final profit: ~$20 million. But what made it special was "the mental chess game that Friday afternoon—all the back-and-forth bluffing. People were calling my desk all Friday afternoon to ask us what was going on between us and the other firm."

### The Gorbachev Speech Crisis (Fall 1988)

[MUST KNOW][N033] **Low-volatility periods can accumulate outsized position risk.** D-mark trading in "very tight trading range" in fall 1988. Lipschutz was short $3 billion in a sideways market, taking incremental risk to capture small moves. At low volatility, traders typically increase position size to maintain same dollar results.

[SHOULD KNOW][N034] **Unpredictable geopolitical events create flash risk in large positions.** Gorbachev made a speech at UN (only knew about it in advance that he'd make "a speech"—didn't know content) interpreting his statements as major reductions in USSR troop commitments. Market interpreted as highly bullish for dollar (fewer military threats = stronger currency). Dollar began moving up sharply with "no liquidity"—very quickly up 1%, then accelerating.

[SHOULD KNOW][N035] **Position size can turn a normal market shock into a crisis.** At 1% move on $3B position = $30M loss in afternoon—after expecting small moves due to tight range. Lipschutz: "I knew that I was in trouble."

[NICE TO KNOW][N036] **Even traders at major institutions experience fear.** Lipschutz: "I can tell you about the one time since I first started trading that I was really scared." The emotion is real even with sophisticated risk management.

### Stop-Picking in Exchange-Traded Markets

[SHOULD KNOW][N039] **"Fast market" conditions on exchanges create structural opportunity for stop-hunting.** A trader had built up a large long position in a narrow range. Stops were placed 40-50 ticks above this range. On a day with expected low liquidity (holiday affecting cash market), the plan was to sell heavily to trigger stops and then reverse (artificial rally followed by sell-off).

- During the morning, market "trades sideways and nothing happens." Around 1 P.M., prices start moving down rapidly.

[SHOULD KNOW][N040] **Price can trade at multiple levels simultaneously during fast market conditions.** Market fell 50, 100, 200+ points within minutes. Floor traders went for the stops below the market (which were 200 points away). But Lipschutz's friend was bidding aggressively hundreds of contracts between 100-200 points lower, filling only 50 contracts even though market traded 200+ points lower with "couple thousand lots" at those levels.

- **Why**: During fast market, designated market maker/exchange participants have "special license to steal above and beyond their normal license to steal."

[NICE TO KNOW][N041] Lipschutz notes he can't prove this always happens in open outcry markets, but observes it "sometimes occurs in some open outcry markets."

### Technical Details of Yen Spread

[NICE TO KNOW][N042] In bull call spread: 23,000 long 54 calls + 23,000 short 55 calls. If both expire in money, each spread position yields $625 profit ($13M total). Maximum potential profit would be if market closes anywhere above 55. But the short 55 call limits gain to 1 point per contract.

[NICE TO KNOW][N043] Cost structure: selling the 55 call for premium income substantially reduces the cost of buying the 54 call, making the position capital-efficient compared to outright call purchase.

### Weekend Position Uncertainty Dynamics

[MUST KNOW][N044] **In international markets with non-overlapping sessions, position holders don't know overnight exercise status until Sunday.** Friday expiration in US, but next market to open is New Zealand Sunday. Position holder doesn't know if counterparty exercised their long calls until Sunday morning notification. This creates unhedgeable overnight risk.

[SHOULD KNOW][N045] **When price settles near a strike at expiration, assignment uncertainty becomes largest risk.** Price at 55 strike level (between 54 and 55 stops) creates maximum ambiguity about which counterparty gets assigned what. Market maker doesn't know if Lipschutz hedged long 54 call; Lipschutz doesn't know if they exercised long 55 call.

[SHOULD KNOW][N046] **Reading market participant actions in one market (cash yen purchase Friday afternoon) can reveal hedging status.** Market maker's cash purchase of yen revealed they hadn't hedged short 54 calls and wouldn't exercise long 55 calls. This gave Lipschutz actionable intelligence before Asian markets opened, where he could have been pushed against.

### The Poker Game Resolution

[NICE TO KNOW][N047] Saturday phone call from market maker asking position status was attempt to "sniff out" Lipschutz's hedging status. By that point, Lipschutz could convey (via mention of their Friday yen purchase) that he understood their position, which was false inference protection.

[NICE TO KNOW][N048] Final profit of $20M was substantially increased because dollar opened sharply lower vs. yen in the New Zealand/Australian market openings on Sunday, moving against the yen position.

### Gorbachev Speech Trade Outcome

[SHOULD KNOW][N049] **Initial $3B short D-mark position was put at risk by unpredictable geopolitical event.** The speech content was unknown in advance, making advance hedging impossible. Position grew through small incremental trading in a narrow range, without hedging the tail risk of major news event.

[NICE TO KNOW][N050] The 1% move represented 1 percent of $3B position, resulting in $30M loss that afternoon. Speed of move ("very quickly") meant potential for much worse outcome if move continued or if position was larger.

[NICE TO KNOW][N051] The trade demonstrates that even well-managed traders maintain positions vulnerable to tail risk when volatility is expected to remain low. The assumption of continued low volatility turned out to be wrong.

### Final Trade Outcome

[NICE TO KNOW][N052] Yen position final outcome: The market maker's weekend behavior (buying yen on Friday) telegraphed their trading intention. Combined with subsequent market movement (dollar opening lower vs. yen), Lipschutz's position profited despite initial hedging uncertainty. The $20M profit came from not just the directional move but from superior information about counterparty intentions.

[SHOULD KNOW][N053] **Highest-stakes trading often involves multiple layers of uncertainty: position-level risk, counterparty hedging status, overnight session risk, and geopolitical tail risk.** Even sophisticated traders at major institutions encounter moments of genuine fear when multiple uncertainties compound on large positions.
