### INTRODUCTION
- [MUST KNOW] N008 The U.S. story is an economic AND political miracle made possible by two sets of ideas published in 1776: Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (voluntary exchange, invisible hand, free markets) and the Declaration of Independence (individual rights, limited government). These are the twin pillars of the entire book.
- [MUST KNOW] N009 Adam Smith's key insight: if exchange is strictly voluntary, it will not take place unless both parties benefit. No coercion needed for cooperation. "By pursuing his own interest he frequently promotes that of the society more effectually than when he really intends to promote it."
- [MUST KNOW] N010 John Stuart Mill's liberty principle: the only justification for interfering with someone's freedom is to prevent harm to others. "Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N011 Economic freedom is essential for political freedom — it disperses power, offsets concentrations of political power. Combining economic and political power in the same hands is "a sure recipe for tyranny."
- [MUST KNOW] N012 The golden age of 19th century freedom produced unprecedented prosperity. But ironically, success bred complacency. People forgot the danger of concentrated government power and began to see government as a tool for good. The Great Depression (caused by government failure in monetary policy, per Ch. 3) was misinterpreted as a failure of capitalism, accelerating the shift toward collectivism.
- [MUST KNOW] N013 The "invisible hand in government" — Friedman's brilliant inversion of Smith: in government, an individual who intends to serve the public interest by fostering intervention is "led by an invisible hand to promote private interests, which was no part of his intention." Government programs consistently produce results opposite to their stated goals.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N014 Friedman references Hayek's *The Road to Serfdom* — we haven't reached the point of no return yet. We are still free to choose whether to continue down the road to serfdom or reverse course.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N015 Agriculture example: at Declaration of Independence, 19 of 20 workers in agriculture. By 1979, fewer than 1 in 20 feeds 220 million people. Not central planning — nations relying on central direction (Russia, China, India) employ 25-50% of workers in agriculture yet can't avoid starvation. The agricultural revolution was driven by private initiative in free markets.

### CHAPTER 1: THE POWER OF THE MARKET
- [MUST KNOW] N016 Two methods of coordinating economic activity: (1) Command — the army method, general to colonel to private. Works only in small groups. Even armies supplement commands with voluntary cooperation. (2) Voluntary exchange — the market method. More subtle but far more fundamental for large-scale coordination.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N017 Even the Soviet Union relies heavily on voluntary market elements. Private plots = less than 1% of agricultural land but produce ~1/3 of total farm output. In labor markets, workers aren't ordered to specific jobs — they apply and choose, much like capitalist countries. Moonlighting is pervasive: a Moscow resident waits months for state repair but hires a moonlighter (likely a state employee) who fixes it promptly. Both are happy.
- [MUST KNOW] N018 Voluntary exchange is a NECESSARY condition for both prosperity and freedom. No society has achieved either without it as the dominant organizing principle. Not sufficient — but necessary.
- [MUST KNOW] N019 "I, Pencil" by Leonard Read — no single person on earth knows how to make a pencil. Wood from Oregon cedar, graphite from Ceylon, brass ferrule from zinc and copper mines, eraser from Dutch East Indies rapeseed oil + sulfur chloride. Thousands of people across many countries cooperate without knowing each other, speaking different languages, practicing different religions, maybe even hating each other — yet the pencil gets made. No central office gave orders. The price system coordinated it all.

#### The Role of Prices
- [MUST KNOW] N020 Prices perform THREE functions: (1) transmit information, (2) provide incentives to act on that information, (3) determine distribution of income. All three are closely interrelated.
- [MUST KNOW] N021 Transmission of information: prices tell only the people who NEED to know, only the information they need. Wood producers don't need to know if pencil demand rose because of a baby boom or 14,000 new government forms. They just need to know someone will pay more for wood and the higher price will last. Current prices + futures prices provide both signals.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N022 The price system automatically solves the information-routing problem: people who transmit info have incentive to find those who can use it; people who can use it have incentive to get it. No one clogs anyone's inbox with irrelevant data.
- [MUST KNOW] N023 Gasoline lines of 1974/1979 (OPEC embargo): Germany and Japan, wholly dependent on imported oil, had NO gas lines. The U.S. did — because government price controls prevented the price system from functioning. Prices were held below market-clearing levels, supplies allocated by bureaucratic command rather than price signals. Result: surpluses in some areas, shortages + long lines in others.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N024 OPEC cartel distorted price information — the quadrupled oil price didn't reflect a physical supply reduction but a cartel arrangement. U.S. price controls then prevented even this distorted signal from reaching consumers, strengthening the cartel and requiring a $10B Department of Energy with 20,000 employees.
- [MUST KNOW] N025 Inflation introduces "static" into price signals. When the price of wood rises, producers can't tell if it's general inflation or genuine increased demand for wood. The information that matters is RELATIVE prices (one item vs. another). High, variable inflation drowns that signal in noise.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N026 Incentives: prices provide both the incentive AND the means to act on information. A higher price of wood gives producers both the reason to produce more and the revenue to do so. The beauty of the system is that information, incentive, and means are bundled together.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N027 Subtle price effects cascade: OPEC oil price rise → higher chainsaw operating costs → slight shift toward handsaws → effect on diesel vs. gasoline trucks for logging → higher relative wood prices → higher pencil prices → consumers economize on pencils. An infinite chain of adjustments, all automatic.

#### Distribution of Income
- [SHOULD KNOW] N028 In the U.S., ~3/4 of all income from market transactions is employee compensation (wages + supplements). About half the rest is proprietor income (mix of personal services + owned capital). Human capital is the major productive resource.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N029 Physical capital accumulation (factories, roads, machines) was essential for growth, but human capital (knowledge, skills, health) has been equally important. The two reinforce each other. Return to human capital has risen faster than return to physical capital — and human capital is harder/costlier to maintain.
- [MUST KNOW] N030 You CANNOT separate the three functions of prices. If income doesn't depend on what you produce, why work hard? Why search for the buyer who values your goods most? Why save? Why maintain capital? If prices can't affect income distribution, the only alternative is command — some authority decides who does what and gets what. The Red Adair example: if his income is the same whether he caps a runaway oil well or not, why risk his life?
- [SHOULD KNOW] N031 Communist countries proved this — they couldn't separate income from prices even in theory. A Hungarian Marxist economist rediscovered Adam Smith's invisible hand and tried to use prices for information/efficiency but NOT for income distribution. "Needless to say, he failed in theory, as the communist countries have failed in practice."
- [MUST KNOW] N032 When everybody owns something, nobody owns it — and nobody maintains it. Buildings in the Soviet Union look decrepit within a year or two, government factory machines break down constantly, citizens resort to the black market for repairs. This is the tragedy of communal ownership.

#### A Broader View
- [SHOULD KNOW] N033 The invisible hand operates far beyond economics. Language developed the same way — no central planner decided grammar rules. Meanings were attributed to words as needs arose, codified later by dictionaries. The French Academy is a late development that mostly rubber-stamps changes it can't control. Scientific knowledge follows the same pattern — disciplines "just growed" because scholars found them convenient, not because anyone planned them.
- [MUST KNOW] N034 Self-interest is NOT myopic selfishness. It's whatever interests the participants — the scientist advancing knowledge, the missionary converting infidels, the philanthropist bringing comfort. All are pursuing THEIR interests as THEY see them. Economics does not assume "economic man" who responds only to monetary stimuli.

#### The Role of Government
- [MUST KNOW] N035 Adam Smith's three duties of government (Friedman adds a fourth): (1) Protect society from external violence/invasion. (2) Protect every member from injustice/oppression by other members — includes courts, justice system. (3) Erect/maintain public works that private enterprise wouldn't (roads, bridges — because toll collection costs exceed benefits). (4) [Friedman's addition] Protect those who cannot be "responsible individuals" — children, the insane.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N036 The third duty is the most dangerous — it can justify almost anything via "externalities" / third-party effects. But government measures ALSO have third-party effects. "Government failure" is no less real than "market failure." The burden of proof should be on those proposing government intervention, requiring a clear balance of benefits over costs.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N037 Every government measure is "a smokestack on its back" — it produces its own externalities through taxes, power accretion, and opportunities for citizens to exploit each other through government.
- [MUST KNOW] N038 Once government undertakes an activity, it is seldom terminated. Failure leads not to abolition but to expansion and larger budgets. This is a crucial asymmetry between government and markets.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N039 Tipping at restaurants is a voluntary mechanism for handling third-party effects — you ensure good service for strangers, and strangers' tips ensure good service for you. Many externalities can be handled without government.

#### Limited Government in Practice
- [MUST KNOW] N040 Hong Kong as the modern exemplar of free markets: <400 sq miles, 4.5 million people, 14x Japan's density, 185x U.S. density — yet one of the highest living standards in Asia. No tariffs, no minimum wage, no price fixing, no government direction of industry. Government spending among lowest in world as fraction of income. Low taxes preserve incentives — businessmen reap benefits AND bear costs of mistakes. Ironic that a British Crown colony followed policies opposite to Britain's welfare state.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N041 Britain after 1846 Corn Laws repeal: 3/4 century of complete free trade, highly limited government. Economic growth was rapid, living standards improved dramatically, government spending fell from ~25% to ~10% of national income by Queen Victoria's Jubilee (1897) — Britain's apex of power.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N042 U.S. 1800-1929: government spending never exceeded 12% of national income (excluding wars). 2/3 was state/local, mostly schools and roads. Federal spending = ~3% of national income in 1928. Immigration was essentially free until after WWI. The "robber baron" myth misrepresents an era of massive charitable activity — SPCA, YMCA, Salvation Army, privately financed schools, colleges, hospitals, orphanages, museums, opera houses all date from this period.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N043 Declining farm prices were a sign of SUCCESS, not exploitation — they reflected mechanization, more land under cultivation, improved communication. Proof: farmland prices rose steadily. Immigrants kept coming for decades because the promise was real — hard work brought rewards unimaginable in the Old World.

### CHAPTER 2: THE TYRANNY OF CONTROLS
- [MUST KNOW] N044 Adam Smith on free trade: "What is prudence in the conduct of every private family, can scarce be folly in that of a great kingdom. If a foreign country can supply us with a commodity cheaper than we ourselves can make it, better buy it of them..." Free trade has near-universal support among economists regardless of ideology, yet tariffs have been the rule throughout history.
- [MUST KNOW] N045 The "interested sophistry" problem: we each rail against "special interests" except when the special interest is our own. Every person believes what's good for them is good for the country. The result: a maze of restrictions that makes almost everyone worse off. "We lose far more from measures that serve other 'special interests' than we gain from measures that serve our 'special interest.'"
- [SHOULD KNOW] N046 Economic controls extend beyond trade to domestic life — restricting freedom of speech, press, and religion. Japan after Meiji Restoration (1867) vs. India after independence (1947) is a key comparison: free trade at home and abroad is the best path for poor countries.

#### International Trade
- [SHOULD KNOW] N047 Economists have near-unanimity on free trade since Adam Smith, yet tariffs persist. Only major exceptions: Britain's century of free trade (1846-WWI) and Hong Kong.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N048 Smoot-Hawley tariff of 1930 — some scholars regard it as partly responsible for the severity of the subsequent depression. Tariffs reduced by international agreements since then, but remain high.

#### The Economic Case for Free Trade
- [MUST KNOW] N049 Three key trade fallacies Friedman demolishes: (1) "Creating jobs" is the goal — wrong, the goal is PRODUCTIVE jobs. We can create any number of jobs by digging holes and filling them. (2) "Exports are good, imports bad" — the OPPOSITE is true. We can't eat, wear, or enjoy exports. Our gain is what we IMPORT. Exports are the price we pay. (3) "Favorable balance of trade" (exporting more than importing) — in your household, you'd surely prefer to pay less for more, yet that's an "unfavorable" balance.
- [MUST KNOW] N050 The exchange rate argument: even if Japan could make EVERYTHING cheaper, the yen/dollar rate would adjust until trade balanced. If Japanese goods were cheaper at 360 yen/$, Japanese exporters wouldn't want dollars (nothing to buy). The yen price of dollars would fall until average imports = average exports. Exchange rates are PRICES and perform the same functions as all prices.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N051 The "unfair low-wage competition" fallacy: American workers are paid in dollars, Japanese in yen. High-wage American workers are NOT threatened as a GROUP. If an American worker is 1.5x as productive, his wages buy ~1.5x as much. What matters is COMPARATIVE advantage — even if we're better at everything, we should concentrate on what we do BEST. The lawyer who types twice as fast as his secretary should still practice law, not type.
- [MUST KNOW] N052 Comparative advantage (coined 150+ years ago): even if one country is more efficient at everything, both benefit from specializing in what they do relatively best. It's wasteful for America to produce anything where it's less than 1.5x as efficient as Japan.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N053 Foreign subsidies argument: if Japan subsidizes steel exports to the U.S., who benefits? U.S. consumers — they get cheap steel. The Japanese taxpayers suffer. "Was it noble of the United States to send goods as gifts in the form of Marshall Plan aid, but ignoble for foreign countries to send us gifts in the indirect form of goods sold below cost?" Subsidies are temporary — when they end, the market readjusts.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N054 National security argument for tariffs: steel industry has never presented cost estimates for alternative ways of providing national security (e.g., stockpiling). Until they do, it's just self-interest dressed up as patriotism. "Sincerity is a much overrated virtue."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N055 "Defend the dollar" argument is wholly artificial. If exchange rates float freely, they perform the same information/incentive functions as all other prices. Dollar weakness = U.S. inflated more than other countries. Governments intervene in exchange markets to hide the consequences of their own inflationary policies. Small disturbances accumulate into major crises.
- [MUST KNOW] N056 Only THREE tariff arguments have any theoretical validity: (1) National security — rarely justified in practice. (2) Infant industry (Hamilton's argument) — "the so-called infants never grow up." Once imposed, tariffs are seldom eliminated. Used to protect established industries, not true unborn infants. (3) Beggar-thy-neighbor / monopoly-monopsony power — valid in theory but invites retaliation.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N057 The retaliation argument ("we need tariffs because others have them") is pure masochism and sadism. Other countries' tariffs hurt THEM. If we retaliate, we add harm to ourselves AND them. "Competition in masochism and sadism is hardly a prescription for sensible international economic policy!"
- [MUST KNOW] N058 Friedman's proposal: unilateral free trade, phased in over 5 years at an announced pace. "Few measures we could take would do more to promote the cause of freedom at home and abroad."

#### The Political Case for Free Trade
- [MUST KNOW] N059 Free trade fosters peace. The century from Waterloo to WWI — when Britain led with near-complete free trade — was one of the most peaceful in human history. People traveled Europe without passports, emigrated freely. When governments intervene in trade, private disputes become inter-governmental disputes. Conflict, not cooperation, becomes the rule.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N060 Free trade eliminates domestic monopolies better than antitrust laws ever could. With only 3 U.S. automakers (one near bankruptcy), monopoly pricing is a threat. Let world automakers compete, and the threat vanishes. Only De Beers diamonds has maintained a world monopoly without government assistance.

#### Central Economic Planning
- [MUST KNOW] N061 Friedman's universal observation: wherever individual freedom exists, economic activity is organized through free markets and progress follows. Wherever the state controls economic activity in detail, citizens have low living standards, are in political fetters, and have little power over their own destiny. The state may produce impressive monuments; the ordinary citizens are instruments.
- [MUST KNOW] N062 East vs. West Germany — the ultimate controlled experiment. Same people, same blood, same civilization, same technical skill. One side erects walls with armed guards, dogs, and minefields to keep citizens IN. The other side: brightly lit streets, free newspapers, people speaking without looking over their shoulders.
- [MUST KNOW] N063 Ludwig Erhard's German economic miracle: on Sunday, June 20, 1948, he simultaneously introduced the Deutsche Mark and abolished almost all controls on wages and prices. He acted on a Sunday because the French, American, and British occupation offices were closed — he was sure they'd countermand his orders. Within days, shops were full of goods. Within months, the economy was humming.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N064 Russia vs. Yugoslavia: both communist, but Yugoslavia under Tito broke with Stalin and moved toward decentralization. Private agricultural land, free markets for produce, small private enterprises (<5 employees). Not free, lower living standard than Austria — but paradise compared to Russia.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N065 Far East market economies (Malaysia, Singapore, Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Japan): income per person $700-$5,000 in late 1970s. Central planning economies (India, Indonesia, China): income per person under $250. The contrast is stark.

#### Japan vs. India
- [MUST KNOW] N066 The closest thing to a controlled experiment in economic policy. Both ancient civilizations, sophisticated cultures, hierarchical societies (Japan: daimyos/serfs; India: Brahmans/untouchables). Both experienced political revolutions led by dedicated leaders determined to transform their countries. India actually had MORE advantages: British-trained civil service, modern factories, railroad system, substantial pre-WWI growth, more foreign aid, superior physical resources (9x Japan's size, more arable land). Japan was isolated for 3+ centuries, had no foreign capital, no one who spoke Western languages.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N067 Japan's only lucky break: European silk crop failure after Meiji Restoration let Japan earn foreign exchange through silk exports. Otherwise, Japan was entirely self-financed.
- [MUST KNOW] N068 Outcome: Japan dismantled feudalism, extended opportunity, improved living standards rapidly, became a world power. India paid lip service to eliminating caste but inequality WIDENED. Population exploded in both — but Japan's growth offset it while India's barely kept pace.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N069 Cultural explanations are wrong. In 1867, early Western residents in Japan wrote: "Wealthy we do not think it [Japan] will ever become." Indians were called "unenterprising and slothful" — yet Indians abroad (Malaya, Hong Kong, Fiji, Panama, UK) are dynamic entrepreneurs. The characteristics attributed to Indians REFLECT rather than CAUSE the lack of progress. A fatalistic philosophy is an accommodation to stagnation, not a cause.
- [MUST KNOW] N070 Hong Kong experiment: Chinese refugees who fled communism in 1949 showed incredible initiative and drive. A new wave of immigrants from Red China (same race, culture) 30 years later showed little initiative — "they want to be told precisely what to do." The difference: 30 years under communism destroyed entrepreneurial instincts. A few years in Hong Kong's free market would change that.
- [MUST KNOW] N071 The great irony: Meiji rulers — who valued national POWER over individual freedom — adopted free-market policies (like the Britain of their era). India's leaders — who ardently valued individual FREEDOM — adopted central planning (like the Britain of THEIR era, post-WWII socialist Britain). Objectives and policies were disconnected. Japan's power-seeking rulers accidentally created freedom; India's freedom-seeking leaders accidentally created control.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N072 Japan's government role was limited: sent students abroad, imported experts, established pilot plants, subsidized some industries. But never tried to control total investment, structure of output, or direction of development. State-retained industries (shipbuilding, iron/steel for military) were a DRAIN on resources. An international treaty prohibiting tariffs >5% for the first 30 years proved an "unmitigated boon."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N073 India's controls: Russian-type five-year plans, government permits required to build a factory, tariffs/quotas on imports, subsidies on exports, foreign exchange controls, controlled wages/prices. Result: smuggling, black markets, illegal transactions — "every bit as ubiquitous as taxes." These black markets performed a "valuable social service" by offsetting the rigidity of central planning.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N074 Textile comparison: Japan let factory production destroy home spinning/weaving naturally — factory textiles started at lowest grade, moved up, became a major export. India SUBSIDIZED hand-loom weaving, holding back factory production. Hand looms DOUBLED from 1948-1978. Britain faced the same problem in the early 19th century and chose Japan's approach — "the temporarily harsh but ultimately beneficent policy of letting market forces work."

#### Controls and Freedom
- [MUST KNOW] N075 "Personal Independence Day" — the day you stop working to pay government and start earning for yourself. In 1929 it was February 12 (Lincoln's birthday). By 1979 it was May 30. If trends continued, it would coincide with July 4 around 1988.
- [MUST KNOW] N076 The ballot box produces conformity WITHOUT unanimity; the marketplace produces unanimity WITHOUT conformity. When you vote, you get a package — items you favor AND oppose. When you shop, you get EXACTLY what you chose. Use the ballot box only where conformity is essential.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N077 Pervasive restrictions on economic freedom: can't practice law, medicine, plumbing without a government license. Can't work overtime on terms you and your employer agree to. Can't start a bank, taxi, airline, railroad without permission. SEC prospectus approval costs $100,000+ — discouraging the small firms government claims to help.
- [MUST KNOW] N078 Corporate tax at 46% (1979) means the federal government owns 46% of every corporation — takes 46 cents of every profit dollar, shares 46 cents of every loss dollar. "In terms of the ownership of corporate enterprise, we are about 46 percent socialist."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N079 Economic controls create a "chilling effect" on free speech. Oil executives tongue-lashed by Senator Henry Jackson for "obscene profits" — not one fought back or left the room. Energy Secretary Schlesinger issued ultimatums: "Support the Administration's proposed tax on crude oil — or else face tougher regulation and a possible drive to break up the oil companies." Academics dependent on NSF/NEH grants self-censor. Freedom is ONE WHOLE — restricting it in one area affects all others.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N080 Religious freedom violated: Amish farmers had property seized for refusing Social Security taxes (which they also refused to accept). Church schools cited students as truants because teachers lacked state certification.

### CHAPTER 3: THE ANATOMY OF CRISIS
- [MUST KNOW] N081 The Great Depression: income cut in half by 1933, output fell by 1/3, unemployment hit 25%. It spread worldwide — in Germany it helped Hitler rise to power; in Japan it strengthened the military clique; in China it accelerated hyperinflation and brought the communists to power.
- [MUST KNOW] N082 The depression was NOT caused by a failure of private enterprise. It was caused by a failure of GOVERNMENT in the one area it had been responsible for since the founding — money. The Constitution (Section 8, Article 1) assigns Congress the power "To coin money, regulate the Value thereof."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N083 Keynesian revolution: the depression discredited the pre-existing view that monetary policy was a potent stabilizer. Opinion swung to "money does not matter." Keynes offered an alternative theory that justified government intervention — captured the economics profession and gave intellectual cover to expanding government.

#### The Origin of the Federal Reserve System
- [MUST KNOW] N084 Panic of 1907: Knickerbocker Trust Company run (Oct 21, 1907) → cascading bank runs → banks restricted payments (wouldn't pay cash on demand). At the height of the shortage, $104 in deposits was needed to buy $100 in currency. The restriction of payments was drastic but WORKED — it cut short bank failures and ended the runs. Recovery began within months; the recession lasted only 13 months.
- [MUST KNOW] N085 Fractional reserve banking explained: banks keep only a few dollars of cash per $100 of deposits. Works fine as long as everyone is confident. If everyone tries to withdraw at once — a panic, like crying "fire" in a crowded theater. In 1907, banks had only $12 of cash per $100 of deposits. Every $1 of deposits converted to cash forced $7 of additional deposit reduction (to maintain the reserve ratio).
- [SHOULD KNOW] N086 Federal Reserve Act (1913): created 12 regional Reserve Banks as "lenders of last resort." They could print Federal Reserve Notes or create deposit credits — an "emergency printing press." Key mechanism became "open market operations": buying government bonds injects "high-powered money" into the system; selling bonds contracts it. No effective limit except the discretion of Fed officials (gold limit was removed).
- [SHOULD KNOW] N087 FDIC (1934) — the method that actually ended banking panics. Depositors no longer need to run because deposits are guaranteed. Since 1934, there have been bank failures but NO banking panics of the old style.

#### The Early Years of the Reserve System
- [SHOULD KNOW] N088 WWI transformed the Fed from a minor body reacting to events into a major force shaping world monetary structure. The U.S. replaced Britain as the center of the financial world. The Fed facilitated war finance through a "roundabout process" — buying Treasury bonds, creating deposits, which became commercial bank reserves, enabling banks to buy more bonds. "The subtlety of the process did not prevent inflation, but it did smooth the operation and conceal what was actually happening."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N089 Post-war, the Fed continued inflating (now financing private business, not war). 1/3 of total wartime inflation occurred AFTER the war. Belatedly discovering the error, the Fed over-corrected sharply, causing the severe but short 1920-21 depression.
- [MUST KNOW] N090 Benjamin Strong — head of NY Federal Reserve Bank from inception until his death in 1928 — was the key figure. Described as "a genius — a Hamilton among bankers." Under his leadership in the 1920s, the Fed performed well as a balance wheel, keeping inflation low and growth stable. It was widely trumpeted that the business cycle was dead.
- [MUST KNOW] N091 Strong's death unleashed a power struggle between the NY Fed and the Washington Board. "Strong's death left the System with no center of enterprising and acceptable leadership." The Board was "still weak and divided." Other Reserve Banks were "reluctant to follow." The System slid into "indecision and deadlock" — with catastrophic consequences.

#### The Onset of Depression
- [MUST KNOW] N092 The stock market crash (Black Thursday, Oct 24, 1929) was important but NOT the beginning. Business activity peaked in August 1929. The crash reflected growing economic difficulties plus the puncturing of a speculative bubble. The Fed — instead of expanding the money supply as in past recessions — allowed it to decline 2.6% through October 1930. By comparison with the 1/3 decline that followed, this seemed mild, but it was larger than in most previous recessions.

#### Banking Crises
- [MUST KNOW] N093 Bank of United States — December 11, 1930: the largest commercial bank ever to fail in U.S. history at that time. It was actually a SOUND bank (eventually paid depositors 83.5 cents on the dollar). Its name misled people into thinking it was an official bank. The rescue plan failed because the New York Clearing House withdrew at the last moment — partly due to anti-Semitism (the bank was Jewish-owned and served the Jewish community). NY Superintendent of Banks Broderick warned: "I told them they were making the most colossal mistake in the banking history of New York."
- [MUST KNOW] N094 352 banks failed in December 1930 ALONE. Had the Fed never been established, the 1907 solution (restriction of payments) would have been used — more drastic than what happened in 1930, but it would have PREVENTED the cascading failures of 1931-33 and the Bank of United States might have survived, just as Knickerbocker Trust did in 1907-08.
- [MUST KNOW] N095 The Fed's existence PREVENTED the private cure by lulling the banking community into believing the Fed would handle it. But the Fed "stood idly by and let the crisis take its course."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N096 September 1931: Britain abandoned the gold standard. The Fed responded — after two years of severe depression — by RAISING the discount rate, the sharpest increase in its history. Purpose: prevent gold drain from foreign holders selling dollars. Effect domestically: catastrophically deflationary.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N097 1932: Congress forced the Fed to make large-scale open market purchases. The effects were "just starting to be felt" when Congress adjourned — and the Fed immediately stopped. The final banking panic of March 1933 culminated in a nationwide bank holiday declared March 6 by President Roosevelt.
- [MUST KNOW] N098 The numbers: 25,000 commercial banks in mid-1929 → 18,000 by early 1933 → only ~15,000 reopened after the bank holiday. ~10,000 banks disappeared in 4 years. For every $3 of deposits/currency in 1929, less than $2 remained in 1933 — "a monetary collapse without precedent."

#### Facts and Interpretation
- [MUST KNOW] N099 The monetary collapse was both CAUSE and EFFECT. It originated in Fed policy, made the economic collapse far worse, but the economic collapse also worsened the monetary collapse through feedback. "Good" loans became "bad" loans as businesses failed. The evidence is "all but conclusive" that the Fed not only had the power but KNEW how to prevent the collapse — the NY Fed repeatedly urged large-scale purchases but was overruled by the Washington Board's power struggle.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N100 Ogden L. Mills, Secretary of the Treasury, said in 1932: "For a great central banking system to stand by with a 70% gold reserve without taking active steps in such a situation was almost inconceivable and almost unforgivable." Yet that is precisely what happened.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N101 Gold flows prove the depression spread FROM the U.S. OUTWARD: U.S. gold stock ROSE from August 1929 to August 1931. Under gold standard rules, the Fed should have expanded money supply in response. Instead, it let money decline. ### CHAPTER 4: CRADLE TO GRAVE
- [MUST KNOW] N103 The 1932 election was a watershed: before it (1860-1932), Republicans held the presidency 56 years, Democrats 16. After (1932-1980), Democrats 32 years, Republicans 16. Federal spending alone multiplied roughly tenfold as a share of national income.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N104 FDR campaigned on CUTTING government waste and balancing the budget, berating Hoover for extravagance. - [MUST KNOW] N105 Edward Bellamy's *Looking Backward* (1887) — first use of the phrase "from cradle to grave." A utopian novel where a man sleeps from 1887 to 2000, finding a world of government planning and security. The 1930s New Deal drew on this intellectual tradition plus Bismarck's Germany, Fabian England, and Swedish social democracy.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N106 WWII's effect on public attitudes was the "mirror image" of the depression's. The depression convinced people capitalism was defective; the war convinced them centralized government was efficient. Both conclusions were wrong. Running a war economy with a single overriding purpose shared by nearly all citizens is very different from permanently controlling an economy shaped by "enormously varied and diverse objectives."
- [MUST KNOW] N107 W. Allen Wallis's insight: socialism, "intellectually bankrupt after more than a century of seeing one after another of its arguments for socializing the MEANS of production demolished — now seeks to socialize the RESULTS of production."

- [MUST KNOW] N109 The paradox of welfare: noble objectives, disappointing results, yet continued pressure for expansion. Social Security in deep financial trouble. #### The Emergence of the Modern Welfare State
- [MUST KNOW] N110 Bismarck (1880s Germany) was the first to introduce comprehensive social security on a large scale. His motives: paternalistic concern for workers + shrewd politics (undermine the Social Democrats). The crucial insight: aristocracy and socialism SHARE a belief in centralized rule by command. They differ only in WHO should rule — an elite by birth, or experts by merit. Both profess to know the "public interest" better than ordinary people. Both end up promoting the interests of their own class.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N111 Britain's Old Age Pensions Act (1908) — noncontributory, means-tested. A. V. Dicey noted the radical shift: previously, receiving government aid meant losing the right to vote. The 1908 act made it a RIGHT without stigma. The National Insurance Act (1911) went further — compulsory health and unemployment insurance, financed by employer/employee/government contributions. Dicey warned it would bring "a far heavier responsibility than anticipated" and amount to "the admission by a State of its duty to insure a man against the evil ensuing from his having no work."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N112 "The shape of Britain today owes more to Tory principles of the nineteenth century than to the ideas of Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels." Winston Churchill left the Tory (aristocracy) party for the Liberal party in 1904 and led social reform legislation — the social legislation was paternalistic in the tradition of the Tory Radicals, not socialist.

- [SHOULD KNOW] N114 New York City — the most welfare-oriented U.S. city (double Chicago's per-capita government spending). #### Social Security
- [MUST KNOW] N115 Social Security's "Orwellian doublethink": payroll taxes labeled "contributions" ("Compulsory is Voluntary"). Trust funds are tiny ($32B for OASI in 1978 — less than half a year's outlays) and consist only of promises by one government branch to pay another. The present value of obligations to ALL covered persons is in the TRILLIONS. "No trust fund in any meaningful sense was being accumulated."
- [MUST KNOW] N116 Social Security is NOT insurance — "The relationship between individual contributions (that is, payroll taxes) and benefits received is extremely tenuous." It's a combination of a particular tax and a particular transfer program. "We find it hard to conceive of a greater triumph of imaginative packaging than the combination of an unacceptable tax and an unacceptable benefit program into a Social Security program that is widely regarded as one of the greatest achievements of the New Deal."
- [MUST KNOW] N117 The tax is REGRESSIVE — flat rate on wages up to a maximum, bearing most heavily on low incomes. The benefit system is arbitrary: a woman who never worked gets the same as a similarly situated woman who qualified on her own earnings. A person over 65 who earns above a modest amount loses benefits but must pay additional taxes. Dividends of $1 million don't disqualify; wages above $4,500 cost $1 of benefits per $2 earned.
- [MUST KNOW] N118 Social Security transfers from poor to rich, not rich to poor. Poor children start working (and paying taxes) earlier. Poor adults have shorter life spans, so they collect benefits for fewer years. "The net result is that the poor tend to pay taxes for more years and receive benefits for fewer years than the rich — all in the name of helping the poor!"
- [MUST KNOW] N119 Director's Law (George Stigler): "Public expenditures are made for the primary benefit of the middle class, and financed with taxes which are borne in considerable part by the poor and rich."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N120 Demographic time bomb: in 1950, 17 workers per beneficiary. By 1970, only 3. By early 21st century, at most 2. Social Security is a "one-sided compact between the generations, foisted on generations that cannot give their consent... more like a chain letter."

#### Public Assistance

#### Medical Care
- [SHOULD KNOW] N125 Government health spending: <$5B in 1960, $68B by 1977. Government's share of total health spending nearly doubled from 25% to 42%. Medicare/Medicaid (1965) triggered explosive cost growth. Total medical spending doubled (as fraction of national income) from 1965 to 1977.
- [MUST KNOW] N126 Dr. Max Gammon's "theory of bureaucratic displacement" (studying British NHS): the more bureaucratic an organization, the more useless work displaces useful work. NHS hospital staffs grew 28% (1965-73), admin/clerical help grew 51%, but output (beds occupied daily) fell 11%. No new hospitals built in NHS's first 13 years. #### The Fallacy of the Welfare State
- [MUST KNOW] N127 The four categories of spending — Friedman's most famous framework:
  - **I**: Your money on yourself → strong incentive to economize AND get value (supermarket shopping)
  - **II**: Your money on someone else → incentive to economize but less incentive for value to recipient (Christmas gifts — if you really wanted to maximize value, you'd give cash)
  - **III**: Someone else's money on yourself → no incentive to economize, strong incentive to get your money's worth (expense-account lunches)
  - **IV**: Someone else's money on someone else → no incentive to economize OR get value (bureaucrat spending on welfare recipients)
  ALL welfare programs fall into Category III or IV. This is the fundamental reason they fail.
- [MUST KNOW] N128 The poor lack not only market skills but POLITICAL skills. Once well-meaning reformers pass a program and move on to their next cause, the poor are "left to fend for themselves and will almost always be overpowered by the groups that have already demonstrated a greater capacity to take advantage of available opportunities" — the middle class and bureaucrats.
- [MUST KNOW] N129 The lobbying waste: if $100 of someone else's money is up for grabs, it pays to spend up to $100 of your own to get it. These lobbying costs are PURE WASTE — they may even exceed the transfer itself, producing a net LOSS.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N130 Category IV spending corrupts: it puts some people in a position to decide what's good for others, creating "God-like power" in one group and "childlike dependence" in the other. The capacity for independence atrophies through disuse. "The end result is to rot the moral fabric that holds a decent society together."
- [MUST KNOW] N131 "The use of force is at the very heart of the welfare state — a bad means that tends to corrupt the good ends."

#### What Should Be Done
- [SHOULD KNOW] N132 Friedman's solution: the negative income tax — replace ALL welfare programs with a single cash transfer to people below a threshold. Proposed by three Presidents, supported across the political spectrum. Benefits: preserves incentive to work (you keep a portion of every dollar earned), eliminates the massive bureaucracy, treats people as responsible individuals, and can be transitional toward eliminating welfare dependence.

### CHAPTER 5: CREATED EQUAL
- [MUST KNOW] N050 Three meanings of "equality" in American history: (1) Equality before God / personal equality — Jefferson's meaning in the Declaration. People are NOT identical but have unalienable rights. Liberty is PART of this definition, not in conflict with it. (2) Equality of opportunity — "une carrière ouverte aux talents." No arbitrary obstacles based on birth, color, religion. Dominant meaning after the Civil War. Also consistent with liberty. (3) Equality of outcome — everyone should finish the race at the same time. "Fair shares for all." This meaning emerged in the 20th century and is in DIRECT CONFLICT with liberty.
- [MUST KNOW] N051 Equality of outcome requires someone to decide what is "fair" — and impose that decision on others. "Are those who make and impose such decisions equal to those for whom they decide?" Orwell's Animal Farm: "All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others." Every attempt to make equality of outcome the overriding principle has ended in terror: Russia, China, Cambodia.
- [MUST KNOW] N052 The inheritance paradox: people resent inheritance of property but not inheritance of talent. Yet from an ethical standpoint, what's the difference? A parent can help a child through education, setting up a business, or leaving property — is there an ethical difference? "Life is not fair. It is tempting to believe that government can rectify what nature has spawned."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N053 The baccarat analogy: if winners must repay losers, no one would play. Life is the same — we all make decisions involving chance (occupation, marriage, investments). If we bear the consequences, we make the decisions. If someone else bears the costs (taxpayers for flood damage), they will restrict our choices. "It is no accident that increasing government intervention has gone hand in hand with the drive for 'fair shares for all.'"
- [MUST KNOW] N054 The system of personal responsibility gave the Henry Fords, Thomas Edisons, John D. Rockefellers the incentive to transform society. Their fortunes came from developing new products/services. "The resulting addition to the wealth of the community as a whole amounted to many times the wealth accumulated by the innovators." These fortunes were then largely devoted to philanthropy — Rockefeller, Ford, Carnegie foundations.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N055 Chicago cultural philanthropy (1880s-1917): Art Institute, Newberry Library, Chicago Symphony, University of Chicago, Field Museum — all established by businessmen, privately supported but designed for the whole city. Helen Horowitz: "a kind of explosion of activity on many different levels." Chicago "seemed to epitomize America."
- [MUST KNOW] N056 Tocqueville's warning: "There exists in the human heart a depraved taste for equality, which impels the weak to attempt to lower the powerful to their own level, and reduces men to prefer equality in slavery to inequality with freedom."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N057 The "new class" (Irving Kristol's term) — government bureaucrats, academics on government grants, think-tank staffers, journalists — are the most ardent preachers of equality yet among the highest-paid in the community. Like the Quakers: "They came to the New World to do good, and ended up doing well."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N058 Israel's kibbutzim — the strongest test case. Communes are high-status, not stigmatized. Yet never more than ~5% of Israel's Jewish population has chosen kibbutz life. "That percentage can be regarded as an upper estimate of the fraction of people who would voluntarily choose a system enforcing equality of outcome."
- [MUST KNOW] N059 Britain's egalitarian failure: top tax rates of 98% on property income, 83% on earned income. Result: NOT equitable redistribution but new privileged classes — bureaucrats, trade union aristocrats, and "new millionaires" who found ways around the laws. "The drive for equality failed for a much more fundamental reason. It went against one of the most basic instincts of all human beings" — Adam Smith's "uniform, constant, and uninterrupted effort of every man to better his condition."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N060 When the law contradicts what most people regard as moral, they will break it. This disrespect spreads to ALL laws. "The growth of crude criminality in Britain in recent decades may well be one consequence of the drive for equality."
- [MUST KNOW] N061 Capitalism and inequality: "Nowhere is the gap between rich and poor wider, nowhere are the rich richer and the poor poorer, than in those societies that do not permit the free market to operate." True of feudal societies, centrally planned societies, and even where central planning was introduced in the name of equality. Russia: "a country of two nations." China's inequality was "more acute in 1957 than in any of the larger nations of the world except perhaps Brazil."

### CHAPTER 6: WHAT'S WRONG WITH OUR SCHOOLS?
- [MUST KNOW] N064 Education history: schools were mostly private and voluntary before 1840s. Schooling was practically universal BEFORE government took over. Teachers/administrators expected greater job security, assured salaries, and more control under government than under parents.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N065 E. G. West's research: both in the US and Britain, government takeover of schools was driven by teachers/administrators/intellectuals, not parents. - [MUST KNOW] N066 Gammon's Theory of Bureaucratic Displacement applied to schools: from 1971-77, total professional staff up 8%, cost per pupil up 58% (11% real), but number of students DOWN 4%, number of schools DOWN 4%, quality DOWN (declining standardized test scores). Input up, output down. Supervisors grew 44% while teachers grew only 14%.
- [MUST KNOW] N067 The consumer/producer distinction: in schooling, parents and children are consumers, teachers and administrators are producers. - [SHOULD KNOW] N069 St. John Chrysostom's (Bronx) and Harlem Prep — examples of successful private alternatives. Chrysostom's: children two grades ahead of public school peers at far lower cost. - [SHOULD KNOW] N070 Alum Rock experiment: only voucher experiment actually carried out. Limited to public schools, no parental add-ons. Despite limited scope, giving parents choice moved McCollam School from 13th to 2nd in its district on test scores. But "ended by the educational establishment."
- [MUST KNOW] N071 The racial issue: vouchers would MODERATE racial conflict. Integration has been most successful when voluntary, not coerced. Violence in public schools exists because victims are compelled to attend. Private schools have been in the forefront of integration. Vouchers would eliminate forced busing.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N072 Kenneth Clark (black educator): "public school systems are protected public monopolies... A monopoly need not genuinely concern itself with these matters... If there are no alternatives... then the possibilities of improvement in public education are limited."
- [MUST KNOW] N073 Higher education equity scandal: government subsidies for higher education transfer money from POOR to RICH. "We know of no government program that seems to us so inequitable... those of us in the middle- and upper-income classes have conned the poor into subsidizing us on the grand scale."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N074 Friedman's "equity financing" proposal for higher education: government advances funds for education in exchange for a percentage of future earnings (like buying equity in a person's earning prospects). Successful graduates repay more, compensating for unsuccessful ones. Could be self-financing and combined with income tax collection.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N075 Carnegie Commission on Higher Education: 14 of 18 members were from higher education institutions. ### CHAPTER 7: WHO PROTECTS THE CONSUMER?
- [MUST KNOW] N076 The "natural history of government intervention": (1) A real or fancied evil leads to demands. (2) A coalition of sincere reformers + interested parties forms. (3) Incompatible objectives (low prices for consumers + high prices for producers) are glossed over with rhetoric about "the public interest." (4) Congress passes a law granting discretionary power. (5) Reformers move on. (6) Interested parties capture the agency. (7) Effects become precisely the opposite of objectives. (8) New legislation is called for to fix problems caused by earlier legislation. A new cycle begins.
- [MUST KNOW] N077 ICC history proves the pattern. Established 1887 by a coalition of reformers AND railroads. Attorney General Olney to railroad president Perkins: "The Commission... is, or can be made, of great use to the railroads. It satisfies the popular clamor for a Government supervision of railroads, at the same time that that supervision is almost entirely nominal... The part of wisdom is not to destroy the Commission, but to utilize it." ICC solved the long-haul/short-haul problem by RAISING long-haul rates to match short-haul rates.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N078 Trucking cartelized by ICC: Motor Carrier Act (1935) gave ICC control over truckers — to protect railroads, not consumers. Of 89,000 initial applications for certificates, only ~27,000 approved. By 1974, firms down to 14,648 while tons shipped increased 27-fold. Certificate value: $2-3 billion — "wealth that corresponds solely to a government-granted monopoly position." Eliminating ICC regulation would reduce costs by ~75%.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N079 Air travel repeated the pattern. CAB controlled 19 domestic trunk lines in 1938; even fewer by 1979 despite enormous growth. Freddie Laker's price-cutting across the Atlantic + Alfred Kahn at CAB led to deregulation — the first major move AWAY from government control. "Its dramatic success — lower fares yet higher earnings — has encouraged a movement toward some measure of deregulation."
- [MUST KNOW] N080 FDA after 1962 Kefauver amendments: new drug introductions fell by more than 50%. Cost to develop a new drug went from ~$500K/25 months (1950s) to $54 million/8 years (1978) — a hundredfold increase in cost vs. doubling of general prices. The U.S. went from global leader to lagging behind. "Drug lag" — many more drugs available in Britain than in the U.S.
- [MUST KNOW] N081 The FDA's asymmetric incentives — Friedman's most devastating critique: (1) Approve a drug that causes harm → your name on every front page, career destroyed. (2) Reject a drug that could save lives → the people who die are never around to protest. "In view of the contrast between the abuse poured on the European drug companies that sold thalidomide and the fame and acclaim that came to Dr. Frances O. Kelsey... is there any doubt which mistake you will be more anxious to avoid?"
- [MUST KNOW] N082 "Barking cats" analogy: "What would you think of someone who said, 'I would like to have a cat provided it barked'? - [SHOULD KNOW] N083 Consumer Products Safety Commission: Tris episode. CPSC required flame-retardant children's sleepwear → 99% treated with Tris → Tris discovered to be carcinogenic → banned. "The initial requirements exposed millions of children to the danger of developing cancer." Government mandated the problem, then claimed credit for fixing it.
- [MUST KNOW] N084 Ralph Nader's Corvair: ten years after castigating it as "unsafe at any speed," a government agency tested it and concluded "The 1960-63 Corvair compared favorably with the other contemporary vehicles used in the tests." Corvairs became collectors' items. "But to most people, even the well informed, the Corvair is still 'unsafe at any speed.'"
- [SHOULD KNOW] N085 The environmental issue: "pollution vs. no pollution" is nonsense. Zero pollution would require abolishing automobiles and economic productivity. The real question is the RIGHT AMOUNT — where the gain from reducing pollution a bit more just balances the sacrifice. Friedman favors EFFLUENT CHARGES (pollution taxes) over regulatory mandates — they're cheaper, more effective, and create market incentives.
- [MUST KNOW] N086 The energy crisis (1970s): "There has been an energy crisis because government created one." Price controls are the key element. "Economists may not know much. But we know one thing very well: how to produce surpluses and shortages." Minimum prices → surpluses (wheat, butter). Maximum prices → shortages (housing, gasoline). Germany and Japan, wholly dependent on imported oil, had NO gas lines because they had no price controls.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N087 The market protects consumers through: (1) middlemen (department stores monitor quality), (2) brand names (reputation as the source of goodwill), (3) private testing organizations (Consumers Union, etc.), (4) competition from alternatives. ### CHAPTER 9: THE CURE FOR INFLATION
- [MUST KNOW] N096 Money rests on a shared fiction — each person accepts paper money because he's confident others will too. "There cannot be intrinsically a more insignificant thing, in the economy of society, than money... it only exerts a distinct and independent influence of its own when it gets out of order" (J.S. Mill). But when it gets out of order, it can do more damage than almost any other contrivance.
- [MUST KNOW] N097 Inflation is ALWAYS and EVERYWHERE a monetary phenomenon. "There is probably no other proposition in economics that is as well established." Inflation occurs when money quantity rises faster than output. Output grows ~3%/year (U.S.) — so any money growth substantially above that produces inflation. Charts for U.S., Japan, Germany, UK, Brazil all show the same near-identical correlation between money per unit of output and consumer prices.
- [MUST KNOW] N098 What does NOT cause inflation: (1) Unions — Japan (weak unions) and Brazil (government-controlled unions) show the same monetary pattern as UK (strongest unions). (2) Greedy businesses — no greedier in high-inflation countries. (3) Oil shocks — a once-for-all price level effect, not continuing inflation. Germany and Japan (100% oil-dependent) CUT inflation after the 1973 shock; the U.S. (50% dependent) didn't. (4) Low productivity — Brazil had rapid output growth AND rapid inflation.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N099 Virginia tobacco money: the first law of the first Virginia Assembly (1619) was about tobacco. It was legal currency for nearly two centuries. Planters grew so much that prices in terms of tobacco rose 40-fold — classic inflation from money supply growth. Gresham's Law applied perfectly: growers used worst tobacco to pay debts, kept best for export.
- [MUST KNOW] N100 Three reasons for excessive monetary growth in the U.S.: (1) Rapid growth in government spending — financed by money creation because taxing and borrowing are politically unattractive. "Financing government spending by increasing the quantity of money looks like magic." (2) Full employment policy — bias toward stimulus that feeds inflation. (3) The Fed's mistake — pursuing TWO incompatible objectives (money quantity AND interest rates), failing at both. The Fed is quicker to correct low monetary growth than high — an inflationary bias.
- [MUST KNOW] N101 The alcoholism analogy: inflation is like alcoholism. The good effects come first (euphoria, higher spending, more jobs), the bad effects later (rising prices, stagnation). The cure reverses the sequence — bad effects first (recession, unemployment), good effects later (lower inflation, healthier economy). "It takes a larger and larger amount — of alcohol or money — to give the alcoholic or the economy the same 'kick.'"
- [SHOULD KNOW] N102 Inflation as a hidden tax: (1) Direct tax on money balances — if inflation is 1%, every holder of money is effectively taxed 1%. (2) Bracket creep — a 10% inflation raises federal tax revenue by 15%+. (3) Repudiating government debt — a savings bond buyer in 1968 who cashed in 1978 got back less purchasing power than he paid, AND had to pay income tax on the nominal "gain."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N103 Keynes on inflation: "There is no subtler, no surer means of overturning the existing basis of society than to debauch the currency. The process engages all the hidden forces of economic law on the side of destruction, and does it in a manner which not one man in a million is able to diagnose."
- [MUST KNOW] N104 Japan case study: textbook cure. Monetary growth peaked at 25%+/year in mid-1973. Inflation followed ~2 years later. Japan then cut monetary growth to 10-15% and KEPT IT THERE for 5 years. After 18 months inflation started declining. By late 1970s, wholesale prices were actually falling. NO price and wage controls were used. Japan displayed the patience the U.S. repeatedly lacked.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N105 Five simple truths about inflation: (1) Inflation is a monetary phenomenon. (2) Government determines money quantity. (3) The only cure is slower monetary growth. (4) Both developing and curing inflation take YEARS. (5) Side effects of the cure are unavoidable. "The real option is only whether we have higher unemployment as a result of higher inflation or as a temporary side effect of curing inflation."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N106 Price and wage controls are counterproductive — they distort the price structure, reduce efficiency, waste labor, and in practice have always been used as a SUBSTITUTE for monetary restraint, not a complement. Markets interpret controls as a signal inflation is heading UP. "In light of the experience of forty centuries, only the short time perspective of politicians and voters can explain the repeated resort to price and wage controls."
- [SHOULD KNOW] N107 Escalator clauses (cost-of-living adjustments in contracts) help mitigate cure side effects by shortening the transition. Better as a temporary device than a permanent one. But two federal areas NEED permanent escalation: income taxes (to prevent hidden tax increases) and government bonds (to prevent repudiation of debt through inflation).

### CHAPTER 10: THE TIDE IS TURNING
- [MUST KNOW] N108 The tide metaphor: The tide toward economic freedom (Adam Smith, Jefferson) flowed strongly until the late 19th century. Then turned toward Fabian socialism / New Deal liberalism — flowing strongly for 3/4 century in Britain, half century in the U.S. By 1980, that tide has crested. The question is whether the next tide moves toward greater freedom (Smith/Jefferson) or toward totalitarianism (Marx/Mao). "That vital matter has not yet been determined."
- [MUST KNOW] N109 The Socialist Party of 1928: "the most influential political party in the United States in the first decades of the twentieth century." Never got more than 6% of the presidential vote. Yet "almost every economic plank in its 1928 presidential platform has by now been enacted into law" — nationalized railroads (Amtrak), government power systems (TVA), unemployment insurance, old age pensions, anti-child labor, progressive taxation, public works programs.
- [MUST KNOW] N110 The "invisible hand in politics" — operates in precisely the OPPOSITE direction to Adam Smith's invisible hand in economics. "Individuals who intend only to promote the general interest are led by the invisible political hand to promote a special interest that they had no intention to promote." Concentrated interests beat diffuse interests every time.
- [MUST KNOW] N111 Why special interests prevail: The merchant marine subsidy costs ~$600 million/year, or $15,000 per worker in the industry. But divided among 200 million Americans, it's $3/person. Ship owners have overwhelming incentive to lobby; citizens have negligible incentive to oppose. This dynamic replicates across thousands of programs. The majority that rules is "a coalition of special interest minorities" — each 2-3% group trades votes on its issue.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N112 The constitutional amendment approach: don't fight each program individually (hydra problem). Instead, adopt "self-denying ordinances" — broad rules limiting government power. The First Amendment works because it treats all speech restrictions as a bundle, not case by case. We need an "economic Bill of Rights": amendments to limit spending, prohibit tariffs, prohibit wage/price controls, end occupational licensure, establish a flat tax, require sound money.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N113 Proposed portmanteau free trade amendment: "The right of the people to buy and sell legitimate goods and services at mutually acceptable terms shall not be infringed by Congress or any of the States." Modeled on the Second Amendment.
- [SHOULD KNOW] N114 Tax reform: a flat rate under 20% on all income above personal exemptions, with no deductions except strict occupational expenses, would yield MORE revenue than the current graduated system (14-70% rates) riddled with loopholes. The corporate income tax should be abolished entirely — it's a hidden tax, constitutes double taxation, and penalizes capital investment.
- [MUST KNOW] N115 Britain, Sweden, and the U.S. all show popular revolt against big government by the late 1970s. In Britain, confiscatory taxes turned the nation into "fiddlers" — from grocers to cabinet members, everyone evades. In Sweden, high taxes produced working less, DIY, barter (dentist trades dental work for legal services). Pat Brennan competed with the U.S. Post Office in Rochester — thrived until the courts shut her down.
- [MUST KNOW] N116 The book's final thesis: "The greatest threat to human freedom is the concentration of power, whether in the hands of government or anyone else. We have persuaded ourselves that it is safe to grant power, provided it is for good purposes." We are still free to choose which way to go. The Constitution was written in a climate of opinion that valued limited government — when that climate changed, the Constitution proved at most "a source of delay to the growth of government power, not an obstacle."
