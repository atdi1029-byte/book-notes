The Catalyst Flywheel

The events that move stocks over two months: why each one works, what the research says, and whether it still does.

The Flywheel buys something cheap and waits for it to move. The "flywheel why" notes ask the next question: what might make it move? This volume is the catalog behind that question. It covers the recurring events that have tended to push a stock up or down over the following weeks and months, from spin-offs and index changes to earnings surprises, buybacks, insider buying and the calendar itself.

Every catalyst gets the same treatment: what happens, why it moves prices (the mechanism), what the research found and whether the effect has faded since it became well known, and how to use it on a two-month hold. Some of these effects are strong and still working. Some were strong once and have been arbitraged away. A few were never as strong as their reputation. Knowing which is which is most of the value, because a catalyst that no longer works is just a story. The guide ends with a dated calendar for the rest of 2026 and a way to tag catalysts in the Flywheel scorecard, so that over time your own records show which ones actually predict moves.

Why Catalysts Work (and Why They Stop)

Markets are good at pricing information quickly. If a company announces something obviously valuable, the price jumps within seconds, and there is nothing left to catch. So why would any predictable event keep moving prices for weeks afterward? Research points to four reasons, and every reliable catalyst in this guide rests on at least one of them.

1. Forced or non-economic trading. Some investors buy or sell for reasons that have nothing to do with value. An index fund must sell a stock the day it leaves the index. A fund that holds only large companies must dump the small spin-off it just received. An investor sells a loser in December to lock in a tax loss. An insurance company must sell a bond once it is downgraded to junk. These sellers push prices below fair value, and when they are done, prices recover. This is the most dependable source of catalysts, because the selling is mechanical and predictable.

2. Slow reaction to information. Investors have limited attention and tend to anchor on old beliefs. When a company beats expectations or changes its outlook, the price moves, but often not all the way, and it keeps drifting in the same direction as analysts revise their numbers and more investors notice. Post-earnings drift and the drift after analyst upgrades are the classic examples.

3. Signals from people who know more. Managers know their company better than anyone. When they buy stock with their own money, start or raise a dividend, or buy back shares, they are sending a message, and the message has tended to be right more often than not.

4. Payment for risk. Some patterns are not mistakes but rewards for bearing risk that others avoid, such as the spread a merger arbitrageur earns for taking the risk that a deal fails, or the extra return around earnings for bearing the risk of a surprise.

Effects built on the first reason tend to last, because the forced sellers keep being forced. Effects built on the second tend to shrink once they are published, because traders race to capture them: the S&P 500 index effect and the January effect are the best-known casualties, and post-earnings drift has faded sharply in large companies. That is why each entry below says whether the effect still works, and why you should be skeptical of any catalyst whose only support is a study from decades ago.

The Two Clocks: Reversal and Momentum

Before the catalysts, one piece of research that sits underneath the whole Flywheel. Stock prices run on two clocks that point in opposite directions, and a buy-the-dip strategy lives on the boundary between them.

The short clock (about one month) favors reversal. Stocks that fell hardest over the past month tend to bounce the next month, and last month's biggest winners tend to give some back. Narasimhan Jegadeesh and Bruce Lehmann documented this separately in 1990. The mechanism is mostly liquidity and overreaction: a burst of selling pushes a price below value, and it rebounds once the selling stops. This is the clock the Flywheel's cheapest-eight ranking is designed to catch.

The medium clock (about 3 to 12 months) favors momentum. Stocks that did best over the past year tend to keep doing better over the next several months, and the worst keep doing worse. Jegadeesh and Sheridan Titman documented this in 1993, and it has since been found in almost every market and asset class. The mechanism is slow reaction: good or bad news gets priced in over many months, not days. A related finding by Thomas George and Chuan-Yang Hwang (2004) is that stocks near their 52-week high tend to keep outperforming, because investors anchor on the high and sell too early.

Put the two together and you get the most important rule in this guide. A stock that dipped over the last few weeks within a longer uptrend is a reversal setup. A stock that has been falling for six to twelve months is a momentum loser, and cheapness alone is not a reason to buy it. The Flywheel's RSI and moving-average signals capture short-term dips, and the 200-day moving average is a rough stand-in for the long clock. A cheap score on a name that is below a falling 200-day average is a warning, not an invitation, unless a catalyst is about to change the story.

Forced Sellers

The most dependable catalysts, because the selling is mechanical: someone has to sell, whatever the price.

FORCED Spin-offs

Direction: weak at first, then up · Window: the first few months to about three years · Still works: partly

A spin-off happens when a company splits off a division into a separately listed company and hands its shares to existing shareholders, usually tax-free. Honeywell's aerospace business (HONA) in June 2026, FedEx Freight (FDXF) the same month, and DuPont's electronics arm (Qnity, Q) in November 2025 are recent examples.

Why it moves: The new shares land in the accounts of people who never chose to own them. An S&P 500 index fund receives shares of a smaller company it may not be allowed to hold. A dividend fund receives a company that pays no dividend. A growth fund receives a slow-growing industrial. Many of them sell in the first weeks, often without looking at the price, and the spin-off trades below its value. Meanwhile the new company's managers, now paid in its own stock, have strong incentives to run it well. That combination, forced selling followed by focused management, is the whole case.

Evidence: Patrick Cusatis, James Miles and Randall Woolridge (Journal of Financial Economics, 1993) found that spin-offs, and their parents, beat comparable companies by a wide margin over the three years after separation, with much of the excess coming from spin-offs that were later taken over. Joel Greenblatt made the strategy famous in You Can Be a Stock Market Genius (1997). The record since then is more mixed: an index of U.S. spin-offs beat the S&P 500 for years and then lagged it, so the edge now depends more on picking the right spin-offs than on buying them all.

How to use it: Don't buy on the first day. The forced selling usually runs for weeks, sometimes months, and the first report as a standalone company is often messy, as Honeywell Aerospace showed when it cut guidance and fell 23% in August 2026. The better entry is after the first one or two quarterly reports, once the index-fund selling is over and the numbers are clean. Read the Form 10 filing (the spin-off's prospectus), look at how much debt the parent pushed onto the new company, and check whether management owns meaningful stock.

Pitfalls: Parents sometimes use spin-offs to dump weak businesses loaded with debt. The parent often keeps a stake it may sell later (FedEx still owns 19.9% of FedEx Freight). Popular spin-offs that everyone is watching don't get the discount.

FORCED Index additions and deletions

Direction: additions up before inclusion, deletions down then up · Window: announcement to a few months after · Still works: additions barely, deletions yes

S&P Dow Jones Indices announces changes to the S&P 500 (and the MidCap 400 and SmallCap 600) a few days before they take effect, usually at the quarterly rebalance in March, June, September and December. Funds tracking those indexes must buy the added stocks and sell the deleted ones at the close on the effective date.

Why it moves: Index funds and closet indexers have to trade huge volumes at a known time, regardless of price. Everyone knows they are coming, so traders buy additions ahead of them and short deletions, and sell to the funds on the day.

Evidence: Andrei Shleifer (1986) found that stocks added to the S&P 500 jumped on the announcement and kept the gain. Robin Greenwood and Marco Sammon ("The Disappearing Index Effect") measured how that faded: the average abnormal return for additions fell from about 7.6% in the 1990s to about 0.8% in 2010–2020, and the average loss for deletions shrank to about −0.6%, as traders learned to position ahead of the funds. Earlier, Honghui Chen, Gregory Noronha and Vijay Singal (Journal of Finance, 2004) found an asymmetry: additions keep their gains, but deletions' losses tend to reverse in the following months.

How to use it: Don't chase additions after the announcement: the edge is mostly gone and the stock often fades after the effective date. Deletions are the more useful setup. A stock falling out of the S&P 500 into the MidCap 400 gets sold by large-cap funds and bought only partly by mid-cap funds, so it is pushed down, and the research says that pressure tends to reverse. A quality company that is deleted and sold down in the weeks around the effective date is a classic Flywheel candidate.

Pitfalls: Deletions happen for a reason: the company has shrunk, often because the business is struggling. Separate the index selling (temporary) from the business decline (maybe not).

FORCED Tax-loss selling and the January rebound

Direction: down into late December, up in early January · Window: November to January · Still works: weaker, mostly in small and beaten-down stocks

Investors in taxable accounts sell losing positions before December 31 to realize losses that offset their gains. The selling concentrates in the year's worst performers, especially smaller stocks owned by individuals, and it peaks in the last weeks of December.

Why it moves: The selling is driven by the tax calendar, not by value, so the year's losers are pushed down further into year-end. In January the selling stops, and some of the same investors buy back (after the 30-day wash-sale window), so prices recover.

Evidence: The pattern was first noticed by the investment banker Sidney Wachtel in 1942 and formally documented by Michael Rozeff and William Kinney in 1976; Marc Reinganum and Richard Roll (both 1983) tied it to tax-loss selling in small, beaten-down stocks. It has proved unreliable since it became famous, failing in several years, and today it shows up mostly in the smallest and most beaten-down stocks, often starting in the last days of December rather than in January.

How to use it: This fits the Flywheel naturally. Every December, look at quality names that are among the year's biggest decliners and have been sold down further in November and December. Buying in the second half of December and holding into February is a textbook two-month window. The effect is stronger in smaller companies and in stocks with large retail ownership.

Pitfalls: The year's losers are often losers for good reason, and momentum (the long clock) works against them. Use this only for names where you believe the business is fine and the price is just tired.

FORCED Credit downgrades and fallen angels

Direction: down on the downgrade, then recovery · Window: the months around a cut to junk · Still works: yes, in bonds; the stock effect is weaker

A "fallen angel" is a company whose bonds are cut from investment grade to junk (below BBB−). Many insurers, pension funds and investment-grade bond funds are not allowed to hold junk bonds, or must hold more capital against them, so they sell.

Why it moves: The forced selling pushes the bonds below fair value, and the fear of a downgrade often hits the stock too, in the weeks before and after. When the selling ends, bond prices tend to recover, which is why a fallen-angel bond fund (ANGL, in the Asset Atlas) has historically beaten the broad junk bond market.

Evidence: Research on insurance companies' forced bond sales (Andrew Ellul, Chotibhak Jotikasthira and Christian Lundblad, Journal of Financial Economics, 2011) found that downgraded bonds were pushed below fair value by regulatory selling and then recovered. That forced-selling discount is the basis of fallen-angel bond funds, which have historically beaten broad high-yield indexes.

How to use it: For stocks, a downgrade to junk that the market saw coming, at a company with enough cash to meet its debts for several years, can mark the low point of the fear. Watch the company's bond prices: if they stabilize after the downgrade, the stock often follows.

Pitfalls: Some fallen angels keep falling into default. The trade works for companies with a path to repair their balance sheet, not those running out of cash.

FORCED IPO lock-up expirations

Direction: down into the expiry · Window: the weeks around 90–180 days after an IPO · Still works: yes, modestly

When a company goes public, insiders and early investors agree not to sell for a set period, usually 180 days (sometimes 90 or staggered). The expiry date is disclosed in the IPO prospectus.

Why it moves: On a known date, a large number of shares become sellable by people who often want to cash out. The market knows this, but the extra supply still tends to push the price down around the date.

Evidence: Laura Field and Gordon Hanka (Journal of Finance, 2001) found an average abnormal return of about −1% to −2% around lock-up expiration dates, with trading volume jumping about 40%, and larger drops when venture capitalists were the sellers. The effect persists because the supply is real, even though the date is known.

How to use it: Mostly as a timing rule: don't buy a recent IPO in the weeks before its lock-up ends. For a young company you like, the weeks after a lock-up expiry, once the selling is visible and done, are usually a better entry than the IPO itself.

Pitfalls: Recent IPOs are volatile and rarely have the long record that the Flywheel's "hold it for a year" test requires.

Information Events

Events that tell the market something new, where the price often doesn't adjust all at once.

INFO Earnings surprises and post-earnings drift

Direction: continues in the direction of the surprise · Window: about 60 trading days · Still works: in small and mid-caps; much weaker in large caps

When a company reports earnings well above or below what analysts expected, the stock jumps or drops on the day. Post-earnings-announcement drift (PEAD) is the tendency for it to keep moving the same way for weeks afterward.

Why it moves: Investors and analysts underreact. Analysts raise estimates gradually rather than all at once, and good news in one quarter makes good news in the next more likely, which the market is slow to price.

Evidence: Ray Ball and Philip Brown (1968) first documented that prices keep drifting after earnings surprises. Victor Bernard and Jacob Thomas (1989, 1990) showed the drift lasts about 60 trading days, that a large part of it arrives around the next earnings reports, and that it is strongest in small companies. It became one of the most studied patterns in finance and has faded as a result: the gap between the biggest positive and negative surprises shrank from about 5% in the 1980s and 1990s to 3% or less by the late 2010s, and a 2022 study pointedly titled "Rest in Peace Post-Earnings Announcement Drift" found it has largely disappeared among large stocks.

How to use it: For the Flywheel, this is mostly a filter. A cheap name that just posted a clear beat and raised guidance, but hasn't moved much, has drift on its side. A cheap name that just missed and cut guidance has drift against it; wait for the second report. In large, heavily followed companies, don't expect much drift: the price usually adjusts on the day.

Pitfalls: The drift is small compared with the noise in a single stock, so it helps across many trades rather than guaranteeing any one.

INFO The earnings announcement premium

Direction: up, on average, in reporting months · Window: the weeks around the report · Still works: probably, but small

Stocks have tended to earn higher returns in the months when they report earnings than in months when they don't.

Why it moves: An earnings report is a burst of risk: the stock can gap either way, and that risk can't be diversified away easily because many companies report at the same time. Investors appear to demand a little extra return for holding through it. Attention may also play a part, as reporting brings in new buyers.

Evidence: Andrea Frazzini and Owen Lamont (2007) found that stocks earn noticeably higher returns in the months they are expected to report, a pattern later studies attributed partly to the extra risk around announcements and partly to the attention they draw.

How to use it: A mild tilt, not a strategy: other things being equal, buying a cheap name a few weeks before its report gives the earnings premium a chance to work within the two-month window. The "Watch" line in every other Flywheel volume gives each name's reporting months for exactly this reason.

Pitfalls: The premium is an average across thousands of stocks. Any single report can go badly, so size positions for the gap risk.

INFO Analyst upgrades, downgrades and estimate revisions

Direction: follows the change · Window: about 1 month for upgrades, up to 6 months for downgrades · Still works: partly

Brokerage analysts change their ratings and estimates constantly. The big moves happen on the day, but there is some follow-through.

Why it moves: A downgrade from an influential analyst changes what many funds hold, and it can take weeks for that to play out. Estimate revisions tend to cluster: once analysts start cutting, they keep cutting for a while.

Evidence: Kent Womack (Journal of Finance, 1996) found that prices keep drifting after rating changes: for a month or so after upgrades, and for up to six months after downgrades, the larger effect. Later work found the drift has shrunk as information spreads faster, but estimate revisions remain one of the more persistent signals used by quantitative funds.

How to use it: Revisions matter more than ratings. A cheap name with estimates still falling is usually cheap for a reason; the better setup is when estimate cuts stop and the first upward revisions appear. The scorecard in the "flywheel why" notes can track this: note whether estimates were rising or falling at the time of the pick.

Pitfalls: Analysts tend to upgrade after prices have risen and downgrade after they have fallen. Most single rating changes are noise.

INFO Buyback announcements

Direction: up · Window: months to years · Still works: yes, especially for cheap stocks

A company announces an authorization to buy back its own shares, usually over one to three years, on the open market or through an accelerated deal with a bank.

Why it moves: Managers who think their stock is cheap buy it back, so an announcement is a signal. The buying itself adds steady demand, and each share bought back raises everyone else's share of future profits.

Evidence: David Ikenberry, Josef Lakonishok and Theo Vermaelen (Journal of Financial Economics, 1995) found an average abnormal return of about 12% over the four years after open-market buyback announcements, and about 45% for cheap ("value") stocks, while expensive stocks showed little benefit. Later studies found the effect weaker but still present, and concentrated among companies that actually complete the buyback.

How to use it: The signal is strongest when a cheap stock announces a large buyback relative to its size (5% or more of the shares) and actually executes it. Many of the names in the Sector Flywheel fit this pattern: Omnicom, Tenet, Crown Holdings and Murphy USA all bought back large amounts of stock in 2026. Check the next quarterly report to see whether the company actually bought.

Pitfalls: Some companies announce buybacks they never complete, and some borrow heavily to fund them. A buyback at a peak valuation destroys value.

INFO Dividend initiations, raises and cuts

Direction: initiations and raises up, cuts down · Window: the announcement plus months of drift · Still works: yes

A company starts paying a dividend, raises it by more than usual, or cuts it.

Why it moves: A dividend is a promise that is painful to break, so managers only start or raise one when they are confident about future cash flow. A cut is an admission that they aren't. The market reacts on the day but, research suggests, not fully.

Evidence: Roni Michaely, Richard Thaler and Kent Womack (Journal of Finance, 1995) found that stocks jumped about 3% on dividend initiations and kept outperforming over the following year, while omissions (cuts to zero) fell about 7% on the day and kept underperforming. The drift after cuts was the stronger and more reliable effect.

How to use it: Treat an unexpectedly large raise or a first-ever dividend at a cheap company as a confirming catalyst. Treat a cut as a reason to wait: after a cut, the drift tends to be down for months. Diageo's 2026 dividend cut, which is why it was left out of the Sector Flywheel, is a recent example of the second kind.

Pitfalls: A very high yield often signals a cut ahead rather than a bargain.

INFO Insider buying

Direction: up · Window: 6–12 months · Still works: yes, for the right kind of purchase

Officers and directors must report their trades in their company's stock to the SEC within two business days, on Form 4. The filings are public and free.

Why it moves: Insiders sell for many reasons (taxes, diversification, buying a house) but buy for only one: they think the stock will go up. Their purchases have been a useful signal, especially at smaller companies where they know more than outside analysts.

Evidence: Josef Lakonishok and Inmoo Lee (Review of Financial Studies, 2001) found insider purchases predict higher returns, mostly in smaller companies. Lauren Cohen, Christopher Malloy and Lukasz Pomorski ("Decoding Inside Information," Journal of Finance, 2012) showed the signal is concentrated in "opportunistic" trades (insiders who don't buy on a routine schedule), which earned large abnormal returns, while routine trades predicted nothing.

How to use it: Ignore routine purchases (the same person buying every year) and focus on unusual ones: several insiders buying at once, a CEO buying a large amount relative to their pay, or buying right after a big price drop. Several executives at a cheap company buying on the open market in the same month is one of the better confirming signals a Flywheel pick can have.

Pitfalls: Insider selling means much less than insider buying. Small token purchases for show are common after bad news.

INFO Activist stakes

Direction: up · Window: the filing plus months to years · Still works: yes

An investor who buys more than 5% of a company intending to influence it must file a Schedule 13D with the SEC. Activist hedge funds use these stakes to push for buybacks, asset sales, board changes or a sale of the company.

Why it moves: The filing signals that an investor with money on the line thinks the stock is undervalued and plans to force changes. Activists often do get changes, and their targets are frequently sold.

Evidence: Alon Brav, Wei Jiang, Frank Partnoy and Randall Thomas (Journal of Finance, 2008) found that target stocks earned about 7% above the market around a 13D filing, with no reversal over the following year, and that targets' profitability and payouts improved afterward.

How to use it: An activist arriving at a cheap, well-run business that has simply been neglected is a strong catalyst for a Flywheel pick. Read the 13D (it states the activist's plans) and look at the activist's track record.

Pitfalls: Some campaigns fail, and a few activists push for short-term moves that weaken the business.

INFO Mergers and acquisitions

Direction: targets jump, acquirers often fall · Window: announcement to closing · Still works: the patterns are stable

When a company agrees to be bought, its stock jumps to just below the offer price and then trades at a small discount (the "spread") until the deal closes. The buyer's stock often falls on the announcement, especially if it is paying a big premium or using its own shares.

Why it moves: The spread is payment for the risk that the deal fails on regulation, financing or a shareholder vote. The acquirer falls because buyers overpay on average.

Evidence: Mark Mitchell and Todd Pulvino (Journal of Finance, 2001) showed that merger arbitrage earns steady excess returns in normal markets but loses heavily when markets crash and deals break together, a payoff that looks like selling insurance. Acquirers' shares, on average, fall when deals are announced, especially large stock-financed deals.

How to use it: For the Flywheel, the main use is defensive: never buy a "cheap" stock without checking whether it is a pending takeover target, since its price is pinned to the deal. That is why Essential Utilities and Axalta were dropped from the Sector Flywheel. An acquirer that falls on a sensible deal can be a good entry once the market has digested it.

Pitfalls: When a deal breaks, the target usually falls back to where it was before, or lower.

INFO Investor days and new targets

Direction: either way · Window: the event and following weeks · Still works: case by case

Companies hold investor or capital markets days every few years to set long-term targets. Everpure's September 2026 analyst day, where it set higher targets and the stock jumped about 17% in a day, and Ferrari's October 2025 day, where modest targets sent the stock down about 15%, show both sides.

Why it moves: Long-term targets reset what analysts put into their models, and the reset takes time to work through estimates.

How to use it: Dates are announced weeks ahead. A cheap stock heading into an investor day, at a company with a record of setting targets it beats, is a reasonable setup. A company that has recently disappointed may use the day to reset expectations lower.

Pitfalls: These events are often "sell the news" once targets are known.

The Calendar

Patterns tied to dates rather than to companies. The numbers in this section were measured directly from S&P 500 data from 1950 to 2025 unless noted.

CALENDAR The seasons: September, and "sell in May"

Direction: weaker May–October, stronger November–April · Window: six months · Still works: yes, but it is a tilt, not a rule

From 1950 to 2025, the S&P 500 rose an average of 7.0% from the end of October to the end of April, and just 2.0% from the end of April to the end of October. The winter half was up in 57 of 75 years; the summer half in 49. September was the only month with a negative average (−0.6%, up in only 45% of years), and it was worse still from 2000 to 2025 (−1.3%, up half the time). November, December and April were the strongest months, each up about 70% of the time.

Why it moves: Nobody has a complete answer. Candidate explanations include vacation-season trading, fund managers' fiscal years, the timing of year-end bonuses and new-year contributions flowing into markets, and investor mood. The effect has been found in dozens of countries, which argues against pure chance.

Evidence: Sven Bouman and Ben Jacobsen (American Economic Review, 2002) found the November–April effect in 36 of 37 countries, calling it the "Halloween indicator." A follow-up covering 1998–2012 in the same markets found it had continued after publication, with winter returns beating summer returns by about 10 percentage points on average.

How to use it: As a tiebreaker. A Flywheel pick bought in late September or October has the seasonal wind at its back for a two-month hold; one bought in early September has it in its face. Don't sell good positions in May because of it: the summer half has still been positive two years out of three.

Pitfalls: Averages hide huge variation. Octobers include both 1987's crash and some of the best months on record.

CALENDAR The turn of the month

Direction: up · Window: last trading day plus first three of the month · Still works: yes

Stocks have tended to do better in the few days around the start of each month. Measured on SPY from 1993 to September 2026, the last trading day of each month plus the first three days of the next made up about 19% of all trading days but delivered about 32% of the total gains, with an average daily return about twice that of other days.

Why it moves: Money arrives on a schedule. Paychecks, 401(k) contributions, pension payments and fund inflows land around month-end and get invested in the first days of the new month.

Evidence: Robert Ariel (1987) and Josef Lakonishok and Seymour Smidt (1988) documented the turn-of-the-month effect in U.S. data going back to the early 1900s. It has held up better than most calendar effects, consistent with its explanation in the timing of cash flows.

How to use it: Only as fine-tuning. If a Flywheel buy is due in the last week of a month, placing it a few days before month-end rather than just after the first puts the flow on your side. It is too small to trade by itself after costs.

CALENDAR Fed meetings

Direction: historically up before announcements · Window: the day before a scheduled decision · Still works: much weaker since it was published

The Federal Reserve holds eight scheduled rate meetings a year, with the decision at 2 p.m. Eastern on the second day and a press conference after.

Why it moves: Uncertainty builds ahead of a decision and is released when it is announced. For years, stocks tended to drift up in the 24 hours before a scheduled decision, as if investors were being paid for bearing the risk of a surprise.

Evidence: David Lucca and Emanuel Moench (Journal of Finance, 2015) found that from 1994 to 2011 the S&P 500 rose on average about half a percent in the 24 hours before scheduled Fed announcements, enough to account for a large share of the stock market's total excess return over the period, with no similar pattern in bonds or around other economic releases. Studies since publication have found the drift much weaker.

How to use it: Less as a trade and more as a risk marker: in a rate-sensitive market like September 2026's, a Fed meeting can move utilities, REITs and banks by several percent in a day. Avoid buying the whole position of a rate-sensitive name the day before a decision; split it around the meeting.

CALENDAR The presidential election cycle

Direction: weak midterm years, strong pre-election years · Window: the four-year cycle · Still works: the pattern has held surprisingly well

The four years of a U.S. presidential term have had very different stock returns. From 1950 to 2025 the S&P 500 averaged about 8% in the first year of a term, 5% in the midterm year (up only 11 of 19 times), 17% in the pre-election year (up 17 of 19 times) and 8% in the election year. The single strongest stretch has been the twelve months after the end of September in a midterm year: an average gain of about 20%, positive in 17 of 19 cycles since 1950 (the exceptions were 2010–11 and 2014–15, both roughly flat).

Why it moves: The usual explanation is political: administrations take unpopular measures early in a term and push for a strong economy heading into the next election, and midterm uncertainty tends to clear in November. The pattern was popularized by Yale Hirsch's Stock Trader's Almanac.

Evidence: The four-year pattern was popularized by Yale Hirsch's Stock Trader's Almanac. Academic tests have found it statistically weak because there are few four-year cycles to test, but the midterm-year low and pre-election-year strength have repeated more often than chance would suggest in the S&P 500 data from 1950 to 2025 shown above.

How to use it: 2026 is a midterm year, and the midterm elections are on November 3. Historically, the fourth quarter of a midterm year has been one of the best times of the four-year cycle to be buying. That doesn't override a bear market, but it is a reason to be more willing, not less, to put cash to work in weakness this autumn.

Pitfalls: Nineteen cycles is a small sample, and the causes are debated.

CALENDAR Quarter-ends, options expiration and rebalancing

Direction: varies · Window: days · Still works: as short-term noise

Several mechanical flows cluster at predictable times: monthly options expire on the third Friday of each month (with the quarterly "quadruple witching" in March, June, September and December), index rebalances take effect at the same quarterly dates, and pension funds rebalance between stocks and bonds at quarter-end.

Why it moves: After a strong quarter for stocks, balanced funds must sell stocks and buy bonds to get back to their targets, and vice versa. Options dealers hedging large positions can pin prices near popular strike prices into expiration and release them afterward.

How to use it: Mostly as a reason not to overread moves in the last days of a quarter or on expiration days. A quality name pushed down on heavy volume on a quad-witching or rebalance day, with no news, is often a gift.

Binary Events

Company events that resolve all at once, up or down. They move stocks more than any catalyst above, and they are the least predictable.

Some catalysts don't drift; they flip. An FDA approval decision (the date, called a PDUFA date, is usually public), a key clinical trial result, a court ruling, a regulatory decision on a merger, or a large government contract award can move a stock 20–50% in a day. The Sector Flywheel is full of them: United Therapeutics' lung-fibrosis approval, Neurocrine's pipeline readouts, the Surface Transportation Board's ruling on Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern, Curtiss-Wright's expected nuclear reactor order.

Binary events don't fit the Flywheel's normal logic. Cheapness doesn't protect you from a failed trial, and a two-month hold that spans a binary date is a bet on the outcome, not a mean-reversion trade. Three rules: know when each holding's binary dates are; size positions so that the bad outcome is survivable; and never use borrowed money on a position that has a binary event inside the holding window. If you like the setup, the lower-risk version is usually to buy after the event, once the result is known, and accept a higher price for the certainty.

Scoring Catalysts

The "flywheel why" notes already record a main reason for each pick and grade it four weeks later: did the reason happen, and did the price go up? The simplest upgrade is to tag each pick's main reason with one of the catalyst types in this guide (for example "earnings drift", "buyback", "index deletion", "seasonal", "rate relief", "oil"), plus a note on the long clock: was the stock above or below a rising 200-day average when it was picked?

After a few dozen graded picks, sort the scorecard by tag. The research in this guide describes what worked across thousands of stocks and decades. Your own tags will show what works for the Flywheel's kind of pick, at its size and holding period, in today's market. If "reason happened and price went up" is common for buybacks and insider buying and rare for analyst upgrades, that is worth more than any study, and it is exactly the question the scorecard was set up to answer.
