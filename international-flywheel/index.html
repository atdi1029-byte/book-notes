The International Flywheel

Trustworthy foreign stocks and country funds: how countries take turns, what can go wrong abroad, and the names worth owning in each market.

Countries take turns the way sectors do. In 2025 and 2026 South Korea's market more than doubled on the memory-chip boom while India's fell as foreign money left; Japan hit records as its banks re-rated on rising rates while China slid on regulation and a weak consumer. At any given moment some country is on sale, for reasons that often have nothing to do with the United States. The Flywheel today holds exactly one of them, Brazil (EWZ), plus the foreign companies that happen to be in the Sector Flywheel.

There are two good reasons to widen that. The first is diversification: foreign markets are driven by different rate cycles, currencies, commodities and politics, so they often get cheap when U.S. stocks don't. The second is price. The S&P 500 trades at about 25 times trailing earnings; developed markets outside the U.S. at about 17 to 18, and emerging markets at about 14. That gap doesn't guarantee anything, but it means foreign stocks start from a lower bar.

The catch is that investing abroad adds risks you don't face at home: currencies, governments that interfere with companies, weaker shareholder rights, capital controls, and taxes withheld at the source. That is why this volume starts with a trust test and sorts every country by it before recommending anything. Every stock here trades on the NYSE or Nasdaq and files reports with the SEC, and none of them repeat names already covered in the other volumes. Prices and data are as of 25 September 2026.

The Trust Test

"Trustworthy" doesn't mean safe from losses; every stock here can fall. It means that when you own a share, you can count on getting your fair piece of what the company earns, reported honestly, in a currency you can take home. Seven checks cover it.

1. Rule of law. Can courts enforce contracts and property rights against the powerful, including the government? This is the foundation for everything else. 2. Shareholder treatment. Who controls the company, and do minority shareholders get the same deal? Families, founders and governments often control foreign companies through special share classes, and they don't always act in outside shareholders' interest. 3. Reporting quality. Is the company listed on a major U.S. exchange, filing annual reports with the SEC (Form 20-F or 40-F) audited by a firm the U.S. audit regulator can inspect? A sponsored listing on the NYSE or Nasdaq is a real commitment; a thinly traded over-the-counter ADR is not. 4. Legal structure. Do you actually own the business? Many Chinese companies listed in the U.S. use a "variable interest entity" (VIE): U.S. investors own a Cayman Islands shell with contracts to the Chinese operating company, not the company itself, and those contracts might not hold up in a Chinese court.

5. Capital controls. Can the company, and you, move money out freely? Argentina has had currency controls on and off for decades; China controls its currency tightly. 6. State influence. Is the government a major owner, and does it use the company for policy? Petrobras holding down fuel prices in an election year is the classic case. 7. Taxes. How much of each dividend does the home country keep before it reaches you, and can you get any of it back? This one doesn't make a stock untrustworthy, but it changes what you actually earn.

Scoring countries on those checks gives three rough tiers. The tiers are about how much trust to extend, which translates into how large a position to hold, not whether to invest at all.

Why Countries Take Turns

A country fund is really a bundle of three bets: the country's companies, its currency and its politics. Each moves for its own reasons, which is why national markets rotate as much as sectors do. Five forces explain most of it.

The dollar. When the dollar weakens, foreign stocks earn more for U.S. investors, because the same euros or yen buy more dollars; when the dollar strengthens, the reverse. The dollar also matters to emerging markets directly, since many borrow in dollars, so a strong dollar tightens their finances. Long stretches of dollar strength (such as 2011–2022) have been long stretches of foreign underperformance, and turns in the dollar have often marked turns in foreign stocks.

What each market is made of. National indexes are lopsided. Taiwan and South Korea are mostly chips and electronics, so they ride the semiconductor cycle. Canada, Australia, Brazil and Norway are heavy in banks, energy and mining, so they follow commodities. Switzerland is pharma and food, so it behaves defensively. India is domestic banks, consumer companies and IT services. Much of what looks like a "country" move is really a sector move in disguise, which is also why a country fund can duplicate bets the Flywheel already has: Taiwan adds to the chip cluster, Brazil to the commodity one.

Local rate cycles. Central banks move at different times. In September 2026 the Fed, the ECB, Norway and Japan were raising rates while Brazil and Israel were cutting. Rising local rates help that country's banks (Japan's bank stocks rose 50–90% in a year) and its currency, but hurt its property and growth stocks.

Valuation. Foreign markets, especially emerging ones, trade at lower multiples than the U.S., partly for good reasons (more risk, slower growth, fewer technology giants) and partly because investors chase whatever has done best recently. When the gap gets wide, it tends to close through foreign outperformance, though the gap can persist for years.

Reform and politics. Some of the biggest national moves come from policy. Japan's push for better governance and more buybacks helped drive its market to records. South Korea's "value-up" program and new shareholder-rights laws helped the KOSPI double. Going the other way, China's regulatory crackdowns and Mexico's judicial reform made investors pay less for the same profits.

Currency: Hedged or Not

Owning a foreign stock means owning its currency too. If a Japanese stock rises 10% in yen while the yen falls 10% against the dollar, a U.S. investor makes roughly nothing. The last year showed how much this matters. The hedged Japan fund DXJ, which cancels out the yen, returned about 40%, while the unhedged EWJ returned about 26%, because the yen weakened as far as 164 per dollar before Japan spent a record amount defending it.

A hedged fund uses currency forwards to lock in today's exchange rate. That removes the currency bet, and it has a cost or a benefit that most people miss: the hedge earns (or pays) the difference in interest rates between the two countries. Hedging a low-rate currency like the yen back into dollars has paid a few percent a year, because U.S. rates are higher; hedging a high-rate currency like the Brazilian real would cost the difference instead, which is why hedged funds exist mostly for developed markets.

The practical rule: hedge when you like a country's companies but expect its currency to weaken, or when you want the stock bet without a currency bet on top. Stay unhedged when the dollar looks expensive and likely to weaken, since a falling dollar is a tailwind for every foreign holding. One caution on the diversification argument: the dollar has tended to rise in U.S. storms, so unhedged foreign stocks usually don't cushion a crash. Where the currency exposure pays off is in years when the dollar weakens, as it did in the first half of 2025.

Taxes Abroad

Most countries withhold tax from dividends paid to foreign shareholders before the money reaches your account. In a taxable U.S. account, you can usually claim that tax back as a foreign tax credit on your U.S. return, up to the U.S. rate. In an IRA or 401(k) you can't, so the withholding is simply lost, which makes high-withholding countries a poor fit for retirement accounts. A few countries let you reclaim part of the tax with paperwork, which is rarely worth it for small positions. The approximate rates for the countries in this guide:

Japan

Trust tier 1 · Country fund: EWJ (P/E ~19, +26% over a year) · Currency: about 157 yen per dollar · Already covered elsewhere: TM, SONY, HMC, MUFG, NTDOY.

Japan has changed more in the last three years than in the previous thirty. After decades of zero or negative rates, the Bank of Japan has raised its policy rate to 1.25%, the highest in about thirty years, and 10-year government bond yields are above 3%. The Tokyo Stock Exchange has pushed companies to lift returns and buy back stock, and many have. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's party won a two-thirds majority in February 2026. The yen is the complication. It weakened as far as 164 per dollar in July before Japan spent a record amount defending it. A weak yen helps Japan's exporters and hurts U.S. investors in unhedged funds; a rebound does the opposite. That is why this section includes both a hedged and an unhedged fund.

When it's on sale: Yen spikes (which hit exporters), global risk-off moves like the August 2024 carry-trade unwind (the Nikkei fell 12% in a single day), and worries that the Bank of Japan will raise rates too fast.

Trust tier 1 · Country fund: EZU (P/E ~18, +16% over a year) · Currency: about $1.14 per euro · Already covered elsewhere: ASML, SAP, TTE, SNY, RACE, BUD, PHG and the country funds EWG, EWQ, EWN, EWP, EWI.

Europe spent 2025 and 2026 in an unusual position: growth picked up (the September business survey was the strongest in over three years), German fiscal rules were loosened to allow much more spending on defense and infrastructure, and European banks, freed from a decade of negative rates, became some of the best performers in the world. South Korea

Trust tier 2 · Country fund: EWY (P/E ~11, +138% over a year) · Currency: about 1,357 won per dollar · Already covered elsewhere: EWY (Atlas).

ASR Grupo Aeroportuario del Sureste

Mexico · Airports · Market cap: ~$7B · P/E: ~13x · Yield: ~2.4% · 2-month swing: ±12%

The operator of Cancún airport, one of the busiest tourist gateways in the Americas, plus eight other airports in southeast Mexico, the main airport in San Juan, Puerto Rico, and airports in Colombia. In September 2026 it closed a debt-financed purchase of 20 airports in Brazil, Ecuador, Costa Rica and Curaçao. Airport concessions are long-lived monopolies that earn fees on every passenger.

2026 has been hard. Traffic is falling (Cancún down 9% in August) on high jet fuel prices from the Iran war, the bankruptcy of Spirit Airlines, seaweed on the beaches and fewer U.S. travelers. The regular dividend was cut, analysts downgraded the stock, and it is about 37% below its February high. Two special dividends are due in November and December.

Shines when: Fuel prices fall, U.S. tourism to Mexico recovers, and the new airports add growth.

Dangers: A long travel slump. Mexican regulation of airport fees. New debt from the acquisition. Mexican withholding (10%).

Watch: Monthly passenger traffic (the most useful number), quarterly reports in Feb, Apr, Jul, Oct.

How to use it: A quality monopoly in a cyclical slump, bought after the damage is visible. Monthly traffic numbers will show the turn before earnings do. It swings about ±12%.

CX Cemex

Mexico · Cement · Market cap: ~$14B · P/E: ~28x trailing, ~11x forward · Yield: ~1.3% · 2-month swing: ±14%

One of the world's largest cement, ready-mix concrete and aggregates companies, with its biggest businesses in Mexico and the U.S. and a sizeable European arm. After a decade spent paying down debt from an overpriced 2007 acquisition, Cemex is cutting costs and restarting dividends.

The stock slid about 30% from its May high without a single cause: softer Mexican margins, new competing capacity, weather in the U.S., an EU cartel allegation that Cemex rejects, and Mexico's broader troubles. Guidance for 2026 profit growth was actually raised.

Shines when: U.S. infrastructure and Mexican construction are strong, and costs fall.

Dangers: Mexico's economy and politics. Cement price competition. Europe's weak construction market.

Watch: Reports Feb, Apr, Jul, Oct. Mexican cement volumes and prices, U.S. margins, and debt.

How to use it: A cheap (about 11 times forward earnings) play on North American construction. It swings about ±14%. Compare it with Eagle Materials in the Sector Flywheel, which has the U.S. exposure without Mexico.

Peru & Argentina

Peru: trust tier 2, country fund EPU (P/E ~9, +55% over a year) · Argentina: trust tier 3, country fund ARGT · Already covered elsewhere: MELI, ARGT, SCCO (via the Atlas).

These two markets are small, volatile and currently in favor. Peru elected Keiko Fujimori as president in June 2026 in a very close runoff, and investors have welcomed the change; its market is near its high on record copper prices. Argentina is two years into President Javier Milei's free-market overhaul: his party won a landslide in the October 2025 midterms, currency controls for individuals were lifted in 2025, and inflation, while still about 33%, is far below its peak. Argentina has defaulted on its debt nine times, so every position there should be sized for the possibility that the reforms stall.

BAP Credicorp

Peru · Bank & Insurance · Market cap: ~$31B · P/E: ~15x · Yield: ~3.8% · 2-month swing: ±9%

Peru's largest financial group: Banco de Crédito del Perú (the country's biggest bank), the Yape mobile payments app used by most Peruvian adults, Mibanco for small businesses, and Pacífico insurance. It is incorporated in Bermuda and its shares list directly on the NYSE. Return on equity was about 20% in the second quarter, and it raised its medium-term target to about 22%.

Shines when: Copper prices are high (Peru is a major producer), politics are stable, and Yape grows into a full digital bank.

Dangers: Peruvian politics, which have produced six presidents in less than a decade. El Niño weather shocks. Bad loans in small-business lending.

Watch: Reports Feb, May, Aug, Nov. Return on equity, loan growth and Yape's profitability. A strategic update is due November 17, 2026.

How to use it: The quality way to own Peru and copper-country growth, up about 54% over the year. It swings about ±9%. Buy it on political scares.

VIST Vista Energy

Argentina · Shale Oil · Market cap: ~$8B · P/E: ~9x · Yield: none · 2-month swing: ±16%

A pure-play producer in Vaca Muerta, the Argentine shale formation that is one of the largest outside the U.S., run by Miguel Galuccio, the former head of YPF. It is incorporated in Mexico, which adds a layer of legal protection. Production grew 32% in the second quarter after it bought Equinor's Argentine assets, EBITDA margins run near 70%, and a new export pipeline due in mid-2027 will let more of its oil reach world markets.

Shines when: Oil is high, Argentina's reforms hold, and export capacity grows.

Dangers: Argentine politics, capital and export rules, and the peso. Oil prices. No dividend.

Watch: Reports Feb, Apr, Jul, Oct. Production, costs per barrel and pipeline progress.

How to use it: A cheap (about 7 times forward earnings) high-growth oil producer that roughly doubled over the year. It swings hard (±16%). Keep it small: it combines oil-price risk with Argentina risk.

China

Trust tier 3 · Country funds: MCHI (P/E ~11, −17% over a year), FXI, KWEB · Currency: about 6.71 yuan per dollar · Already covered elsewhere: BABA, BIDU, PDD, NTES, YUMC (Sector Flywheel), FXI, KWEB.

China is the cheapest major market in this guide and the hardest to trust. The economy slowed to 4.3% growth in the second quarter of 2026, the weakest in years; the property slump continues; and stimulus has been targeted rather than broad. Regulators have again shown they can hit companies hard: Trip.com, China's largest online travel company, was fined about $763 million in an antitrust case in July, its co-founders left the board, and the stock fell about 50%. In September, regulators opened a security probe into two leading AI startups. The Hang Seng is about 12% below its January high and far below its 2018 record.

On the other side, Xi Jinping's state visit to Washington in late September extended the U.S.–China trade truce into early 2027, and Chinese companies are cheap, profitable and returning more cash to shareholders than ever. Most U.S.-listed Chinese companies use the VIE structure described in the trust test, which is the main reason for tier 3.

When it's on sale: Almost always, lately. The better question is when it stops being cheap, which has tended to happen in short, violent rallies on stimulus or trade news. China positions should be small, diversified, and sold into those rallies rather than held for a turn that keeps not arriving.

Fund MCHI Stock JD

MCHI iShares MSCI China ETF

China fund · ~590 stocks · Expense ratio: 0.59% · P/E: ~11x · Yield: ~2.0% · 2-month swing: ±9%

About 590 Chinese companies listed in Hong Kong, the U.S. and mainland China: Tencent (about 14%), Alibaba, the big state banks, Xiaomi, Meituan, Ping An, NetEase and PDD. It is broader than the Atlas's FXI (which holds only 50 large companies) and less concentrated in internet stocks than KWEB. Diversification is the point: in China, any single company can be hit by a regulator overnight.

Shines when: Beijing launches broad stimulus, U.S.–China relations improve, and consumer spending recovers.

Dangers: Regulation, geopolitics, the property slump and the VIE structure of many holdings.

How to use it: The diversified way to hold a small China position at about 11 times earnings. It swings about ±9%. Buy after policy scares and trim into stimulus rallies.

JD JD.com

China · E-commerce · Market cap: ~$36B · P/E: ~18x · Yield: ~3.7% · 2-month swing: ±16%

China's largest direct online retailer, which, unlike Alibaba's marketplaces, owns much of its inventory and runs its own nationwide logistics network, making it the closest Chinese equivalent to Amazon. It pays a dividend of $1 per ADS and buys back stock. In 2025 it entered food delivery, starting an expensive price war with Meituan that is slowly becoming less costly. It is also trying to buy Germany's Ceconomy (MediaMarkt) for about $2.5 billion, which is under EU review with a decision due October 23.

Shines when: Chinese consumer spending recovers, the delivery price war ends, and government subsidies for electronics and appliances return.

Dangers: The VIE structure and Chinese regulation. Price wars. A weak electronics market (its appliance sales fell 12% in the second quarter). U.S.–China tension.

Watch: Reports Mar, May, Aug, Nov. Retail revenue growth, food-delivery losses and buybacks.

How to use it: A cheap Chinese consumer name with real assets, a dividend (about 3.7%) and a well-known value investor (Michael Burry) among its holders. It swings hard (±16%). Keep it small.
