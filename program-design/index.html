1. How to Read a Program Line

Every exercise in the tracker is stored as one line with six parts: name, English name, muscle, technique, set scheme, and rest in seconds. The technique and the set scheme are where all the design decisions live. Once you can read those two fields fluently, the rest of this guide is just pattern recognition.

The rule hiding in this table

Every technique in the "Fatigue" and "Density" families appears in programs with short rest (60 s) and high reps (8–12+). None of them appear in the heavy blocks with 120–180 s rest and 6–8 reps, where 90–100% of exercises are plain straight sets. Your trainer never stacked heavy weight and fatigue techniques in the same block. That single decision explains more about these programs than any other.

The phases

Each program carries a phase tag. Four of them cycle; two are special cases.

  • acc Accumulation — build volume. Moderate weight, 8–12 reps, 60–80 s rest, many exercises, lots of sets. The purpose is to make you tolerate more work than last time.
  • int Intensification — make the work harder without adding sets. Pyramids that reach 5–8 reps, rest-pause, drop sets, supersets. Same or slightly fewer exercises than accumulation.
  • spe Specialization — point extra volume at one weak muscle while keeping everything else on maintenance. Look for the muscle whose set count jumps.
  • rea Realization — cash in. Fewer exercises, heavier loads, longer rest, no fancy techniques. This is where the strength you built shows up on the bar.
  • peak Peak strength — the heaviest version of realization, or a metabolic peak that ends a cycle before a reset.

Do not treat the tags as gospel. Two of the sixteen are tagged with a phase that the numbers do not support (ABCD07 says specialization but trains like realization; ABCD11 says peak but trains like intensification). The tag is what the block was called. The rep scheme, rest, and set count are what it is. When they disagree, believe the numbers, and in section 5 I will show you where they disagree.

3. The Seven Laws Behind Every Program

You asked for the why. Here it is, compressed. Everything in the sixteen programs is downstream of these seven decisions. If you internalize them, you can write a program your trainer would recognize as one of his. If you break one of them, you will usually be able to feel it within three weeks.

Law 1 — Volume and intensity are a see-saw, never a ladder

The heaviest programs are the smallest programs. ABCD08 has 73 working sets at an average of 6–7 reps with 119 s rest. ABCD03 has 112 sets at 11–12 reps with 75 s rest. Nobody gets both. When the weight goes up, the set count comes down, the rest goes up, and the exercise list gets shorter. When the weight comes back down, all three reverse.

The reason is recovery, not time. Heavy sets cost far more to recover from than moderate ones, in the joints and in the nervous system as much as in the muscle. A block that tries to keep accumulation-level volume at realization-level loads is the classic self-written program, and it is the one that produces a nagging elbow in week four and a stalled bench in week six. Your trainer's response to "I want to lift heavier" was always "then we do less." Every single time.

The see-saw also runs inside a single day. Look at any heavy block: the first compound has 180 s rest, the second 120, the isolation 90 or 60. The rest is proportional to how heavy the set is, because that is what the rest is for.

Law 2 — Intensity techniques live in the high-rep phases; heavy blocks are boring on purpose

Count the techniques. ABC02 (intensification, 8–12 reps): 3 pyramids, 6 bi-set lines, 2 rest-pause, 1 failure, 1 drop. ABCD03 (intensification): 6 pyramids, 6 superset lines, 2 drops, 2 rest-pause, 2 failure. Now ABCD04 (realization): 25 straight sets, 1 drop set. ABCD07: 21 straight, 1 rest-pause, 1 back-off. ABCD08: 23 straight, 1 rest-pause.

The fatigue techniques (drop, rest-pause, failure, pre-exhaust) exist to squeeze more stimulus out of moderate weights. That is exactly what you want when the weight is moderate and the goal is volume. It is exactly what you do not want when the weight is heavy, because heavy work to failure plus a drop set is how you turn a productive 3x6 into a week of tendon pain. Your trainer let the weight be the intensity in the heavy blocks, and let the technique be the intensity in the lighter blocks. Never both.

A corollary: density techniques (bi-sets, supersets, tri-sets) are for small or cheap muscles. In sixteen programs they were used on abs (constantly), arms (ABCD03), delts (front raise + press, ABC03 and ABCD01), calves, and one leg pair (extension + lunge). They were never used on the first compound of the day. Supersetting your bench press is a way to make your bench press worse, not a way to save time.

Law 3 — Movement patterns are permanent; the implements rotate

Every push day in sixteen programs contains a horizontal press. Every pull day contains a row and a vertical pull. Every leg day contains a knee-dominant compound, a leg extension or lunge, a leg curl, and calves. The pattern is fixed. What changes is the tool:

  • Horizontal press: barbell bench (10 programs) → incline DB (12) → DB flat (5) → machine press → Smith → dips (6) → weighted push-ups.
  • Vertical pull: lat pulldown (12) → V-bar pulldown (10) → pull-ups → chin-ups → reverse-grip pulldown.
  • Row: bent-over barbell (11) → seated cable (10) → one-arm DB (7) → wide-grip cable → T-bar → Kroc row → kettlebell.
  • Knee-dominant: leg press (13, the most-used exercise of all) → hack squat (7) → back squat (4) → Smith squat (4) → Bulgarian split squat (2).

The point of rotating the implement is that a barbell bench and an incline DB press train the same muscles through slightly different lines of pull, so you can change the stimulus without losing the skill or the strength you built. The point of not rotating the pattern is that the pattern is the thing you are actually trying to get strong at. A program that swaps the horizontal press for a cable crossover for two months is not a variation; it is a two-month hole in your bench.

Law 4 — Inside a wave, change one big variable; between waves, change everything

Compare consecutive programs by how many exercises carry over:

  • ABCD07 → ABCD08: 60% of exercises kept. Reps went from 3x8 to 3x6. That is the whole change. Same movements, one notch heavier.
  • ABCD01 → ABCD02: 62% kept. Same arms-day split; drop sets swapped for pre-exhaust and failure sets.
  • ABCD10 → ABCD11: 57% kept. Pyramids stayed on the compounds; accessories went from 3x10 to 3x12 and drop sets came back on day D.
  • ABCD08 → ABCD09: 10% kept. After the two heaviest blocks, 28 of 31 exercises were new, the reps went from 6 back to 10, and a forearm slot appeared. Total reset.
  • ABCD11 → ABC+D1 (your first self-written program): 6% kept. Also a reset, whether you intended it or not.

When two blocks are part of the same climb, they share most of their exercises so you can carry the weights forward and actually see the progression; changing the exercise and the rep scheme at once means every number resets and you learn nothing about whether you got stronger. When a climb is over, and it is over after the realization block, everything resets on purpose: new movements, new rep range, new rest. The novelty is the recovery.

Law 5 — A block is about thirty sessions, not a number of weeks

Every three-day program is 10 weeks. Every four-day program is 7 weeks. That looks like two arbitrary numbers until you multiply: 10 × 3 = 30 sessions, 7 × 4 = 28 sessions. Your trainer sized blocks by exposures, not by the calendar. The more often you hit a muscle each week, the faster you adapt to the stimulus and the sooner it stops working, so the block ends sooner.

This is the rule to use when you change frequency. A five-day split would be a five- or six-week block. A two-day full-body program could run fifteen weeks. And a block that has stopped producing new weights before its thirtieth session is a block that ended early; do not grind out the calendar.

Law 6 — Push and pull stay balanced; the leftover budget goes to a target

Chest and back working sets track each other within two or three sets in fourteen of sixteen programs. The two exceptions are specialization blocks: ABC03 (15 chest / 23 back) and ABCD01 (12 chest / 17 back, plus 18 sets of delts). Both were followed by programs that rebalanced.

The reason is the shoulder. Pressing volume without matching pulling volume rounds the shoulders forward and shortens the pecs, which is how the rotator-cuff prehab ended up in every program from ABC04 on. Balanced push/pull is not aesthetics; it is the cheapest injury insurance there is. Once the balance is set, whatever recovery budget is left over goes to one target muscle for a block: biceps in ABC05 (16 sets), triceps in ABCD02 (16), abs in ABCD04 (20), chest and back together in ABCD05 (22 each). One target. Never three.

Law 7 — The ride-alongs never leave, and they moved to the front

Some things appear in every program regardless of phase:

  • Calves on every leg day, 3–6 sets, usually 12–20 reps, 30–60 s rest.
  • Abs on two or three days per week, in every single program.
  • Shoulder internal and external rotation, 1–2 sets of 10, on every upper day from ABC04 onward. Twelve programs in a row.
  • Warm-up sets on the first compound of each muscle group (2x8 or 2x10), and, in the heavy blocks, on every exercise.

And notice where the abs and the cuff work sit. In ABC01 through ABC03, abs were the last line of the day. From ABC04 onward, abs and rotations are the first lines of the day, before the heavy press. This is a practical fix for a practical problem: work that is at the end of the session is work that gets skipped when you are tired or late. Putting the boring, important stuff first guarantees it happens, warms up the trunk and cuff before they have to stabilize a heavy bar, and costs nothing because four sets of cable crunches do not tire out your chest. Copy this. Whatever you keep skipping, move it to slot one.

The seven laws in one breath

Volume and intensity see-saw. Techniques only in the light phases. Patterns fixed, implements rotate. One variable per block inside a wave, everything between waves. Thirty sessions per block. Push equals pull, surplus to one target. Ride-alongs always, and up front.

4. Anatomy of a Training Day

The laws tell you how programs relate to each other. This section tells you how a single day is built. Every day in the sixteen programs follows the same slot order, and once you see the slots you will never need to wonder what goes where.

The universal day. Slot 0 was added from ABC04 onward; before that the abs sat at the end and were the first thing skipped.

Why this order? Because fatigue is a resource you spend, and the heavy compound is the thing that most deserves to be done fresh. Everything after slot 1 is progressively more tolerant of being done tired. A drop set on the leg extension while your quads are already cooked is fine; it is arguably the point. A drop set on the hack squat while your quads are fresh is a waste of the freshness. Isolation work also cannot hurt you much when it is done fatigued, whereas a heavy squat done fatigued absolutely can.

The slot count is also the volume control. A push day in an accumulation block fills every slot and sometimes doubles slot 2 or 3 (ABC02 had four chest exercises). A push day in a realization block fills slots 0, 1, 2, 3, and one arm move and stops (ABC04: six lines). You never remove slot 1 to make room for something else.

4.4 The Arms Day (ABCD01–ABCD04 only)

For four programs the fourth day was arms plus abs. The template:

  • Abs tri-set or straight sets first (crunch / leg raise / plank).
  • Biceps: one barbell or machine curl with a pyramid or failure scheme, one DB curl 4x8, one cable curl to failure.
  • Triceps: mirror image, rope pushdown pyramid, skull crushers 4x8-12, kickback or single-arm French press with rest-pause or failure.
  • ABCD03 turned the whole day into antagonist supersets: barbell curl + skull crusher, French press + barbell curl, cable curl + pushdown, with 90 s rest after each pair.

The arms day pushes biceps and triceps volume from 8–10 sets to 12–16 sets per week. That is what it is for. It was run for four consecutive blocks (28 weeks) and then retired in favor of a fourth upper-body day, which suggests either the arms caught up or the trainer decided the fourth day was better spent on a second chest/back exposure. Either way, it is a specialization tool, not a permanent fixture.

4.5 The Upper (MMSS) Day (ABCD05–ABCD11)

MMSS is membros superiores, upper limbs. From ABCD05 on, the fourth day was a compressed second upper session. It is always six to eight lines and always follows the same recipe:

  • Two chest moves: a press and a fly or second press. Always the implement that days A did not use. If Monday's bench was barbell, Thursday's is DB or machine. ABCD05: A = barbell bench + incline DB, D = DB bench + incline barbell. Swapped exactly.
  • One triceps move.
  • Two back moves: a vertical pull and a row, again the implements A/B did not use. ABCD08: B = wide cable row + seated row + V-bar; D = lat pulldown + one-arm DB row.
  • One biceps move.
  • Sometimes a lateral raise or upright row, and in ABCD11 the abs bi-set rides here.

The upper day is why chest and back volume in the ABCD05+ programs sits near 20 sets each while the push and pull days themselves are no bigger than in the ABC era. Frequency went from one exposure per week to two for everything above the waist, while legs stayed at one. That matches the priorities of the person being trained: this is an upper-body-first program, and the fourth day says so explicitly.

5. Program by Program

Now the sixteen in order. For each one: what it is, what changed from the block before, why that change makes sense, and what is worth stealing. The numbers in each header are days · weeks · exercises · working sets · average rest · average rep range. I count shoulder rotations as prep, not as shoulder work, and warm-up sets are excluded from the set counts.

5.1 ABC01 – ABC05: the three-day era

ABC01 · accumulation · 3 days · 10 weeks · 25 exercises · 91 sets · rest 61 s · reps 10–14

What it is. The baseline. Every exercise is 4x8-12 straight sets with 60 s rest, except the first compound of each day, which gets two warm-up sets of ten and 80 s. Push day is four chest movements (barbell bench, incline machine, cable fly, DB bench), a DB press, a side-and-front raise sequence, two triceps moves, and an abs bi-set at the end. Pull day is four back movements (pulldown, V-bar, one-arm row, barbell row), reverse fly, single-arm lateral, two biceps moves. Leg day is hack squat, back squat, leg press, leg extension with a drop, walking lunges, seated leg curl, and six sets of seated calves.

The design idea. Establish the skeleton and let the weights find their level. There is almost nothing clever here on purpose: an accumulation block for someone new to a coach is a block where the coach learns what you can lift and you learn the movements. The one sophisticated touch is the four-rung descending-rep pyramid on the rope pushdown and rope curl (20-30/15-20/10-15/5-10), which is a way to warm up small joints thoroughly while still finishing heavy.

Steal this. The push/pull/legs skeleton, the balance (16 chest / 16 back), and the rule that the first compound gets warm-ups and longer rest than everything after it.

ABC02 · intensification · 3 days · 10 weeks · 27 exercises · 98 sets · rest 69 s · reps 10–13

What changed. The skeleton is the same, 48% of exercises carried over, and the techniques were layered on. Barbell bench became a pyramid (12-15/10-12/8-10/5-8), the first time you touched a 5–8 rep set. Machine fly got bi-set with push-ups to failure. Cable fly picked up rest-pause. A decline machine press was added so there were five chest lines. Pull-ups to failure now open pull day; the pulldown is a pyramid; the seated row is rest-pause; the one-arm row is an ascending pyramid. On legs, extension and lunges became a bi-set and the lying leg curl got a drop set. Rest on the squats went to 90 s.

Why. This is the textbook move from accumulation to intensification: keep the volume roughly where it was (91 → 98 sets) and make each set cost more. The pyramids introduce heavy singles-digit sets without making the whole session heavy; the failure and rest-pause work extends the moderate sets past where they would normally stop. You are being taught to work harder inside the same movements you already know, which is exactly why the exercises did not change much.

Steal this. The transition itself. When a block has stopped producing new weights, the cheapest next block is the same exercises with a pyramid on the main lift and one fatigue technique per day on an isolation move.

ABC03 · specialization · 3 days · 10 weeks · 27 exercises · 99 sets · rest 67 s · reps 10–12

What changed. Only 30% of exercises carried over, and the volume moved. Push day leads with incline barbell (4x8-10), then incline DB flyes, then incline DB press pyramid: three incline moves in a row, with the machine fly rest-pause after. Front raise and DB press became a bi-set. Pull day is five back movements (pull-ups, V-bar, seated row, one-arm row pyramid, underhand row rest-pause) for 23 sets, plus three biceps moves for 11. Leg day changed character completely: stretching, then a deadlift (3x10 warm-up + 4x10), leg press, a truncated pyramid on the seated leg curl (2x12-15/2x8-12/2x5-8), Bulgarian split squats, and Smith calves. Quads dropped from 17 to 9 sets.

Why. This is what specialization looks like in practice: the extra sets go to upper chest and back, the deadlift and the six-set leg-curl pyramid shift the leg day toward the posterior chain, and the quads are cut to pay for it. Notice that total volume barely moved (98 → 99). Specialization is a redistribution, not an addition. Notice too that the reps crept down (4x8-10 on the lead lifts instead of 8-12), which is the intensification trend continuing underneath.

Steal this. The truncated pyramid for a muscle you want to bring up: two sets on each of three rungs gives six quality sets with a built-in warm-up and a built-in heavy finish.

ABC04 · realization · 3 days · 10 weeks · 19 exercises · 80 sets · rest 70 s · reps 9–12

What changed. Eight exercises disappeared. Bench is 4x6-8, incline DB is 4x6-8, the pyramids on the rope pushdown and cable curl now end at 6-8, the leg curl pyramid ends at 5-8. Rest on the compounds went to 90 s. And two things appeared that never left again: shoulder internal and external rotations at the top of pull day, and abs (leg raises 5x12-15) as the first line of the day instead of the last.

Why. Three blocks of accumulating and intensifying have to be cashed in. The realization block strips the exercise list to the essentials so the sets you keep can be genuinely heavy, and the drop in total sets (99 → 80) is what pays for the drop in reps. The cuff work arriving at the same moment the loads went up is not a coincidence; heavy pressing at 6-8 reps is where shoulders start to complain, and the rotations are the cheapest insurance available. Moving abs to the front is the fix for abs being skipped, which they had been for three programs.

Steal this. Cutting exercises when the weight goes up. If you cannot name which eight lines you would remove from your current program to make it a realization block, you do not yet have a realization block.

ABC05 · accumulation · 3 days · 10 weeks · 24 exercises · 98 sets · rest 64 s · reps 10–12

What changed. The reset. Only 29% carried over. The reps go back to 8-12 and pyramids (1x12/2x8-10/2x5-8) carry their own warm-up, so explicit warm-up lines almost vanish. Push day opens with two light sets of cable fly as a primer before DB bench; pull day opens with two light sets of seated row before the barbell row. Overhead extension and bench dips are a bi-set; preacher and hammer curls are a bi-set; stiff-leg deadlift and lying leg curl are a bi-set. Biceps get four exercises and 16 sets; triceps 12; abs 9 with the ab wheel to failure up front. Prep slot (ab wheel or leg raises, then rotations) is now on both upper days.

Why. After a realization block you cannot simply go heavier again; the joints and the motivation both need the loads to come down and the exercise list to change. The primer sets (two easy sets of an isolation move before the heavy compound) are a gentler version of pre-exhaust: they wake the muscle up without tiring it. The arms getting 28 combined sets is a quiet specialization tucked inside the accumulation block, and it was the setup for the next four programs, which gave arms their own day.

Steal this. The primer-set idea, and the hamstring bi-set (a hinge paired with a curl) as a way to double hamstring work without adding a slot.

5.2 ABCD01 – ABCD04: the arms-day era

Here the program went to four days and the block length dropped from ten weeks to seven. The fourth day is arms plus abs. Chest and shoulders share a day; back and rear delts share a day; legs get a full day.

ABCD01 · intensification · 4 days · 7 weeks · 30 exercises · 107 sets · rest 65 s · reps 10–14

What it is. The most drop-set-heavy program of the sixteen (five of them) and the first appearance of 5x5-8 on the lead lifts (incline DB press, lat pulldown). Chest day opens with a machine fly drop set before the pressing, a pre-exhaust in spirit; machine press gets a drop; front raise and upright row are a bi-set. Back day: six sets of cable crunches, rotations, then the only true up-and-down pyramid in all sixteen programs on the barbell row (12-15/10/5/10/12-15), a cable row drop, pulldown 5x5-8, wide row, lateral raise drop, reverse fly. Arms day: abs tri-set, then three biceps and three triceps moves with a pyramid, a straight 4x8, and a failure or rest-pause finisher on each side. Legs: stretching, hack squat 3x8 after warm-ups, DB lunges, extension + walking-lunge bi-set, leg press, leg curl drop, calf press 3x20-30.

Why. Going from three days to four is itself the accumulation; the trainer skipped straight to intensification because the fourth day already added the volume (98 → 107 sets). Delts get 18 sets, the most of any program, because chest-and-shoulders and back-and-rear-delts each put a full delt slot in. The 5x5-8 on the lead lifts is the heaviest straight-set scheme you had seen: five sets is a lot of exposures to a near-limit weight, and it only works because the rest of the day is machines and cables.

Steal this. The 15/12/2x5-10 arm pyramid, and the idea that one lift per day can be 5x5-8 as long as everything around it is moderate.

ABCD02 · specialization · 4 days · 7 weeks · 26 exercises · 106 sets · rest 66 s · reps 9–11

What changed. 62% of exercises kept (Law 4). The novelty is pre-exhaust done seriously: cable fly for six sets of 10-12 before any pressing on push day, and seated cable row for six sets before the barbell row on pull day. Then four chest presses (DB bench pyramid, incline barbell 4x8, incline DB 4x8, machine press with a drop). Pull day has a pulldown and a reverse-grip pulldown back to back. Arms day now uses "one working set then three to failure" on the preacher machine and the rope pushdown, and single-arm French press at 4x6-8. Leg day is compact: hack pyramid, leg press, extension with a strip set, hip abductor (first glute-specific line), lying leg curl, calf pyramid.

Why. The specialization here is chest and arms (19 chest sets, 16 triceps, 16 biceps). Pre-exhaust is the tool: by the time you press, the pecs are already worked, so the press finishes them rather than the triceps or front delts finishing first. Most of the reps sit at 8, so this is also a step heavier than ABCD01; the trainer never ran two blocks at the same rep target in a row.

Steal this. The six-set isolation opener when one muscle refuses to grow from compounds alone. And the rule of "one hard set, then failure sets" for arm finishers, which produces enormous fatigue with only four lines.

ABCD03 · intensification · 4 days · 7 weeks · 30 exercises · 112 sets · rest 75 s · reps 11–12

What it is. The biggest program of the sixteen, and the one where every tool was used at once. Push: bench leg raises + plank bi-set, rotations, bench pyramid 12/10/8/6 with 120 s rest (the first time 120 s appeared), machine press drop, incline DB pyramid 12/10/8/6 at 120 s, cable fly rest-pause, push-ups to failure, a machine shoulder press drop, front raises. Five chest lines. Pull: dragon flag, barbell row pyramid at 120 s, Kroc row with a failure set, pulldown pyramid, V-bar rest-pause, kettlebell row, single-arm laterals. Arms: three antagonist supersets (curl + skull crusher, French press + curl, cable curl + pushdown) with 90 s after each pair. Legs: leg extension 6x10-12 first, then leg press 4x10 at 120 s, hack squat 4x12 with only 45 s, seated curl pyramid, lying curl 4x6-8, calf pyramid.

Why. This is the top of the wave. Volume, pyramids to 6, rest-pause, failure, supersets, and pre-exhaust all in the same seven weeks. It is sustainable for seven weeks precisely because it is only seven weeks, and because the block after it (ABCD04) is the exact opposite. If you ever want to know what "as much as you can recover from" looks like for you, it looks like this. Note the rest on the lead lifts going to 120 s while the isolation rest stays at 60: the see-saw inside the day.

Steal this. The antagonist-superset arms day, which is the most time-efficient way to get 24 arm sets done, and the leg-extension-first leg day for a quad-focused block.

ABCD04 · realization · 4 days · 7 weeks · 26 exercises · 94 sets · rest 99 s · reps 10–11

What changed. Every technique gone except one drop set on reverse flys. Every working line is 4x8 (or 3x8 on legs) with 120 s rest. Push: plank on ball 4x90 s, jackknife, rotations, bench, incline DB, dips, machine fly, lateral raise. Pull: ab wheel, dragon flag, rotations, pull-ups, seated row, V-bar, barbell row, reverse fly. Arms: four lines of 4x8 at 120 s, plus rower crunches 4x30 and cable crunches. Legs: hack, stiff-leg deadlift, extension, seated curl, lunges 3x16 steps, calves, each with one warm-up set and 120 s. Abs get 20 sets, the most of any program.

Why. After ABCD03 the only sensible next block is one that takes everything away except the weight. The straight 4x8 at 120 s is a strength-endurance scheme that lets the loads from the pyramids become working weights for four full sets. The average rest jumping from 75 to 99 s is the clearest signal in the whole table that a block is a realization block. The abs volume ballooning is the trainer using the recovery budget that low-volume heavy work leaves free: abs do not compete with your bench, so when the bench is heavy, abs are cheap.

Steal this. The template itself. "Every line 4x8 at 120 s, one warm-up set, no techniques" is a complete realization block you can write in ten minutes for any split.

5.3 ABCD05 – ABCD08: the strength climb

The split changed again. Arms day was retired and replaced with an upper-body day (MMSS), which put chest and back at two exposures a week. These four blocks are one continuous climb in load: 4x8 pyramids → waves to 6 → 3x8 → 3x6. Rest climbs with it: 89 → 108 → 132 → 119 s.

ABCD05 · peak strength · 4 days · 7 weeks · 23 exercises · 102 sets · rest 89 s · reps 9–10

What it is. Twelve pyramids, all ending in three or four sets of 8, plus warm-ups on nearly everything. Push: cross crunch, rotations, incline DB 2x8wu+4x8 at 120, bench 2x8wu+4x8 at 120, cable fly 4x8-12, front raise pyramid 15/12/2x10, French press 4x8. Pull: crunch 3x30, rotations, barbell row 4x8 at 120, seated row 4x8, pulldown 4x8-12, lateral raise pyramid, preacher curl 4x8. Legs: five lines, each a pyramid finishing at 3-4x8 (extension, leg press, Bulgarian, seated curl, calves with a 15-rep back-off). Upper: DB bench, incline barbell, skull crushers, V-bar, one-arm row, barbell curl, every one 12/10/3x8 at 120 s.

Why. This is the first program with upper body twice a week, and the trainer made the fourth day mirror days A and B with the opposite implements (barbell bench on A, DB bench on D; incline DB on A, incline barbell on D; seated row and barbell row on B, one-arm row and V-bar on D). The pyramid-to-8 scheme lets a second weekly exposure happen without either day being a grind: the 12 and 10 rungs are warm-ups you count, and only the 8s are hard. "Peak" is the label; "heavy 4x8 with a built-in ramp" is what it is.

Steal this. The upper-day mirror rule: same patterns as A and B, opposite tools.

ABCD06 · intensification · 4 days · 7 weeks · 25 exercises · 106 sets · rest 108 s · reps 8–10

What changed. The loads step up. Bench is a wave, 2x8wu+10/8/6/6, with 180 s rest, the first 180 in any program. The one-arm row gets the same wave and the same 180 s. Dips 4x8 at 120. DB press 4x8. Single-arm French press 4x6-8. Concentration curls 4x6-8. Legs: leg press pyramid to 4x8-12, back squat 4x8-12 at 120, stiff-leg deadlift 4x8-12 at 120, single-leg extension 3x8, seated curl with a drop, calf pyramid with a 15-rep back-off. Upper: incline DB 12/10/8/3x6, machine fly 3x6-8 with a drop, DB skull crushers to 3x6-8, pulldown 12/10/8/3x6, single-arm seated row 4x8, DB curl to 3x6-8.

Why. Intensification in this era means "heavier," not "more techniques." The waves and pyramids reach 6 reps on almost every lift, the drop sets survive only on machines (fly, curl, reverse fly), and rest is now above 100 s on average. Chest sits at 21 sets, the second highest ever, but the sets are shorter and heavier. This is the block that decides whether the next two blocks of 3x8 and 3x6 will be productive: the 6-rep rungs here are the rehearsal.

Steal this. The 10/8/6/6 wave. It is a pyramid that spends two sets at the heavy end instead of one, which is where the strength adaptation actually happens.

ABCD07 · specialization (label) — trains as realization · 4 days · 7 weeks · 25 exercises · 74 sets · rest 132 s · reps 8–9

What it is. Every single working line is 2x8wu+3x8 (or 1x8wu+3x8 on arms) with 120–180 s rest. Forty warm-up sets, seventy-four working sets. Push: cable crunch, rotations, incline DB at 180, bench at 180, cable fly 120, DB press 120, skull crushers 120, rope 120. Pull: 200 crunches however many sets it takes, rotations, barbell row, seated row, pulldown all at 180, laterals, concentration curl, hammer curl at 120. Legs: Smith lunge, Smith squat at 180, leg press, seated curl with a rest-pause finisher, calf press with a back-off. Upper: DB bench, machine press, single-arm French, V-bar, one-arm row, preacher, all 3x8.

Why. Whatever the tag says, this is a strength block: the exercise count is the same as ABCD06 but the sets dropped from 106 to 74, the rest went to 132 s, and there is nothing in it but straight sets. Two warm-ups on every exercise, not just the first, is the tell: when the working weight is heavy on the isolation moves too, they need ramping. The 200-rep crunch target is the abs equivalent of ABCD04's 20 sets: cheap work in a block with spare recovery.

Steal this. "3x8 everything, two warm-ups everything, 120–180 s everything" as the first half of a two-block strength peak.

ABCD08 · realization · 4 days · 7 weeks · 25 exercises · 73 sets · rest 119 s · reps 6–7

What changed. 60% of exercises kept, and the 8s became 6s. That is the entire difference. Bench 2x8wu+3x6 at 180, dips 3x6, cable fly 3x6-8, machine press 3x6, pushdown 3x6, skull crushers 3x6. Pull: 300 crunches, then wide row, seated row, V-bar, laterals 3x8, concentration and preacher curls 3x6. Legs: extension 3x6-8, leg press 3x6-8, DB lunges 3x6, seated curl 3x8 with rest-pause, Smith calves 3x8-12. Upper: incline DB, machine press, DB skull crushers, pulldown, one-arm row, barbell curl, all 3x6.

Why. This is the heaviest program of the sixteen and the smallest. It is the payoff for ABCD05, 06, and 07. Because the movements are the ones from ABCD07, every weight on the bar is a known quantity plus a small jump; the trainer did not make you learn a new exercise while lifting the most you had ever lifted. That is Law 4 at its most literal. After this block the loads were as high as they were going to get in this cycle, and the next program had to be a reset.

Steal this. The pairing. A 3x8 block followed by the same exercises at 3x6 is the simplest two-step strength peak there is, and it is exactly what your own programs have not done yet.

5.4 ABCD09 – ABCD11: reset and rebuild

ABCD09 · accumulation · 4 days · 7 weeks · 31 exercises · 96 sets · rest 91 s · reps 10

What it is. Twenty-eight of thirty-one exercises are new. Everything is 3x10 after one or two warm-up sets, with 80–180 s rest. No techniques at all: thirty straight-set lines. Push: dumbbell circles 4x12 as a shoulder warm-up, reverse-grip Smith bench, weighted dips, incline seated cable fly at 37°, seated Viking press, barbell triceps extension, pushdown, lying chest-supported wrist curls, cable crunch. Pull: rotations, seated lat pull-in, T-bar row, Reeves row, behind-the-back upright row, cable preacher, hammer curl, Zercher back curl (1x5wu+3x5, the only straight-sets-of-5 line in sixteen programs), weighted boat crunch. Legs: barbell hack squat, Smith squat, single-leg Smith good morning, quad-focused leg press, calves, Bosu crunches. Upper: 30° incline Smith, flat DB fly, JM press, rack pulls, pullover, incline DB curl, dead-stop cheat curl, weighted sit-ups.

Why. After fourteen weeks at 3x8 and 3x6, the reset has to do three things: bring the reps up so the joints get a break from limit loads, change the movements so there is a fresh stimulus and no pressure to match old numbers, and keep the rest fairly long because the strength you built means even 3x10 is now heavy. All three are here. The exotic exercise selection (Reeves row, Zercher back curl, rack pulls, JM press, Viking press) is not showing off; it is the point. A new exercise is "free" progress for four to six weeks because the early gains are neural. Forearms appear for the first time because there is room.

Steal this. The reset recipe: 3x10, straight sets, mostly new movements, moderate rest. And the principle that novelty is a recovery tool, not just a boredom tool.

ABCD10 · intensification · 4 days · 7 weeks · 23 exercises · 73 sets · rest 83 s · reps 9–10

What changed. Back to familiar movements (22% carried over from ABCD09, but most are old friends from earlier blocks), and the scheme is 12/10/8/5 on every lead lift: bench, dips, pushdown, V-bar, barbell row, preacher machine, leg extension, seated curl. Accessories are 3x10. Only 23 exercises and 73 sets, the same size as ABCD08. Upper day: incline DB and pulldown with 2x10-12 plus a drop, lateral raise, rope, upright row, incline curl. Plank 3x2 min.

Why. The 5-rep top set on every pyramid is a heavy single-set touch of the weights from ABCD08, once per exercise per week, inside a program that is otherwise moderate. That is how you keep strength while the volume stays low enough to recover from the reset block. The small exercise count means each pyramid gets full attention. Quads at 7 sets is the lowest in any program; leg day is five lines. This is a lean, focused block, which is what an intensification block after a novelty block should be.

Steal this. The 12/10/8/5 pyramid as a strength-maintenance device: it hides one near-max set inside four sets of ramping.

ABCD11 · peak (label) — trains as volume rebuild · 4 days · 7 weeks · 29 exercises · 88 sets · rest 77 s · reps 10–11

What changed. 57% carried over. The pyramids stay on the compounds (machine press, dips, pulldown, V-bar, leg extension, all 2x8wu+12/10/8/5), but the accessories go from 3x10 to 3x12 and multiply: three triceps moves on push day, three biceps moves on pull day. Smith squat, Smith lunge, and leg press at 3x12. Seated curl 12/10/8/8+Drop. Upper day brings drop sets back on the incline machine, incline DB, and wide row (2x10-12+1xDrop), with laterals, single-arm laterals, single-arm French press, rope, and incline curls. Mountain climbers and rower crunches as an abs bi-set. Rest falls back to 77 s.

Why. This closes the cycle by rebuilding volume on top of the strength from ABCD10: same heavy pyramids, more and longer accessory work, drop sets back on the upper day. The trainer called it a peak; structurally it is the first half of a new accumulation, and had the sequence continued the next block would likely have been a full accumulation with new movements. Arms at 15 triceps / 12 biceps and quads at 13 show the volume coming back everywhere at once.

Steal this. The split-personality block: heavy pyramids on compounds and high-rep accessories in the same session lets you hold strength and add volume for a few weeks before a full reset. This is also the last program your trainer wrote before you started writing your own, so it is the block your ABC+D1 should have been compared against.

6. How the Programs Connect: The Wave

Individually, each program is a sensible seven or ten weeks. Together they are something better: three overlapping waves of load, each one built on the last. Here is the whole sequence drawn as one timeline.

Four cycles. Blue bars are working sets, orange dots are average rest. Each cycle ends with the shortest bar and the highest dot, then resets.

Read it as three cycles.

Cycle 1 — ABC01 → ABC05 (50 weeks, three days)

acc → int → spe → rea → acc. Build the base (01), make it harder (02), redistribute toward back and posterior chain (03), cut and cash in (04), reset with new movements and an arms emphasis (05). The rep target drifts from 8-12 down to 6-8 and back. Rest barely moves (61 → 70 → 64) because the whole cycle lives in the moderate-load world. This is the "learn the system" cycle.

Cycle 2 — ABCD01 → ABCD04 (28 weeks, arms-day split)

int → spe → int → rea. The fourth day added the volume, so accumulation was skipped. Drops and 5x5-8 (01), pre-exhaust and failure sets on chest and arms (02), everything at once at 112 sets (03), then strip it all to 4x8 at 120 s (04). Rest climbs from 65 to 99 s across the cycle. This is the "find the ceiling" cycle.

Cycle 3 — ABCD05 → ABCD08 (28 weeks, upper-day split)

4x8 pyramids → waves to 6 → 3x8 → 3x6. A straight strength ladder with two upper exposures a week. Sets fall 102 → 106 → 74 → 73; rest rises 89 → 108 → 132 → 119. Each block keeps most of the previous block's exercises so the weights carry forward. This is the "get strong" cycle and it is the one your own programs have not yet reproduced.

Cycle 4 (partial) — ABCD09 → ABCD11 (21 weeks)

acc (novelty) → int (12/10/8/5) → volume rebuild. Reset with new movements at 3x10, re-touch the heavy weights inside pyramids, then add accessory volume back. Sets 96 → 73 → 88. Where it was heading: a full accumulation block, then another climb.

4. D1 → D2 kept only 22% of exercises

Inside a wave, blocks share exercises so the pyramids in the intensification block start from known weights. Going from D1 to D2 you changed the movements and added pyramids and rest-pause, so every pyramid began with a guessed load. D2 → D3 at 45% was much better. For D3 → D4, keep 55–65%.

Minor, but worth knowing

Hamstrings at 3 sets in D1 on a nine-line leg day is quad-heavier than anything the trainer wrote (his floor was 3 too, but on six-line leg days). Forearms at 4–6 sets is fine and was your own addition; keep it as a ride-along. And D1 had 33 explicit warm-up sets at 60 s rest, which is a lot of clock: warm-ups on every line belong to the heavy blocks, where the rest is long anyway.

8. The Build-Your-Own Playbook

Ten steps. Do them in order and write the answers down before you write a single exercise. Most bad programs come from starting at step 5.

Step 1 — Locate yourself in the wave

Look at the last two blocks. Which phase were they? How many sets, what rest, what reps? The transition table in section 6 tells you what comes next. If the last block was a build-up (acc, int, spe) and you have not cashed in for two or more blocks, the next block is realization, no matter how much you want to try a new exercise. If the last block was a realization or strength block, the next is a reset, no matter how strong you feel.

Step 3 — Choose the split and the block length together

Three days = 10 weeks. Four days = 7 weeks. If you change the split, treat the first block as accumulation regardless of what you call it. Do not change the split in a realization block; the whole point of realization is that everything is familiar except the weight.

Step 4 — Decide push/pull balance and the one target

Chest sets ≈ back sets, within three. Then pick one muscle that gets the surplus this block, and one that pays for it. Write both names down. If you cannot name the payer, you are about to add volume instead of moving it, and the set count from step 2 will tell on you.

Step 5 — Fill the slots with patterns, then pick implements

For each day, write the slots first (section 4) as patterns: "horizontal press, incline press, fly, delt press, long-head triceps, pushdown." Only then choose the tool for each. Two rules for the tools:

  • Carry-over. Inside a wave, keep 55–65% of last block's exercises; the ones you keep should be the lead lifts. Between waves, keep 10–30%.
  • Mirror on the upper day. Whatever implement days A and B used, day D uses the other one for the same pattern.

Step 6 — Assign the rep schemes slot by slot

The lead lift gets the phase's lead scheme and the longest rest. The second compound gets a straight scheme one notch lighter. Isolation gets the phase's accessory scheme and, in the light phases, the day's one technique. Arms and delts get straight sets or a bi-set. Never put a fatigue technique on slot 1.

Step 7 — Warm-ups

Light phases: two warm-up sets on the first compound of each muscle group, none elsewhere (or let the pyramid be the warm-up). Heavy phases: two on every compound, one on every isolation. Warm-ups are 8 reps at roughly 50–60% of the working weight, and they are not counted in the set total.

Step 8 — Add the ride-alongs

Rotations 1–2x10 at the top of every upper day. Abs first on two or three days (cable crunch, leg raises, ab wheel, dragon flag, plank; AMRAP totals in the heavy blocks). Calves 3–6 sets on leg day. Forearms if you want them, 3–6 sets on pull day. These do not count against the target muscle budget, but they do count in the set total, so budget 12–20 sets for them.

Step 9 — Audit the numbers

  • Total working sets inside the phase range from step 2?
  • Chest within three sets of back?
  • Exactly one muscle above its usual range, and one below?
  • Techniques: zero or one per day in heavy phases, two or three in light phases, none on slot 1?
  • Rest on slot 1 the longest of the day, isolation the shortest?
  • Block length matches the split?
  • Lead lifts carried over from last block if this is the same wave?

If any line fails, fix it now. It is much cheaper to delete an exercise on paper than to discover in week four that the program does not recover.

Step 10 — Write the exit criteria

Decide before you start what ends the block: the calendar (7 or 10 weeks), or two consecutive sessions where the lead lifts did not move, whichever comes first. Then decide, also before you start, what the next phase will be. A block with a known successor is a block you will finish.

11. One-Page Reference Card

The laws

  1. Volume ↔ intensity see-saw. Heavier means fewer sets, fewer exercises, longer rest.
  2. Fatigue techniques only when reps ≥ 8 and rest ≤ 90 s. Heavy blocks are straight sets.
  3. Patterns are permanent; implements rotate.
  4. Same wave: keep 55–65% of exercises, change the scheme. New wave: change 70–90% of exercises.
  5. A block ≈ 30 sessions. 3 days → 10 weeks. 4 days → 7 weeks.
  6. Chest ≈ back. One target muscle per block, one payer.
  7. Rotations, abs, calves, warm-ups: always. Abs and rotations first.

The day

Prep (abs + cuff) → heavy compound (2 wu, longest rest) → second compound, different angle or tool → isolation (technique lives here) → delts → two arm moves. Rest and load fall left to right.

The sequence

acc → int → (spe) → rea → reset. Two or three up, one to cash in, one to reset. Never more than three build-ups in a row.
