If You Only Remember 10 Things

  1. Markets coordinate billions of transactions daily without anyone in charge — the right amount of tuna arrives at a Paris restaurant because prices, not bureaucrats, carry the information.
  2. Every policy, regulation, and institution creates incentives that shape behavior — sometimes in the opposite direction from what was intended.
  3. The market is amoral, not immoral — it rewards scarcity rather than social value, which is why diamonds cost more than water.
  4. Government is essential (property rights, public goods, externalities) but easily captured by small, organized interest groups who extract benefits at everyone else's expense.
  5. Information asymmetry explains why health insurance markets fail, why McDonald's succeeds, and why your real estate agent may not have your best interests at heart.
  6. Human capital — skills, education, creativity — is the only sustainable source of rising living standards for individuals and nations alike.
  7. Financial markets are powerful wealth-creation engines, but perverse incentives (principal-agent problems, short-term bonus structures) produced the 2008 catastrophe.
  8. The Federal Reserve controls the money supply and therefore the pace of economic growth — getting monetary policy wrong can cause depressions, hyperinflation, or world wars.
  9. Trade makes all participating countries richer on average, but the gains are diffuse while the losses are concentrated — which is why protectionism is politically popular despite being economically destructive.
  10. Poor countries are poor primarily because of bad institutions (corruption, weak property rights, bad governance) — not because of insufficient natural resources or foreign aid.

Foreword

Burton G. Malkiel, the Princeton economist famous for A Random Walk Down Wall Street, opens with a defense of his profession. Thomas Carlyle called economics the “dismal science” not because it was dull but because its central truth is uncomfortable: scarcity is pervasive. We must choose between competing satisfactions, between jam today and jam tomorrow, and everything worth having requires work and sacrifice. Malkiel argues that the public image of economists as boring number-crunchers is largely their own fault — opaque writing, excessive mathematics, and an unwillingness to admit what they don't know.

Economics is harder than physics, Malkiel contends, because you cannot run controlled experiments on human behavior. Weather forecasting models that simply predict “tomorrow will be like today” often outperform sophisticated satellite analysis. Yet despite this uncertainty, economics has produced genuine knowledge. We know that incentives strongly influence behavior. We know that every sale involves a voluntary exchange. We know that obvious profit opportunities are rarely overlooked — the foundation of efficient markets theory.

“Practical men, who believe themselves to be quite exempt from any intellectual influence, are usually the slaves of some defunct economist.”— John Maynard Keynes

What surprised Malkiel most is how much economists agree. Ask ten economists about taxi medallions in New York, rent control, or free trade, and you will get strikingly similar answers. A bipartisan majority of economists is quite likely to unite on the opposite side of a bipartisan coalition of politicians. The real division, Malkiel suggests, is not between liberal and conservative economists but between economists of any stripe and politicians of any party.

Thinking like an economist means identifying trade-offs within constraints, measuring costs in terms of foregone benefits, pursuing efficiency, taking an incremental approach, and recognizing that competitive markets are a particularly efficient mechanism for expressing individual choices. Wheelan, Malkiel says, has an “anti-Midas touch — if he touched gold he would turn it to life.” The book contains no equations, no graphs, and no jargon, yet it covers the full landscape of modern economics.

Introduction

The scene is strikingly familiar. A graduate student stands at the front of a lecture hall drawing graphs on a chalkboard. The material is dry and mathematical. Students are rarely asked why basic economics made the collapse of the Soviet Union inevitable, what economic benefit smokers provide to nonsmokers (they die earlier, leaving more Social Security and pension benefits for the rest of us), or why mandating generous maternity leave may actually hurt women in the job market. These are the kinds of provocative, counterintuitive questions that make economics fascinating — and they never appear on the exam.

The result is that many of the brightest, most intellectually curious people are economically illiterate. The media are full of references to Ben Bernanke and the global financial crisis, but how many citizens can explain what the Federal Reserve chairman actually does? Every political debate features candidates warning that outsourcing and globalization are “stealing” American jobs. That assertion is the economic equivalent of warning that the U.S. Navy is at risk of sailing over the edge of the world.

“This is not economics for dummies; it is economics for smart people who never studied economics.”— Charles Wheelan

The French 35-hour workweek is a perfect case study in economic illiteracy. In 2000, the Socialist-led government lowered the maximum workweek from thirty-nine to thirty-five hours to combat chronic double-digit unemployment. The logic: if people with jobs work fewer hours, there will be work left over for the unemployed. The policy has a certain intuitive appeal — then again, so does using leeches to suck toxins out of the body. The French policy was based on the lump of labor fallacy: the mistaken belief that there is a fixed number of jobs in an economy that must be rationed. The American economy created millions of Internet-related jobs that didn't exist in 1980, all without any government directive to divvy up work hours. By 2008, the Sarkozy government passed legislation allowing companies to negotiate the thirty-five-hour requirement away.

Even free-market advocates concede that unfettered markets can produce deeply flawed outcomes. Milton Friedman himself acknowledged this. Consider the American love affair with automobiles: we buy the car, pay for gas and insurance, but we don't pay for the emissions we leave behind, the congestion we cause, the wear on public roads, or the danger we pose to drivers in smaller cars. The effect is like being on the town with Dad's credit card — we do a lot of things we wouldn't do if we had to pay the whole bill.

Claudia Goldin and Cecilia Rouse, economists at Harvard and Princeton, found an elegant way to study discrimination. In the 1950s, American orchestras began using “blind” auditions, with aspiring musicians performing behind screens. When judges couldn't see the performer's gender, women were roughly fifty percent more likely to advance past the first round and several times more likely to make the final cut. Economics is not just about money — it is a powerful set of analytical tools that can be used to look back and explain why events unfolded as they did, to look around and make sense of the world, and to look forward and anticipate the effects of major policy changes.

The 2008 financial crisis was predictable from the incentives built into the system. Mortgage brokers were paid on commission by banks that made the loans, so more mortgages meant more commissions and bigger mortgages meant bigger commissions. Banks didn't worry about loan quality because they could quickly sell those loans to third-party investors — an adult version of hot potato where it doesn't matter how bad the loan is as long as you pass it on before the borrower defaults. Wall Street then bundled thousands of these questionable mortgages into securities and sold them to investors around the world. The chain of misaligned incentives was, in hindsight, a disaster waiting to happen.

Behavioral economics has emerged as one of the most productive areas of the field, combining psychology and economics to explore how humans actually make decisions. We underestimate some risks (obesity) and overestimate others (flying). We let emotion cloud our judgment and overreact to both good news and bad news. As New York Times columnist David Brooks noted, classical economic models explain a lot — but not how so many people could be “so stupid, incompetent and self-destructive all at once.”

Most of the great ideas in economics are intuitive when the dressings of complexity are peeled away. One need not know where to place a load-bearing wall to appreciate the genius of Frank Lloyd Wright. The most important concepts derive their power from bringing logic and rigor to bear on everyday problems. That is naked economics — and it should not be accessible only to the experts.

Chapter 1: The Power of Markets

In 1989, as the Berlin Wall was toppling, Douglas Ivester, head of Coca-Cola Europe, made a snap decision. He sent his sales force to Berlin and told them to start handing out Coke. Free. Representatives were literally passing bottles through holes in the Wall. East German currency was worthless — scraps of paper to the rest of the world — so the venture was a money-losing proposition in the short run. But by 1995, per capita Coke consumption in the former East Germany had risen to match West Germany, already a strong market. It was Adam Smith's invisible hand passing Coca-Cola through the Berlin Wall. Coke representatives weren't undertaking a humanitarian gesture; they were expanding their global market, boosting profits, and making shareholders happy. And that is the punch line of capitalism: the market aligns incentives so that individuals working for their own self-interest produce a thriving and ever-improving standard of living for most members of society.

Economists sometimes ask, “Who feeds Paris?” — a rhetorical question drawing attention to the mind-numbing complexity of a modern economy. The right amount of fresh tuna makes its way from a South Pacific fishing fleet to a restaurant on the Rue de Rivoli. A neighborhood fruit vendor has exactly what his customers want every morning, from coffee to fresh papayas, even though those products may come from ten or fifteen different countries. No government bureaucrat orchestrates any of this. A Soviet official visiting an American pharmacy marveled at the thousands of remedies lining the shelves and asked, “But how can you make sure that every store stocks all of these items?” The question betrayed a total misunderstanding of how markets work. There is no central authority telling stores what to stock. The Soviet economy failed because government bureaucrats tried to direct everything from the number of bars of soap produced by a factory in Irkutsk to the number of university students studying electrical engineering in Moscow. The task proved overwhelming.

“Economy is the art of making the most of life.”— Gary Becker (borrowing from George Bernard Shaw)

Gary Becker, the University of Chicago economist who won the Nobel Prize in 1992, captured the essence of the discipline. Economics studies how we allocate a finite supply of everything worth having: oil, clean water, perfect bodies, people who can fix jammed photocopy machines. The disparities are stark. Alex Rodriguez was paid $275 million to play baseball while one in five American children lives in poverty. Near Wheelan's home in Chicago, the Three Dog Bakery sells birthday cakes for dogs at $16 each while the Chicago Coalition for the Homeless estimates that fifteen thousand people sleep on the streets on any given night. Three-quarters of the people in Chad lack access to clean drinking water. The World Bank estimates that half the world's population survives on less than $2 a day.

Economics begins with one foundational assumption: individuals act to make themselves as well off as possible. They seek to maximize their utility, a concept similar to happiness but broader — it includes getting a typhoid immunization and paying taxes. Crucially, maximizing utility is not synonymous with acting selfishly. Oseola McCarty, a ninety-one-year-old laundress from Hattiesburg, Mississippi, gave away $150,000 to endow a scholarship at the University of Southern Mississippi — a school she had never attended. She simply derived more utility from giving than from spending on herself. Wesley Autrey jumped onto New York subway tracks to shield a stranger having convulsions as the Number 1 train bore down on them, with his two young daughters watching from the platform. Americans give more than $200 billion to charity annually. None of this contradicts the utility framework.

The concept of opportunity cost is richer than the cash price on a receipt. There is nothing “free” about concert tickets if you have to stand in the rain for six hours. Taking the bus for $1.50 may cost more than a $7 taxi if you're running late for a meeting with a client who controls a $50,000 account. The real cost of anything is what you must give up to get it. This broader view of cost explains important social phenomena: a ten percent decrease in the street price of cocaine increases the number of adult users by roughly ten percent. Falling birth rates in the developed world aren't caused by children becoming more expensive to feed and clothe — those costs have actually fallen. The primary cost of raising a child today is the earnings forgone when a parent quits or cuts back on work, and because women now have far better professional opportunities, this opportunity cost has skyrocketed. As one economist's neighbor put it after leaving her career to raise her second child, “It's expensive to quit being a neurologist.”

Firms take inputs — land, steel, knowledge, baseball stadiums — and combine them to add value, like a chef turning $30 of groceries into an $80 meal. Markets direct resources to their most productive use. Brad Pitt earns about $30 million per film, not because he's the world's best actor in absolute terms, but because his unique talent can “open” a movie — millions of people will see a film just because he's in it. Insurance agencies would pay him $30,000 for the same charisma, so he goes where he adds the most value. Sherwin Rosen of the University of Chicago called this the superstar phenomenon: in markets with very large audiences, small differences in talent get magnified into enormous pay differentials. LeBron James needs to be only slightly better than his competitors to capture an enormous share of a massive market.

Prices serve as information signals — “giant neon billboards,” in Wheelan's phrase. When tuna becomes more popular at Parisian restaurants, the restaurateur orders more from his wholesaler. The wholesale price rises. Fishermen in the Pacific earn more for their tuna catch. Some salmon fishermen switch to tuna. Others keep their boats out longer or invest in more expensive fishing methods. Nobody coordinated any of this — prices did all the work. Meanwhile, barriers to entry prevent some markets from functioning competitively: truffles cost $500 a pound because they grow only in the wild (found by pigs or dogs), Pfizer holds a legal monopoly on Viagra through its patent, American and United control eighty percent of the gates at O'Hare Airport, and Microsoft Word dominates not because it's the best software but because of network effects — everyone uses it, making switching prohibitively costly.

Price discrimination — selling the same item to different people at different prices — is everywhere once you learn to spot it. Airlines are the masters: a Saturday-night stay requirement separates business travelers willing to pay $1,800 from leisure travelers who'll shell out no more than $250. Al Gore complained during the 2000 campaign that his mother and his dog took the same arthritis medication but his mother paid far more. The same medicine sold to humans and pets at different prices is airline seats all over again. Technology will only make price discrimination more precise. Priceline lets every customer bid a different amount for the same hotel room. Grocery stores post one price, charge another to coupon clippers, and a third to holders of frequent-shopper cards that track every purchase.

The market economy is a powerful force for making our lives better — firms can only earn profits by delivering goods that people actually want to buy. Competition drives innovation and relentlessly pushes costs down. In 1900, a three-minute phone call from New York to Chicago cost $5.45, the equivalent of about $140 today. Now the same call is essentially free. But the market is amoral, not immoral. It rewards scarcity, which has no inherent relation to social value. Diamonds are worth thousands of dollars a carat while water — infinitely more essential to life — flows nearly free from the tap. The market does not provide goods that we need; it provides goods that we want to buy. The most talented doctors provide face-lifts for Hollywood stars who can pay rather than health insurance for the poor who cannot. Markets are “what democracy is to government: a decent, if flawed, choice among many bad alternatives.”

Chapter 2: Incentives Matter

The black rhinoceros is one of the most endangered species on the planet. About four thousand roam southern Africa, down from sixty-five thousand in 1970. A single rhino horn fetches $30,000 on the black market — believed to be an aphrodisiac and fever reducer in parts of Asia, and used for traditional dagger handles in Yemen — in countries where per capita income hovers around $1,000. The black rhino is worth far more dead than alive to the impoverished people who live near it. Economics can explain exactly why the species is in trouble and suggest what might actually save it.

Communal ownership creates perverse incentives. If a single greedy, selfish rancher owned all the world's rhinos, he would breed and protect them just as cattle ranchers manage their herds — not from altruism, but because a steady supply of horns is more valuable than a one-time slaughter. But rhinos are communal property. Local villagers get no benefit from living rhinos (only crop damage). Conservation is a free-rider problem: if one tour company invests in protecting rhinos, competing companies enjoy the same benefit without the cost. The only party with a clear, powerful incentive is the poacher.

Conservation solutions that work all share one feature: they align incentives. Costa Rica set aside more than twenty-five percent of its land as national parks. Tourism generates over $1 billion annually — eleven percent of national income — and if local citizens share the profits from eco-tourism, they have a powerful reason to keep the animals alive. The mountain gorilla offers a cautionary counterexample. Only about 620 remain in the dense jungles of East Africa. Locals preserved gorilla habitat not from any great respect for the animals but because tourist money exceeded the value of timber. When civil wars devastated the region and tourists stopped coming, the incentive collapsed.

“The gorillas are important when they bring in tourists. If not, they are not. If the tourists don't come, we will try our luck in the forest.”— local man quoted in the New York Times

Adam Smith's foundational insight remains the most powerful idea in economics: “It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest.” Bill Gates didn't drop out of Harvard to join the Peace Corps — he founded Microsoft, became one of the richest people alive, and launched the personal computer revolution in the process. Self-interest makes the world go around. The slogan “From each according to his abilities, to each according to his needs” made a wonderful folk song; as an economic system, it led to everything from inefficiency to mass starvation. East German car factories were literally destroying value — the manufacturing process was so inefficient that finished cars were worth less than the raw steel that went into them. The Hindustan Fertilizer Corporation in India operated for twelve years with twelve hundred employees reporting to work daily, but the plant never produced a single ton of salable fertilizer. There was no mechanism to shut it down because the government was bankrolling the operation.

The principal-agent problem pervades modern economies. Burger King's sign — “Your meal is free if you don't get a receipt” — is an ingenious solution. Rather than spending money to monitor cashiers who might sell food without ringing it up and pocket the cash, Burger King enlists customers to do the monitoring. Stock options were supposed to be the CEO equivalent of the Burger King receipt sign, aligning executive compensation with shareholder value. Paul Volcker warned Wheelan that options are “an instrument of the devil.” He was right: Michael Jensen of Harvard Business School calls options “managerial heroin” because they create incentives for managers to chase short-term stock price gains while inflicting long-term damage. Companies with large options grants are more likely to engage in accounting fraud.

The 2008 financial crisis was a principal-agent catastrophe on a global scale. Investment bankers earned enormous commissions buying and selling “toxic assets” without bearing the full risk — heads they win, tails the firm loses. Credit rating agencies gave stellar ratings to securities that later proved worthless because the agencies were paid by the very firms selling the bonds being rated — “a little like a used car salesman paying an appraiser to stand around the lot and provide helpful advice to customers.” At Lehman Brothers, those most responsible for the collapse were never required to return the massive bonuses they'd earned in the good years.

The prisoner's dilemma illustrates how rational individual behavior can produce terrible collective outcomes. Two suspects, separated for interrogation, each face the same calculus: confessing is the best strategy regardless of what your partner does. Yet both confessing (twenty-five years each) produces a far worse outcome than both staying silent (five years each). This logic plays out in fisheries worldwide. Rhode Island fisherman John Sorlien told the New York Times: “Right now, my only incentive is to go out and kill as many fish as I can. I have no incentive to conserve the fishery, because any fish I leave is just going to be picked up by the next guy.”

The solution is property rights. In Port Lincoln, Australia, the community capped the number of lobster traps and sold transferable licenses. A license purchased in 1984 for $2,000 now fetches roughly $35,000. Lobsterman Daryl Spencer put it simply: “Why hurt the fishery? It's my retirement fund. No one's going to pay me $35,000 a pot if there are no lobsters left.” A 2008 Science study found that fisheries managed with individual transferable quotas were half as likely to collapse as those using traditional methods.

Perverse incentives — the law of unintended consequences — produce some of the most counterintuitive policy failures. Requiring car seats for children on airplanes sounds sensible until you realize that forcing families to buy an extra seat makes some choose to drive instead, and driving is dramatically more dangerous than flying. Mexico City's 1989 pollution program banned cars one day per week based on license plate numbers; families responded by buying second, older, dirtier cars, and air quality actually got worse. London's congestion charge shows incentives used correctly: a five-pound fee for driving into central London during peak hours cut traffic twenty percent, doubled average speeds, halved bus delays, and increased bus ridership by fourteen percent.

Chapter 3: Government and the Economy

When Wheelan's wife got pregnant, he traded his Honda Civic for a Ford Explorer. The decision to buy an SUV affected everyone else on the road — the danger to drivers in smaller cars, the exhaust, the congestion, the contribution to climate change — yet none of those people had any say in the transaction. An externality exists whenever the private costs of behavior differ from the social costs. At the Ford dealership, the price tag included gas, insurance, and monthly payments but nothing for asthmatic children, pregnant women in Mini Coopers, or Pacific island nations at risk of submersion.

When externalities are significant, the market doesn't just fail to solve the problem — the market is the problem. In the movie Michael Clayton, a polluting agribusiness company maximizes profits by selling a pesticide that poisons local water supplies. Farmers unknowingly reward the company by buying its cheaper, more effective product. Competitors who invest in non-toxic alternatives are punished by the market. The only redress comes through government-supported mechanisms: the courts. Robert Frank of Cornell argues that America is in an SUV arms race — families buy bigger vehicles because everyone else does. The solution isn't to ban the Hummer but to raise the private cost of driving it so that it reflects the social cost.

Ronald Coase, who won the Nobel Prize in 1991, showed that when transaction costs are low and property rights are clearly defined, private parties can resolve externalities without government intervention. If Wheelan values quiet at $100 an hour and his neighbor Stuart values bongo drumming at $50, Wheelan can pay Stuart $75 to stop — both are better off. An Ohio power company emitting a “bizarre blue plume” simply bought the entire town it was poisoning, paying residents roughly three times their home values in exchange for signed agreements never to sue. But Coase's solution only works when the number of parties is small. CO2 emissions affect six billion people — and it takes a long time to write checks to six billion people, especially when you're already late for work.

Government doesn't merely fix market failures — it makes markets possible in the first place. Countries without functioning governments are not oases of free-market prosperity; they are places where conducting even the simplest business is expensive and dangerous. Nigeria's firms face “BYOI” — bring your own infrastructure. Angola spent $900 million in oil revenue on weapons in 1999 while one in three children died before age five. A UN report placed much of the blame for world poverty squarely on bad government.

Government defines and protects property rights, without which markets cannot function. You own your home, your car, your business — you can sell them, rent them, or use them as collateral for a loan. Without property rights, imagine tending crops all summer only to have your neighbor harvest them. Pfizer's Viagra patent allows the company to charge $7 per pill when the ingredients cost pennies, but without that patent protection, Pfizer would never have invested the roughly $600 million it costs to bring a new drug to market. When patents expire, generic competitors typically drop the price by eighty to ninety percent.

Government provides public goods — things the private sector chronically underprovides because it's impossible to exclude people who don't pay. A lighthouse, once built, protects all ships; you can't tell a captain to close his eyes as he sails past. National defense, basic research, law enforcement, and parks all share this characteristic. Stephen King tested the free-rider problem by offering installments of a novel online for $1 per download on the honor system, warning the story would end if fewer than seventy-five percent paid. Only forty-six percent did, and the project folded.

Libertarian paternalism, a concept developed by Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein, offers a middle ground between pure laissez-faire and heavy-handed regulation. The key insight: decisions are powerfully driven by defaults and inertia. If your employer auto-enrolls you in a retirement savings plan, most people stick with it. If they don't auto-enroll, many never sign up even when the option is available. The “nudge” is to make the default option the one that's best for most people — anyone can opt out at any time. Spain's “opt-out” organ donation system (you're a donor unless you indicate otherwise) produces a cadaveric donation rate fifty percent higher than America's opt-in system.

Government also redistributes wealth, but economics cannot tell us how much redistribution is “right.” Amartya Sen's thought experiment: three men need a single job. The first is the poorest. The second is the unhappiest (recently impoverished, unaccustomed to deprivation). The third has a chronic health problem that wages could cure. Who should get the job? There is no right answer — and the same fundamental ambiguity applies to every redistribution question in a modern economy.

Chapter 4: Government and the Economy II

Government is good at doing some things and tragically bad at doing others. The Department of Motor Vehicles has a monopoly on driver's licenses — and it shows. Why would they be friendly, extend hours, or keep the office clean? None of these things will produce even one more customer. If a rat runs across the counter at your Chinese take-out restaurant, you stop ordering there. If you stop going to the DMV, you go to jail. Government is like the Soviet Lada joke: a woman orders a car, is told to return in two years on March seventeenth. “Morning or afternoon?” she asks. “The plumber is coming that day.” If the USSR taught us anything, it's that monopoly stifles innovation.

Even where government has an important role, it doesn't have to produce the goods itself. Government can plan, finance, and solicit competitive bids from private contractors. At a health care town hall during the Obama administration, a woman declared, “I don't want government-run health care. I don't want socialized medicine. And don't touch my Medicare.” The irony: Medicare is government-run health care — Americans over sixty-five see private doctors and get reimbursed by the federal government. Even the CIA opened its own venture capital firm, In-Q-It (named after Q from James Bond), because government procurement was too slow to capture cutting-edge technology.

Regulation carries inherent trade-offs. Milton Friedman recounted an exchange at the American Bar Association where an economist argued for more permissive bar admission standards. A lawyer objected: “The country cannot afford anything but Cadillac lawyers!” But if only Cadillacs were available, most people couldn't afford any transportation at all. Sometimes Toyota Corollas are perfectly adequate. George Stigler, who won the Nobel Prize in 1982, demonstrated that firms and professional associations often seek regulation as a tool to hobble competitors. The Illinois Cosmetology Association lobbied for stricter manicurist licensing not to protect consumers but to block immigrant-run discount nail salons charging $6 instead of $25.

DDT illustrates the agonizing trade-offs regulation involves. It is a persistent organic pollutant that devastates ecosystems. Should it be banned? Malaria kills more than a million people annually — three hundred million suffer from the disease — and DDT is the most cost-effective way to control mosquitoes. The next best alternative is four times more expensive and less effective. A Tanzanian researcher calculated that if seven Boeing 747s full of children crashed into Mount Kilimanjaro every single day, the world would notice — that's the scale of malaria deaths. Even the Sierra Club and the Environmental Defense Fund endorsed DDT for malaria control. When 120 nations met to ban persistent organic pollutants, they specifically exempted DDT.

Crony capitalism — when politicians decide who gets credit — corrupts the allocation of capital that markets handle so well. Politically connected projects get funding while worthy ventures starve. When two leading sites for a particle accelerator were under consideration in the early 1990s, northern Illinois had the existing infrastructure and federal lab. Texas got the project. A technology expert explained: “Because George Bush was president.” The government spent roughly $1 billion on the Texas project and then abandoned it.

Fiscal drag — the idea that taxes reduce economic well-being even when spent wisely — operates in two ways. First, taxes directly reduce purchasing power: every dollar spent on jet fighters is a dollar not spent on televisions, vacations, or charity. Government may not be creating jobs, just moving them around. Second, taxes change behavior in wasteful ways. At fifty percent marginal tax rates, some workers simply quit — the worker loses income, and the government collects nothing. This deadweight loss is like a burglar stealing your family photo album: the thief gains nothing, yet you lose something irreplaceable.

The Laffer Curve — the idea that at very high tax rates, cutting taxes can actually increase revenue — is true in theory but has been grotesquely misused in practice. Arthur Laffer sketched it on a napkin at a Washington restaurant in 1974 (Dick Cheney's napkin, to be precise). At ninety-five percent tax rates, cutting rates almost certainly boosts revenue. But both the Reagan and George W. Bush tax cuts produced large budget deficits, not revenue windfalls. Bruce Bartlett, a former Reagan and Bush administration official, lamented that supply-side economics morphed from a defensible insight into the “implausible notion that all tax cuts raise revenue.” Even Greg Mankiw, who chaired Bush's Council of Economic Advisers, asked on his blog: “If you think tax cuts increase revenue, why advocate spending restraint?”

Wheelan's framework for thinking about government distills to four categories: government that enhances the economy's productive capacity (essential), government that shrinks the pie but is socially desirable (redistribution), reasonable disagreements about the appropriate level of social spending (values), and government involvement that is purely destructive (special-interest rent-seeking). The Earned Income Tax Credit exemplifies smart policy design: unlike traditional welfare that paid people for not working, the EITC subsidizes low-wage workers through the tax system, making work pay.

Chapter 5: Economics of Information

McDonald's didn't create a better hamburger. Their genius is information. Every McDonald's tastes the same in Moscow, Mexico City, or Cincinnati. Driving through Nebraska at nine o'clock at night, you spot McDonald's and “Chuck's Big Burger.” You know exactly what McDonald's will deliver — clean, safe, inexpensive, working bathroom, predictable food. Chuck's might be amazing or might give you E. coli. You know all of this before you get out of your car in a state you've never visited. Branding solves the consumer's information problem for products whose quality can only be determined after purchase — or not at all.

George Akerlof, Michael Spence, and Joseph Stiglitz shared the 2001 Nobel Prize for their work on asymmetric information — problems that arise when one party to a transaction knows more than the other. Akerlof's famous 1970 paper “The Market for Lemons” showed that in the used-car market, sellers know more about quality than buyers. This creates adverse selection: owners of good used cars are less likely to sell (they know the true value), so the market fills with lemons. Buyers anticipate this and demand a discount, which drives out even more good cars. In theory, the market for high-quality used cars should barely function at all.

The adverse selection death spiral in health insurance follows the same logic. An insurer covers twenty thousand fifty-year-old men at $1,300 per year. The healthiest men know they're overpaying and drop out. The pool becomes sicker on average, so premiums rise. More healthy men leave. Premiums rise again. Eventually only the sickest remain and the market collapses. Group policies solve this by preventing individuals from opting out. Individual policies, however, attract precisely those who need insurance most — no matter how much the company charges. Genetic testing amplifies the problem enormously: a single strand of hair contains predispositions to heart disease, cancer, and Alzheimer's. The Economist, hardly a left-wing publication, concluded that genetic testing “may become the most potent argument for state-financed universal health care.”

Health care is the ultimate information problem because five forces collide simultaneously: patients who don't pay the bill demand maximum care, doctors maximize income by delivering maximum treatment, insurers maximize profits by paying for minimum treatment, technology introduces massively expensive options of uncertain value, and it's prohibitively costly for anyone to prove the “right” course of treatment. For prostate cancer, “watchful waiting” costs essentially nothing while proton radiation therapy — requiring a football-field-sized accelerator — costs about $100,000. A RAND Corporation analysis found “no therapy has been shown superior to another.”

Statistical discrimination is rational but troubling. A law firm interviewing two equally qualified Harvard Law graduates — one male, one female — may rationally hire the man because women still bear disproportionate child-rearing responsibility. The firm's logic makes economic sense even as it offends our values and violates federal law. An especially counterintuitive finding: employers who check criminal backgrounds are actually more likely to hire African-American workers. Without access to criminal records, employers with no racial prejudice but an aversion to hiring criminals will statistically discriminate against black male applicants, twenty-eight percent of whom have served time compared to four percent of white males.

The Hope Scholarship proposal showed how adverse selection can destroy an otherwise brilliant idea. Clinton proposed letting students borrow for college and repay as a percentage of post-graduation income rather than in fixed payments. Brain surgeons would pay back more; kindergarten teachers, less. The fatal flaw: future Wall Street bankers would avoid the program because a conventional loan would be cheaper. Future kindergarten teachers and Peace Corps volunteers would flock to it. The pool would consist only of low earners, making the program unable to recover its costs. Yale tested this approach and “quietly canceled after five years.”

Screening mechanisms allow parties to tease out private information by forcing people to sort themselves. Insurance deductibles are the classic example: healthy people choose high-deductible policies (lower premiums, fewer claims), while sick people choose low-deductible policies. College degrees serve a similar screening function. Krueger and Dale found that students admitted to both a highly selective school and a moderately selective one earned roughly the same salaries regardless of which they attended. The quality of the student matters more than the name on the diploma. Krueger's advice: “Don't believe that the only school worth attending is one that would not admit you.”

Firms signal quality through the trappings of success — marble lobbies, Impressionist paintings, Italian suits. These have no inherent relation to professional competence, but in a world of imperfect information, they serve the same function as a peacock's bright feathers. In parts of Asia, offices are kept absurdly cold to signal the firm can afford massive air conditioning. Sara Lee took this logic to its extreme in 1997 by deciding to sell off all manufacturing and attach its brand names to products made by others. The company's strategy for growth was to produce nothing.

Chapter 6: Productivity and Human Capital

Human capital is the sum of skills embodied in an individual — education, intelligence, creativity, work experience, entrepreneurial vigor, even the ability to throw a baseball fast. It's what you'd have left if someone stripped away all your assets and dropped you on a street corner with nothing but the clothes on your back. Bill Gates would be fine — companies would snap him up as a consultant or board member. Someone who dropped out in tenth grade with a meth addiction? Not so well. Even the sultan of Brunei might struggle, because his wealth comes from sitting on oil, not from personal skills.

Skill scarcity, not social value, determines compensation. Robert Solow, the Nobel laureate and avid baseball fan, was asked if it bothered him that Roger Clemens earned more for a single Red Sox season than Solow's entire Nobel Prize. “No,” he replied. “There are a lot of good economists, but there is only one Roger Clemens.” Productivity — what we can produce per hour of work — is the only sustainable source of rising living standards. The average American is five times as rich as in 1940, not because the day is any longer but because what we can accomplish in twenty-four hours has changed dramatically.

The most insightful way to think about poverty, in America or anywhere in the world, is as a dearth of human capital. People are poor because they can't find good jobs, but that's the symptom, not the illness. The underlying problem is lack of skills. The poverty rate for high school dropouts is twelve times that of college graduates. India is poor primarily because thirty-five percent of its population is illiterate. A Palestinian family Wheelan profiled insisted their son finish his engineering degree in three years, taking extra classes every quarter. The father, an accountant, explained that an accountant can practice nearly anywhere in the world — and that's where Palestinians might end up. After Saddam Hussein's 1990 retreat from Kuwait, Palestinians were expelled. The family's investment in portable human capital proved prescient.

Two thought experiments illuminate the power of human capital. Drop one hundred thousand high school dropouts on a busy Chicago intersection: social calamity, crime soars, government services are overwhelmed. Drop one hundred thousand graduates from top universities on the same corner: many find jobs immediately, some start businesses, firms relocate to exploit the talent glut, and economists call it a boon comparable to waves of immigration.

The skills premium has been widening for decades. Technology and globalization have increased the demand for highly educated workers while reducing opportunities for those without advanced skills. A college degree used to be a luxury; now it's closer to a necessity. The gap between college and high school earnings has more than doubled since the 1970s, and the trend shows no sign of reversing. Countries that invest in education and health — the East Asian “tigers” had few natural resources but invested heavily in human capital — see the biggest returns on their investment.

Education is not just about productivity; it's about signaling. Much of the value of a college degree comes not from what you learned in the classroom but from the fact that you proved capable of completing a demanding four-year program. Employers use the degree as a screening device, a shortcut for intelligence, discipline, and work ethic. This explains the persistent premium even for degrees in fields with no obvious connection to the job being performed.

Women's education may be the single best investment available in developing countries. Educated women have fewer children, invest more in each child's health and education, are more productive economically, and are less vulnerable to exploitation. Lawrence Summers, when he was chief economist at the World Bank, declared that educating girls yields a higher return than any other investment available in the developing world. The countries with the worst development outcomes are invariably those where women have the least education and fewest rights.

Creativity and entrepreneurship are forms of human capital that are especially hard to cultivate through formal education. Joseph Schumpeter's “creative destruction” requires not just skilled workers but people willing to take risks, challenge conventions, and build something new from the rubble of old industries. At the start of the twentieth century, half of Americans worked in farming; now about one in a hundred does. Two things have not happened: we haven't starved, and unemployment isn't forty-nine percent. As Wheelan puts it: “Imagine our collective loss of utility if Steve Jobs, Steven Spielberg, and Oprah Winfrey were corn farmers.”

Chapter 7: Financial Markets

Investing is a lot like dieting. The grapefruit and ice cream diet swept Wheelan's college campus — eat grapefruit and ice cream and watch the pounds melt away. It didn't work. His neighbor, a medical doctor and university professor, lost money in the tech crash and started studying chart patterns to predict stock movements. Wheelan's reaction: “Grapefruit and ice cream!” When it comes to personal finance, even intelligent people abandon good sense faster than you can say “miracle diet.” The rules for building wealth are strikingly simple but require discipline and short-term sacrifice.

Financial markets serve four essential functions. First, they raise capital, making investment possible. Esther Gelabuzi, a widow in Uganda with six children, used a tiny microloan of roughly $195 to set up a midwifery clinic without electricity; she has since delivered about fourteen hundred babies. Opportunity International made 325,000 micro-loans in twenty-four developing countries with a ninety-six percent repayment rate. Second, markets store and grow excess capital. The Sultan of Brunei's billions would lose purchasing power under a mattress ($1 billion in 1970 is worth about $180 million today in real terms). Third, markets insure against risk — from the mundane (health and auto insurance) to the exotic (catastrophe bonds paying twelve percent if no major hurricane strikes, the $260 million World Cup cancellation bonds that protected against a terrorism disruption). Fourth, markets enable speculation.

Credit default swaps — essentially insurance policies on whether a third party will repay its debt — went from a legitimate hedging tool to a weapon of mass financial destruction. Anyone could buy a CDS without owning the underlying debt, and for any single mortgage security, there might be hundreds or thousands of CDS contracts outstanding. When the housing bubble popped, losses were magnified thousands of times over. AIG's Financial Products division had earned $2.5 billion in pretax profits in 2005 by selling what turned out to be catastrophically underpriced insurance on complex, poorly understood securities. Simon Johnson, former IMF chief economist, described the strategy as “picking up nickels in front of a steamroller.” AIG accumulated over $400 billion in outstanding insurance obligations. The U.S. government committed roughly $180 billion to rescue it.

The efficient market hypothesis doesn't mean markets are always right; it means that consistent, predictable outperformance is nearly impossible because everyone is looking for the same opportunities. The analogy is a $500,000 brownstone in Lincoln Park listed at $250,000. Should you buy it? Three questions: Why would anyone sell a half- million-dollar house for half price? If it's such a sure thing, why hasn't your real estate agent bought it? And if it's been advertised, why hasn't a bidding war pushed the price back up? Picking stocks is like picking the shortest checkout line at the grocery store — everyone sees the same obvious clues (the guy with two full carts, the coupon lady) and acts on them. Even when you dodge the coupon lady, the person ahead of you forgot to weigh their avocados.

“Enron, Enron, Enron. Or Lehman, Lehman, Lehman.”— Charles Wheelan, on concentrated stock positions

Data backs up the theory: over any given year, slightly fewer than half of actively managed mutual funds beat the S&P 500. Over twenty years — the relevant time horizon for retirement savers — only forty-five percent beat the index. $10,000 invested in the average active fund in 1973 grew to $355,091; the same amount in an S&P 500 index fund grew to $364,066. Warren Buffett and even Richard Thaler (the behavioral economist who created a fund to exploit human irrationality) put most of their personal retirement savings in index funds.

Wheelan distills investing into four rules that pass the economic “sniff test.” First: save, invest, repeat. Compound interest is “the greatest invention of all time.” The cost of living better in the present is living less well in the future. Second: take risk, earn reward. Riskier investments must offer higher expected returns to attract capital. If a government bond pays six percent, your neighbor Lance's sunflower combustion engine had better offer something significantly higher. Third: diversify. Spreading across many independent investments preserves expected return while dramatically reducing risk. But assets must be truly independent — bundling thousands of mortgages was supposed to be like flipping a hundred independent coins, but when the housing bubble popped, all the coins came up tails simultaneously. Fourth: invest for the long run. A casino always wins over time; so does a diversified portfolio with a positive expected return. Day trading captures all the costs of trading with none of the benefits of patience.

Behavioral finance challenges the efficient market view. Herd behavior, overconfidence, and the tendency to see patterns in randomness can produce bubbles and busts that rational models can't explain. A Carnegie Mellon/Stanford/Iowa study found that brain-damaged patients with impaired emotional responses but intact logic made thirteen percent more money in investment games than healthy controls — because they took more calculated risks and were less emotional about losses. Robert Shiller of Yale argued in 2000 that stocks were overvalued in Irrational Exuberance; five years later he correctly called the housing bubble. Yet even critics of efficient markets agree that average investors shouldn't try to beat the market. Andrew Lo and Craig MacKinlay, who wrote A Non-Random Walk Down Wall Street, still advise ordinary investors to buy index funds.

When an adviser promises twenty to forty percent returns, one of three things must be true: the investment is very risky, your adviser has discovered something all the world's sophisticated investors missed, or your adviser is incompetent or dishonest. “All too often the answer is number three.” Think Bernie Madoff.

Chapter 8: The Power of Organized Interests

For nearly forty years, the federal budget included a cash payment to American mohair farmers. Mohair comes from the Angora goat and is a wool substitute. The subsidy was created in 1955 at the behest of the armed forces to ensure a sufficient supply of yarn for military uniforms in the event of a war. The military switched to synthetic fibers around 1960. The government continued paying mohair farmers for another thirty-five years. The subsidy was eventually eliminated when it became the poster child for pork-barrel absurdity. And then, when the rest of us turned our attention elsewhere, it came back — the 2008 farm bill includes subsidies for wool and mohair producers through 2012.

Gary Becker formalized what economists call the economics of regulation: when it comes to interest group politics, it pays to be small. Small, well-organized groups are the most successful in the political process because the costs of whatever favors they extract are spread over a large, unorganized segment of the population. One thousand mohair farmers each receiving a $100,000 check care intensely about the subsidy — probably more than they care about any other policy issue. Three hundred million taxpayers each paying mere pennies don't care at all.

The ethanol subsidy is the mohair of the modern era. Gasoline blended with ethanol is taxed 5.4 cents less per gallon. A 1997 Government Accounting Office study found ethanol had little effect on the environment or on foreign oil dependence, cost the Treasury $7.1 billion in forgone tax revenue, and may actually worsen some kinds of air pollution. But ethanol is crucial to Iowa corn farmers, and Iowa is crucial to presidential races. Every mainstream presidential candidate except John McCain has supported the ethanol subsidy. Bill Bradley opposed it as a New Jersey senator, but when he arrived in Iowa as a presidential candidate, he “suddenly found it in his heart” to support tax breaks for ethanol. As the New York Times noted: “Regardless of whether ethanol is a great fuel for cars, it certainly works wonders in Iowa campaigns.”

A striking pattern emerges across countries: where farmers are a small minority (America, Europe), governments provide large agricultural subsidies. Where farmers are a large portion of the population (China, India), farmers are forced to sell crops below market price so urban dwellers get cheap food. In both cases, the large group subsidizes the small group — perfectly consistent with Becker's theory.

George Stigler's regulatory capture theory demonstrates that firms and industries often benefit from the very regulations they publicly oppose. They use the political process to generate rules that help them or hobble competitors. Teacher certification is a prime example: unions consistently support more rigorous requirements for new teachers while exempting current ones. Albert Einstein, when he arrived in Princeton, was not legally qualified to teach high school physics. A Hamilton Project study of 150,000 students over three years found that good teachers matter enormously (top quarter produced ten percentile points more than bottom quarter), but certification has zero correlation with classroom performance.

Joseph Schumpeter's creative destruction is good for society but bad for those in the path of progress. The individuals standing in capitalism's way will use every tool available, including politics, to protect themselves. Roger Ferguson Jr., former vice chairman of the Federal Reserve, warned that “policymakers who fail to appreciate the relationship between the relentless churning of the competitive environment and wealth creation will end up focusing their efforts on methods and skills that are in decline.” It's one thing to retrain displaced Pony Express workers as telegraph operators; it's quite another to ban the telegraph.

Everyone despises earmarks — except their own. At a 2009 candidates' forum in Illinois, every candidate excoriated pork-barrel spending until the moderator asked whether they'd oppose an earmark for Children's Memorial Hospital, located three hundred yards away. Every candidate backpedaled. A member of Congress who secures funding for the hospital gets a ribbon-cutting ceremony with cupcakes and speeches. How did the funding pass? Not by inspiring oratory, but by supporting a bill with nine thousand earmarks, one of which was his.

Bob Kerrey, former Democratic senator from Nebraska, captured the deeper problem: “The most important corruption that happens in politics doesn't go away even if you had full public financing of campaigns. And that is: I don't want to tell you something that's going to make you not like me.” Campaign finance reform doesn't change the fact that two percent who care deeply about an issue are a more potent political force than ninety-eight percent who feel mildly opposed but aren't motivated enough to do anything about it.

Chapter 9: Keeping Score

In the late 1980s, Wheelan was a young speechwriter for the governor of Maine. One of his primary responsibilities was finding jokes — “Funny jokes. Belly laughs, not chuckles.” One joke captures the economic anxieties of that era perfectly. George H.W. Bush is hit on the head with a horseshoe at Kennebunkport and slips into a coma. Nine months later he awakens with President Quayle at his bedside. “Are we at peace?” “Yes.” “Unemployment rate?” “About four percent.” “Inflation?” “Under control.” “Amazing! How much does a loaf of bread cost?” Quayle scratches his head: “About 240 yen.” The joke captured widespread fear of Japanese economic domination — which never materialized. Japan suffered more than a decade of stagnation while the United States entered its longest expansion in history.

GDP — gross domestic product, the total value of all goods and services produced in an economy — is the first number any president recovering from a horseshoe-induced coma would ask for. What matters is real GDP (adjusted for inflation, not nominal) and GDP per capita (divided by population). India's GDP is $3.3 trillion versus Israel's $201 billion, but Israel is far richer: GDP per capita of $28,300 compared to India's $2,900.

The Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas devised a brilliant way to express economic progress: measure how long the average worker must work to buy basic goods. In 1900, a pair of stockings cost one hour and forty-one minutes of labor. By 2000: eighteen minutes. A three-pound chicken took two hours and thirty-seven minutes of work in 1919; by 2009, just thirteen minutes. A cell phone “cost” 456 hours of work in the mid-1980s; today, about nine hours — a ninety-eight percent drop. “Making money takes time, so when we shop, we're really spending time. The real cost of living isn't measured in dollars and cents but in the hours and minutes we must work to live.”

What India's per capita GDP of $2,900 means in human terms: the country has more than one hundred thousand cases of Hansen's disease (leprosy). It is easily cured — one $3 dose of antibiotic for a mild case, a $20 regimen for severe cases. The World Health Organization provides the drugs free. But India's health care infrastructure isn't good enough to identify and reach the afflicted. More than one hundred thousand people are horribly disfigured by a disease that costs three dollars to cure.

GDP has significant limitations. It doesn't count unpaid work — cooking, childcare, and housework contribute nothing, but outsourcing them to restaurants, daycare, and cleaning services does. It ignores environmental degradation: clear-cutting a forest adds to GDP while the lost forest doesn't subtract. China's State Environmental Protection Administration calculated “Green GDP” figures and found that 2004's headline ten percent growth was really about seven percent after subtracting $64 billion in pollution costs. Sixteen of the world's twenty-five most polluted cities are in China. GDP ignores income distribution — a country where a tiny elite grows fabulously rich while most citizens get poorer can still show impressive per capita growth. And GDP has no correlation with happiness: from 1970 to 1999, Americans reporting themselves as “very happy” fell from thirty-six to twenty-nine percent even as real incomes rose substantially.

Recessions stem from economic shocks — stock or property bubble collapses, oil price spikes, deliberate Fed tightening, commodity crashes in developing countries. The most fascinating thing about recessions is how they spread. If we all believe the economy will get worse, then it will get worse, because our rational individual response (spend less, save more) collectively makes the situation worse. FDR's declaration that we have “nothing to fear but fear itself” was excellent economics. Giuliani urging New Yorkers to go holiday shopping after 9/11 wasn't wacky — spending generates confidence that causes recovery.

The 2008 Great Recession was particularly devastating because it attacked household wealth directly. Christina Romer, chair of Obama's Council of Economic Advisers, estimated that U.S. household wealth fell seventeen percent between December 2007 and December 2008 — five times the magnitude of the decline in 1929. The Lehman Brothers collapse triggered a cascade panic. A Treasury official described the aftermath to The New Yorker: Tim Geithner received a call from a “titan of the financial system” who said he was doing fine, “but his voice was quavering.” Geithner immediately called back: “Don't call anyone else. If anyone hears your voice, you'll scare the shit out of them.”

Government has two tools for fighting recessions: fiscal policy (spending and tax cuts) and monetary policy (Fed interest rate adjustments). Fiscal policy has an appealing Keynesian logic but a devastating practical flaw: it is made in Congress, not in theory. In most postwar recessions, Congress did not pass response legislation until after the downturn had ended. In May 1977, Congress was still passing recovery legislation for the recession that ended in March 1975. Monetary policy works faster but has limits — in 2007, the Fed pushed rates to near zero but consumers and businesses still wouldn't borrow. Keynes called this “pushing on a wet noodle.”

Key economic indicators beyond GDP include: Okun's Law (GDP growth of three percent keeps unemployment stable; each point above or below moves unemployment by half a point), the Gini coefficient measuring income inequality (US at 45, France at 28, Brazil at 57), government spending as a share of GDP (US at roughly thirty percent — low by developed-world standards; France and Sweden exceed fifty percent), and the current account deficit (not inherently bad — early America ran deficits to build industrial capacity — but dangerous when imports aren't funding productive investment).

Chapter 10: The Federal Reserve

Hours after the September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks, the Federal Reserve issued a two-sentence statement: “The Federal Reserve System is open and operating. The discount window is available to meet liquidity needs.” Those terse, technical words calmed global markets. The following Monday, the Fed cut interest rates by half a percentage point. No other institution in the world, public or private, has tools with such direct impact on the global economy. Paul Krugman put it bluntly: “If you want a simple model for predicting the unemployment rate in the United States, here it is: It will be what Greenspan wants it to be, plus or minus a random error reflecting the fact that he is not quite God.”

The Fed's mandate is to facilitate a sustainable pace of growth, but the job is fiendishly difficult. The economy has a speed limit of roughly three percent GDP growth per year. Push faster and excess demand generates inflation. In the late 1990s, autoworkers threatened to strike over forced overtime and fast-food restaurants offered signing bonuses. The wall had been hit. William McChesney Martin Jr., Fed chairman in the 1950s and 1960s, offered the definitive job description: “The Fed's job is to take away the punch bowl just as the party gets going.”

Sometimes the Fed has to intervene after the party has gone wildly out of control. Paul Volcker ended the inflationary party of the 1970s by hitting the monetary brakes hard. Inflation had climbed from three percent in 1972 to 13.5 percent in 1980. Volcker cranked interest rates above sixteen percent. The result was the severe recession of 1981-82: GDP shrank three percent, unemployment climbed to nearly ten percent. But by 1983, inflation was back to three percent. “Obviously it would have been easier and less painful if the party had never gone out of control in the first place.”

The Fed controls interest rates through two main tools. The discount rate is what banks pay to borrow directly from the Fed (carrying a stigma — like borrowing from your parents at twenty-five). More importantly, the federal funds rate is what banks charge each other for overnight loans. The FOMC sets a target and then uses open market operations to achieve it — buying government bonds from banks with newly created money. The bank swaps bonds (inert assets) for cash (lendable funds), and through fractional reserve banking, a $100 million injection can multiply to $1 billion as each bank lends most of what it receives and recipients deposit at other banks.

Money is distinct from wealth. Wealth consists of real things — houses, cars, human capital. Money is merely a medium of exchange. In theory, a simple economy could function through barter. In practice, the logistical challenges of using chickens to buy books on Amazon make a medium of exchange essential. The word “salary” comes from Roman soldiers paid in sacks of salt. In U.S. federal prisons, when cash was banned, cigarettes became the currency. When smoking was banned, cans of mackerel (“the mack”) became the standard unit of exchange. A haircut costs two macks at the Lompoc Federal Correctional Complex.

Since 1971, when the United States went off the gold standard, dollars have been redeemable for nothing. They are pieces of paper whose value derives solely from collective confidence that they can be exchanged for real goods in the future. In India, shopkeepers, taxi drivers, and merchants refuse torn or soiled rupee notes — even though the central bank considers them legal tender. Rational people refuse them because they believe others will refuse them. “Our faith in paper currency is predicated on the faith that others place in the same paper.”

Hyperinflation destroys this confidence catastrophically. A German newspaper cost a third of a mark in 1921; two years later, seventy million marks. The mark became so worthless that households burned banknotes as fuel. In Latin America during the 1980s, some countries' largest import was paper currency. Even moderate inflation is destructive — it erodes savings, makes “safe” investments lose purchasing power, punishes retirees on fixed incomes, and creates misleading comparisons (Hollywood proclaiming box-office “records” without adjusting for inflation). Governments sometimes deliberately create inflation to erase their debts — a hidden tax on everyone who holds the currency.

Deflation is even worse. Japan's two-decade experience showed the death spiral: falling prices cause consumers to postpone purchases, firms cut prices further, the economy contracts, and falling prices feed on themselves. The Fed's catastrophic mistake during the Great Depression was allowing the money supply to fall twenty-eight percent as bank failures cascaded — and actually raising interest rates in 1931 to defend the gold standard. Ben Bernanke, a Depression scholar who had once advocated that Japan “drop bank notes out of helicopters,” used every conventional tool and invented new ones: cutting rates seven times, lending directly to investment banks for the first time in history, accepting mortgage-backed securities as collateral, and creating $500 to loan to anyone who needed it under Article 13(3) of the Federal Reserve Act. Christina Romer called it “a high point in central bank history.”

Chapter 11: International Economics

In 1992, George Soros made nearly $1 billion in a single day by betting against the British pound. Britain was part of the European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM), which pegged the pound to 2.95 German marks. But Britain was in a deep recession, and the pound was falling as international investors fled for more profitable opportunities elsewhere. The British government had two tools to defend the peg: spend foreign currency reserves buying pounds, or raise interest rates to attract capital. Both were unattractive — reserves were running low, and raising rates during a recession was “bad economics and even worse politics.” Soros called the government's bluff, betting heavily that Britain would abandon the peg. On “Black Wednesday,” September 16, 1992, Britain withdrew from the ERM and the pound immediately lost more than ten percent of its value.

Purchasing power parity (PPP) suggests that exchange rates should reflect what the same basket of goods costs in different countries. If everyday goods cost $25 in the United States and 750 rubles in Russia, one dollar should be worth about 30 rubles. But official exchange rates deviate wildly from PPP because roughly three-quarters of goods and services are nontradable: you can arbitrage TV prices across borders, but you can't arbitrage haircuts, babysitting, or restaurant meals. The Economist's Big Mac Index uses the McDonald's hamburger as a rough PPP gauge. In 2009, a Big Mac cost $3.57 in the US and 12.5 renminbi in China, implying the renminbi should be worth about 3.5 per dollar. The official rate was 6.83 — making the renminbi massively undervalued. China deliberately keeps its currency cheap to boost exports.

A weak currency is good for exporters (a Ford Taurus becomes cheaper for Parisians) and bad for consumers (an imported Toyota costs more). A strong currency is the opposite. Neither is inherently good or bad. In 1992, when the dollar was weak, the New York Times declared that “the declining dollar has turned the world's wealthiest economy into the Filene's Basement of industrial countries.” A government that deliberately undervalues its currency is essentially taxing consumers of imports and subsidizing export producers.

Exchange rates are driven by economic growth (which attracts capital), export demand, interest rate differentials, and safe-haven effects. During the 2008 crisis, the U.S. dollar actually strengthened despite America being ground zero for the global meltdown — because investors worldwide “fled to safety” in U.S. treasuries. Government attempts to intervene in currency markets are like trying to “warm up a cold bathtub with one spoonful of hot water” — foreign exchange markets trade trillions of dollars daily.

The euro's fundamental problem is that countries sharing a currency surrender independent monetary policy. Greece can't devalue to make its exports cheaper; Ireland couldn't raise rates to cool its overheating Celtic Tiger economy. The United States works as a common currency area because labor moves freely between states, federal fiscal transfers cushion regional shocks, and cultural unity supports adjustment. Europe has none of these advantages.

Iceland's spectacular collapse illustrates what happens when a tiny economy takes on outsized financial risk. Iceland (population 300,000) had GDP per capita among the world's highest. Its three biggest banks held combined assets ten times the nation's GDP. When the financial crisis hit, all three collapsed simultaneously. The government couldn't bail them out — the banks were simply too big. McDonald's pulled out of the country because importing food ingredients became too expensive. The IMF stepped in with a rescue package.

Capital flight creates self-reinforcing crises. When investors lose confidence, they sell the local currency and flee, driving it down further, making imports more expensive, stoking inflation, and causing more investors to flee. The Asian financial crisis of 1997 devastated Thailand, Indonesia, and South Korea in exactly this pattern. The IMF serves as lender of last resort for countries, much as the Fed does for banks — providing emergency loans with conditions (austerity, reforms) that are controversial because they can deepen short-term pain.

The national savings rate tells a worrying story. American personal savings fell from over nine percent in the 1960s to roughly zero by the end of the 1990s. Savings finance investment, and investment drives productivity. When Americans don't save, we must borrow from abroad — and any country dependent on foreign capital must worry that when times get tough, international investors will panic and flee. Social Security is a pay-as-you-go pyramid scheme: current workers pay for current retirees. In 1960 there were five workers per retiree; now three; by 2032, only two. The fix is economically simple but politically explosive: raise the payroll tax, raise the retirement age, cut benefits, or boost worker productivity. “If you think any of these solutions would be politically palatable, please go back and read Chapter 8 again.”

Chapter 12: Trade and Globalization

Comparative advantage is the most important concept in international trade. Even if one country is better at making everything, both countries benefit from trade if each specializes in what it does relatively best. Wheelan could be a better typist and a better writer than his assistant, but it still makes sense for the assistant to type because Wheelan's time spent typing is time not spent writing — which is far more valuable. The same logic applies to nations. Trade creates losers, but the gains always exceed the losses. The correct response is not to block trade but to compensate those who are displaced through retraining, transition aid, and education.

The sweatshop debate is where economic reasoning most sharply diverges from popular morality. Nicholas Kristof and Sheryl WuDunn reported from Asia that the worst thing that can happen to sweatshop workers is for their factory to close. In Phnom Penh, they found children scavenging in garbage dumps for recyclable materials — dangerous, dirty, and sometimes fatal work. One Cambodian teenager said her dream was to get a job in a garment factory: “At least that way, I wouldn't have to work in the dump.” Life magazine reported on Pakistani children sewing soccer balls, sparking outrage and boycotts. The children were fired from the factory — and many ended up in worse situations.

Sweatshops are symptoms of poverty, not its cause. Nike pays Vietnamese workers roughly $600 a year — a pathetic amount that also happens to be twice the average Vietnamese worker's annual income. South Korea, Taiwan, and Japan all went through sweatshop phases on their way to prosperity. The relevant question is never “are sweatshops good?” but “compared to what?”

Trade protectionism hurts the poor most. Tariffs and quotas raise the price of imported goods. Since the poor spend a larger share of their income on basic necessities (clothing, food), protectionism functions as a regressive tax. U.S. sugar quotas keep American sugar prices at roughly double the world level, benefiting a small number of producers at the expense of every consumer and every business that uses sugar. Agricultural subsidies in the US and Europe depress world prices for cotton and other commodities, devastating farmers in West Africa who could otherwise compete. Oxfam estimated that U.S. cotton subsidies cost West African farmers more than America's entire aid budget to Africa.

The “race to the bottom” argument — that globalization forces countries to lower environmental and labor standards to attract business — is not supported by evidence. Countries with strong institutions and good governance attract more investment, not less. Trade makes countries richer, and richer countries invest more in environmental protection. The Environmental Kuznets Curve shows that early industrialization increases pollution, but continued growth eventually leads to cleaner practices. Blocking trade keeps countries poor, which is worse for the environment in the long run.

Institutions like the World Trade Organization and “fast-track” trade authority exist specifically to help the greater good prevail over narrow interests. Fast-track authority means Congress can only vote up or down on a trade agreement — no amendments allowed. The logic: if legislators can exempt their favored industries, every district will carve out an exception and the agreement becomes meaningless. The WTO works similarly at the international level, defining what countries must do to join (open markets, eliminate subsidies, phase out tariffs) and giving politicians cover to say no to their domestic special interests.

Outsourcing of services represents the next frontier of trade. Indian radiologists can read X-rays, Indian accountants can do tax returns, and Indian programmers can write software — all delivered via the internet. This threatens a category of American workers who previously thought their jobs were safe from foreign competition. The economic logic is identical to manufacturing trade: it makes both countries richer on average while creating concentrated losers who deserve transition support.

The brain drain from poor to rich countries deprives developing nations of the human capital they need most. Some economists argue that the possibility of emigration actually incentivizes education — people invest in skills hoping to leave, but many don't actually emigrate, so the country ends up with more educated people overall. The evidence is mixed. What is clear is that trade restrictions, not trade itself, are the bigger threat to developing-world prosperity.

Chapter 13: Development Economics

The wealth gap between nations is staggering and growing. The richest one percent of the world's population earns as much as the bottom fifty-seven percent. In 1820, the ratio of per capita income between the richest and poorest countries was about three to one; by 1992, it was seventy-two to one. The question of why some nations are rich and others poor is the most important question in economics.

Institutions matter more than natural resources. Botswana (diamonds, good governance) became one of Africa's success stories. Sierra Leone (diamonds, corruption and civil war) became one of its worst tragedies. Resource-rich countries often suffer the resource curse: natural wealth creates incentives for corruption, civil conflict, and neglect of other economic sectors. Hernando de Soto's research showed that the world's poor hold trillions in assets (homes, businesses) they can't leverage because they lack legal title. Without clear property rights, you can't use your house as collateral for a loan. In Egypt, de Soto found that assets held by the poor without legal title were worth fifty-five times all foreign investment ever recorded in the country.

Geography matters but isn't destiny. Tropical countries face genuine disadvantages — disease burden, poor soil quality, climate extremes. Jeffrey Sachs argues that massive foreign aid is needed to overcome these “poverty traps.” William Easterly counters that $2.3 trillion in aid over fifty years has produced disappointing results because it doesn't address root causes. Dambisa Moyo argues in Dead Aid that aid to Africa has been “an unmitigated political, economic, and humanitarian disaster.” The truth probably lies in between: targeted aid (vaccines, mosquito nets, clean water) works; large untargeted transfers to corrupt governments often don't.

Corruption is the single biggest obstacle to development. Mobutu Sese Seko reportedly stole $5 billion during his rule of Zaire while his country became one of the world's poorest. The correlation between corruption and poverty is overwhelming. Kleptocrats steal foreign aid. Dictators prevent the creative destruction that generates growth because it threatens their power. Development requires functioning institutions, which are exactly what poor countries lack.

Countries that are open to trade grow faster. China's explosive growth began after Deng Xiaoping opened the economy in the late 1970s. India accelerated after liberalizing reforms in 1991. North Korea (completely closed) and Myanmar (largely closed) have stagnated. But openness alone isn't sufficient — Russia opened rapidly but lacked the institutional framework to benefit. The East Asian tigers (South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong) had few natural resources but invested heavily in human capital. South Korea went from poorer than Ghana in 1960 to a wealthy OECD nation.

Women's education is the single most effective investment in development. Educated women have fewer children, invest more in each child's health and education, are more productive economically, and are less vulnerable to exploitation. The countries with the worst development outcomes are invariably those where women have the least access to education. Every dollar spent on girls' schooling yields returns that cascade through generations.

The East Asian tigers offer the clearest proof that human capital trumps natural resources. South Korea was poorer than Ghana in 1960. It had no oil, no diamonds, no significant mineral wealth. What it did have was a government committed to investing in education and building institutions that rewarded productive activity rather than rent-seeking. Within a generation, South Korea joined the OECD as a wealthy nation. Botswana shows the same pattern in Africa: diamond wealth was invested in schools and infrastructure rather than stolen by elites, making it one of the continent's rare success stories.

The foreign aid debate often misses the deeper point: the “Kill Bill” problem of development. Governments in poor countries frequently have strong incentives to pursue policies that keep them in power rather than policies that promote growth. Development requires not just money but functioning institutions — and building institutions is the one thing that no amount of external aid has reliably achieved.

Epilogue: Life in 2050

Wheelan closes with seven unresolved questions that will shape the next forty years. Will we address climate change — the ultimate externality where costs are global and deferred? Will the global economy become more or less integrated despite the powerful political backlash against globalization? Will income inequality continue widening as the human capital premium rises? Will the fiscal time bomb of national debt, Social Security, and Medicare detonate? Will the developing world catch up, or will sub-Saharan Africa continue falling behind while China and India surge? Will we confront the paradoxical problems of our own success — obesity, congestion, environmental degradation? And will we be happier, given that decades of economic growth haven't clearly improved our psychological well-being?

Economics doesn't provide definitive answers to these questions, but it provides the framework for thinking about them clearly. The most dangerous responses to economic challenges come from those who ignore economic principles entirely — whether they're French politicians mandating thirty-five-hour workweeks, American politicians promising that tax cuts pay for themselves, or developing-world dictators destroying their economies to maintain power.

Stripped of jargon, equations, and graphs, the great ideas are intuitive. That is naked economics — and, as Wheelan demonstrates across thirteen lively chapters, it can even be fun.

Trading & Investing Lessons

Incentive Analysis Before Every Trade

Wheelan's entire Chapter 2 is a masterclass in asking “who benefits and who bears the risk?” before any transaction. Every trade has a counterparty. When a broker recommends a stock or a fund manager launches a new product, the first question should be: how are they compensated? The 2008 crisis happened because mortgage brokers, investment bankers, and rating agencies all profited from transactions whose risks they didn't bear. Apply this lens to any financial product being sold to you.

Efficient Markets and the Index Fund Imperative

Wheelan's grocery checkout line analogy is the best explanation of why stock-picking is so hard: even when you dodge the obvious obstacles (coupon lady, full carts), unpredictable events determine who gets out fastest. Over twenty years, fifty-five percent of active funds underperform a simple index. Warren Buffett and Richard Thaler both put their personal savings in index funds. The trading lesson: don't confuse skill with luck in a random environment.

Price Signals as Market Intelligence

Prices are “giant neon billboards” broadcasting information about supply, demand, and expectations. Wheelan's description of how tuna prices coordinate an entire supply chain without central direction applies directly to reading price action: prices aggregate the information and expectations of all participants. The signal is in the price, not in the narrative.

Behavioral Biases Are Trading Hazards

The hot hand fallacy, the tendency to see patterns in randomness, overconfidence after wins, and the inability to take small losses are all documented in the book. Brain-damaged patients made better investment decisions because they were less emotional. The trading lesson: systematic rules outperform discretionary decisions precisely because they bypass the cognitive biases Wheelan catalogs.

Diversification Isn't Just About Numbers

Bundling thousands of mortgages was supposed to be like flipping a hundred independent coins — but when the housing bubble popped, all the coins came up tails. True diversification requires genuinely independent assets. A portfolio of ten tech stocks is not diversified. Correlation spikes during crises, which is exactly when you need diversification most.

Currency as a Trading Factor

Wheelan's Chapter 11 on exchange rates shows that currency movements can overwhelm the returns on foreign investments. Toyota's operating earnings swing $450 million for every one-yen change against the dollar. For traders holding international positions, currency risk is not a secondary consideration — it can be the dominant factor.

Key Takeaways

Self-Interest Drives Prosperity

The market aligns private incentives with social welfare. Coca-Cola at the Berlin Wall, Bill Gates leaving Harvard, fishermen switching from salmon to tuna — self-interest, channeled through markets, produces outcomes that no central planner could orchestrate.

Incentives Are Everything

Every institution, regulation, and policy creates incentives. The question is never “will people respond to incentives?” but “what incentives have we accidentally created?” Mexico City's driving ban increased pollution. London's congestion charge reduced it. Same goal, opposite incentive design, opposite results.

Markets Are Amoral

Markets reward scarcity, not social value. This is not a bug — it's the mechanism by which resources flow to their most productive use. But it means markets will never ensure that the poor have health care or that endangered species survive without deliberate intervention.

Information Asymmetry Is Pervasive

Adverse selection, moral hazard, signaling, and screening pervade every market from used cars to health insurance to college admissions. Many apparently puzzling institutions (brands, deductibles, college degrees, occupational licenses) exist to manage information problems.

Human Capital Is the Ultimate Resource

Countries with oil but bad institutions (Nigeria) are poorer than countries with no resources but good institutions and educated populations (Singapore). Investing in human capital — especially women's education — yields the highest returns of any development strategy.

Government Is Essential but Dangerous

Without government, markets cannot exist (no property rights, no contract enforcement, no currency). But government power attracts rent-seekers, and the political process systematically favors small, organized groups over the diffuse public interest.

Trade Makes Countries Richer

Comparative advantage means specialization benefits everyone. The gains are diffuse (slightly cheaper goods for millions) while the losses are concentrated (shuttered factories in specific towns). This asymmetry makes protectionism politically popular despite being economically destructive.

Monetary Policy Matters Enormously

The wrong monetary policy can cause depressions, hyperinflation, or world wars. The right monetary policy can cushion shocks and facilitate growth. Central bank independence is crucial because politicians have strong incentives to pursue short-term monetary expansion regardless of long-term consequences.

Contrarian Ideas

Sweatshops Are a Step Up

The problem with Asian sweatshops is not that there are too many but that there are not enough. Workers take these jobs voluntarily because they're the best available option. Closing sweatshops doesn't create better alternatives — it eliminates the best option poor workers have.

Environmental Protection Is a Luxury Good

Wealthy nations spend more on the environment because they can afford to. Imposing rich-country environmental standards on developing nations whose people are starving is bad economics. If your children were at risk of dying from malaria, you wouldn't care how many butterflies you killed.

Smokers Subsidize Nonsmokers

Smokers die seven years earlier on average, meaning nonsmokers collect more Social Security and pension benefits relative to what they paid in. Philip Morris commissioned a report showing the net fiscal benefit of smoking to the Czech government was $148 million.

Mandating Maternity Leave May Hurt Women

Generous maternity mandates give employers an incentive to discriminate against young women in hiring. The well-intentioned policy may reduce the very opportunities it was designed to protect.

The French 35-Hour Week Destroyed Jobs

Based on the lump of labor fallacy — the belief that there's a fixed amount of work to go around. The American economy creates millions of new jobs without government rationing of work hours.

Requiring Car Seats on Planes May Kill Children

Forcing families to buy an extra airline seat pushes some to drive instead. Since driving is dramatically more dangerous than flying, the mandate could result in more child deaths, not fewer.

Proposals & Frameworks

Four Rules of Investing

(1) Save, invest, repeat — compound interest is everything. (2) Risk equals reward — don't accept equity-level risk for bond-level returns. (3) Diversify across truly independent assets. (4) Invest for the long run — patience is the only reliable edge.

Wheelan's Four-Category Government Framework

(1) Government that enhances productive capacity (essential). (2) Government that shrinks the pie but is socially desirable (redistribution). (3) Reasonable disagreements about social spending levels (values). (4) Government involvement that is purely destructive (rent-seeking).

Externality Solutions Toolkit

Regulate (ban or restrict), tax (raise private cost to match social cost), assign property rights (Coase theorem), or nudge (change defaults). Economists generally prefer taxation over outright bans because taxes raise revenue, preserve choice, and let the market allocate efficiently.

Libertarian Paternalism / Nudge Theory

Make the default option the one that's best for most people. Auto-enroll in retirement plans. Opt-out organ donation. The nudge preserves freedom of choice while steering toward better outcomes.

What Didn't Fit

The Iowa Farmer's Tractor

While reporting for The Economist in Iowa, Wheelan met a corn, soybean, and cattle farmer who pointed to an old tractor parked outside his barn. “That tractor cost $7,500 new in 1970,” he said, then pointed angrily at a shiny new one: “Cost me $40,000. Can you explain that?” The answer was inflation — the new tractor wasn't really more expensive in real terms because the prices of the farmer's crops had risen proportionally. But the farmer, wearing overalls and looking at a city boy in a tie driving a Honda Civic, was not impressed by the explanation. The following year, when Wheelan was sent to write about Kentucky tobacco farmers, he had the good sense to rent a pickup truck.

The Particle Accelerator Boondoggle

Northern Illinois had the existing infrastructure and a major federal laboratory. Texas won the contract for a high-speed particle accelerator because George H.W. Bush was president. The government spent roughly $1 billion on the Texas site and then abandoned the entire project. A Wall Street Journal headline captured the dynamic: “Industries That Backed Bush Are Now Seeking Return on Investment.” Jamie Dimon of JPMorgan Chase called government relations his company's “seventh line of business.”

India's Court System

Abdul Waheed filed a lawsuit against his neighbor Mohammad Nanhe over a drainage dispute. The case came to trial in June 2000 in Moradabad, near New Delhi — thirty-nine years after it was filed. Both parties were dead; their relatives inherited the case. By one calculation, if no new cases were filed in India, it would take 324 years to clear the existing backlog. A seventy-five-year-old man was released from a Calcutta jail after waiting thirty-seven years to be tried for murder — all witnesses and the investigating officer were dead. And by developing-world standards, India has relatively good government institutions.

The Lompoc Mackerel Economy

When the U.S. Bureau of Prisons banned smoking, inmates needed a new currency. They settled on cans of mackerel — “the mack” — which are portable, durable, don't spoil, can be purchased at the commissary for about a dollar each, and serve as a convenient unit of account. A haircut costs two macks. Some prisons have since switched to plastic pouches of mackerel because the cans can be fashioned into weapons. The mackerel economy demonstrates that money will emerge spontaneously in any community that needs to facilitate exchange.

The Woman Who Didn't Want Socialized Medicine

At a health care town hall during the Obama administration, a woman delivered an impassioned speech: “I don't want government-run health care. I don't want socialized medicine. And don't touch my Medicare.” Medicare is, of course, government-run health care — Americans over sixty-five see private doctors and get reimbursed by the federal government. The anecdote perfectly captures the disconnect between Americans' abstract hostility toward government programs and their concrete dependence on them.

About the Author

Charles Wheelan is a senior lecturer and policy fellow at Dartmouth College and a former correspondent for The Economist. He holds a PhD in public policy from the University of Chicago's Harris School, where he studied under some of the most prominent economists of the twentieth century, including Gary Becker, Robert Willis, and Sam Peltzman. He also studied with Paul Volcker and Kenneth Rogoff.

Wheelan describes himself as a “translator ” of centuries of great economic thinking rather than an original researcher. His gift is taking complex ideas and making them accessible through vivid real-world examples and a conversational writing style that is unusually engaging for the subject matter. He is also the author of Naked Statistics and Naked Money, continuing the franchise of making academic disciplines accessible to general readers.

In 2009, Wheelan put his policy knowledge to the test by running for Congress in a special election for Illinois's Fifth Congressional District, competing against more than twenty other candidates. He lost, but the experience gave him firsthand appreciation for the political dynamics he dissects in Chapter 8. For the record, he opposed the ethanol subsidy — a relatively meaningless position given that his entirely urban district doesn't contain a single farmer.

Why This Book Matters

Naked Economics matters because economic illiteracy has real consequences. The French thirty-five-hour workweek, Ross Perot's “giant sucking sound,” Mexico City's pollution program, and the cascade of misaligned incentives that produced the 2008 financial crisis all stem from a fundamental failure to think through how people respond to rules, prices, and institutions. This book arms general readers with the conceptual toolkit to evaluate political claims, understand financial decisions, and see through policies that sound compassionate but produce perverse outcomes.

The book's enduring value lies not in any single insight but in the way it trains the reader to think. After reading Naked Economics, you will instinctively ask “what are the incentives? ” when a new policy is proposed, “who bears the risk?” when a financial product is marketed, and “compared to what?” when someone condemns sweatshops, free trade, or market outcomes. These are the questions that separate economic literacy from economic illiteracy, and they are more important in the twenty-first century than ever before.

Wheelan proved that economics can be explained without a single equation, graph, or piece of jargon — and that when stripped to its essentials, it is one of the most fascinating and relevant subjects in the world. That demonstration alone makes the book important. The fact that it's also genuinely entertaining is what makes it stick.
