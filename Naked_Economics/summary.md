# Naked Economics: Undressing the Dismal Science
## By Charles Wheelan | Foreword by Burton G. Malkiel
### W.W. Norton & Company, 2010 (Fully Revised and Updated) | ~370 pages

## One-Line Summary
A witty, jargon-free tour of economic principles showing how markets, incentives, information, government, money, trade, and human capital shape every aspect of modern life.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Markets work through self-interest, not altruism.** Adam Smith's invisible hand — people pursuing their own goals inadvertently serve society. Coca-Cola rushed into East Germany not out of charity but to expand its market; the result was identical to humanitarian aid.

2. **Incentives are the most powerful force in economics.** Every policy, regulation, and institution creates incentives — sometimes perverse ones. Mexico City's driving ban increased pollution because families bought second (older, dirtier) cars. London's congestion charge cut traffic 20% because it aligned the cost of driving with the social cost.

3. **Government is essential but easily captured.** Markets fail (externalities, public goods, information asymmetry, monopolies), requiring government intervention. But government is vulnerable to organized interests — small groups extract concentrated benefits while diffuse costs go unnoticed (mohair subsidies, ethanol, sugar quotas).

4. **Information asymmetry explains much of economic life.** Adverse selection (health insurance death spiral), moral hazard (bailout expectations), signaling (college degrees as sorting mechanism), and brand power (McDonald's consistency) all stem from the fact that one party knows more than the other.

5. **Human capital is the engine of prosperity.** Productivity — what we can produce per hour — is the only sustainable source of rising living standards. Education, health, and institutions that foster innovation determine whether nations thrive or stagnate.

6. **Financial markets are powerful but imperfect.** They raise capital, manage risk, and allocate resources efficiently — but perverse incentives (principal-agent problems, short-term bonus structures) can produce catastrophic failures. Index funds beat most active managers over 20 years.

7. **The Federal Reserve is the most powerful economic institution on earth.** It controls the money supply and therefore interest rates, inflation, and the pace of economic growth. Getting monetary policy right is "the economic equivalent of brain surgery."

8. **Trade makes countries richer — but creates concentrated losers.** Comparative advantage means even countries that are worse at everything benefit from specializing. The correct response to trade displacement is compensation and retraining, not protectionism.

9. **Development depends on institutions, not resources.** Property rights, rule of law, honest government, openness to trade, investment in human capital (especially women's education), and corruption control matter far more than natural resources or foreign aid.

10. **Economics is like gravity — ignore it at your peril.** Every policy choice involves trade-offs, opportunity costs, and incentive effects. Economic illiteracy leads to policies that sound compassionate but produce perverse outcomes (French 35-hour workweek, driving bans that increase pollution, protectionism that hurts the poor most).

## Thematic Breakdown

### Markets & Prices (Ch 1)
The power of decentralized decision-making, price signals, opportunity cost, utility maximization, the market as amoral (not immoral), creative destruction, and the superstar phenomenon.

### Incentives & Behavior (Ch 2)
Principal-agent problems, perverse incentives, the tragedy of the commons, prisoner's dilemma, behavioral economics (bounded rationality, hot hand fallacy), and the black rhino as case study.

### Government's Role (Ch 3-4)
Externalities, public goods, information problems, taxation, regulation — why government is necessary but also how organized interests (Ch 8) capture and distort government action.

### Information Economics (Ch 5)
Adverse selection, moral hazard, signaling, brands as information shortcuts, and why markets for health insurance are fundamentally different from markets for bread.

### Human Capital & Productivity (Ch 6)
Education as investment, the skills premium, why Bill Gates is rich, and productivity as the only sustainable source of rising living standards.

### Financial Markets (Ch 7)
Raising capital, storing wealth, managing risk, efficient markets, behavioral finance, index funds, and the four rules of investing.

### Macroeconomics (Ch 9-10)
GDP and its limitations, the business cycle, fiscal vs. monetary policy, inflation/deflation, the Federal Reserve's tools and challenges, and Ben Bernanke's unconventional crisis response.

### International Economics (Ch 11-12)
Exchange rates, purchasing power parity, trade and comparative advantage, sweatshops, protectionism, the euro's structural problems, and Iceland's collapse.

### Development (Ch 13)
Why nations are rich or poor — institutions vs. resources, property rights, corruption, foreign aid debate (Sachs vs. Easterly), women's education, and openness to trade.

## Style & Approach
Wheelan writes in a conversational, witty style packed with vivid real-world examples (Coca-Cola at the Berlin Wall, drug trafficker submarines, prison mackerel currency, blind orchestra auditions). No equations, no graphs, no jargon. Each chapter builds from a memorable story to a core economic principle to policy implications. The book is essentially an Econ 101 course delivered as a series of entertaining essays.
