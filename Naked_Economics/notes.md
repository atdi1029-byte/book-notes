# Naked Economics: Undressing the Dismal Science — Charles Wheelan
## Notes

### FOREWORD (by Burton G. Malkiel) — pp. 19-26

N001 [SHOULD KNOW] Thomas Carlyle labeled economics "dismal science" not because it was boring, but because scarcity is pervasive — we must make choices between competing satisfactions, between jam today and jam tomorrow, and everything has a cost requiring work and sacrifice.

N002 [SHOULD KNOW] Economics is harder than physical sciences because we cannot run controlled laboratory experiments and people don't always behave predictably. Behavioral economics combines psychology and economics but we still can't predict individual behavior with precision. "Far from understanding everything does not mean we understand nothing."

N003 [MUST KNOW] Malkiel identifies core elements of thinking like an economist: deductive reasoning with simplified models (supply/demand), identifying trade-offs within constraints, measuring costs in terms of foregone benefits (opportunity cost), pursuing efficiency (getting most from limited resources), marginalist/incremental approach, recognizing resources have diverse uses and substitutions can be made, predilection for individual choice and competitive markets.

N004 [SHOULD KNOW] Economists agree far more than the public thinks. Bipartisan majority agrees: free international trade improves living standards, tariffs/quotas reduce welfare, rent controls reduce housing volume/quality, NYC taxi medallion limits and rent control cause shortages. "A bipartisan majority of economists is quite likely to unite on the opposite side of a bipartisan coalition of politicians."

N005 [NICE TO KNOW] Peter Lynch (Fidelity Magellan) once said spending 14 minutes talking to an economist wastes 12 minutes. Ironic because fund managers are now regularly evaluated using techniques from financial economists. Options traders carry handheld computers with economic models for pricing puts and calls.

### INTRODUCTION — pp. 27-38

N006 [MUST KNOW] Wheelan's core argument: Economics is poorly taught (dry, mathematical, graph-heavy) which drives away intellectually curious people. But the ideas are intuitive, powerful, and relevant. "This is not economics for dummies; it is economics for smart people who never studied economics." The great ideas are intuitive when dressings of complexity are peeled away — "That is naked economics."

N007 [SHOULD KNOW] Examples of counterintuitive economic thinking that the book promises to explore: (1) Why collapse of Soviet Union was inevitable without price system; (2) Smokers provide economic benefit to nonsmokers (die earlier, leaving more Social Security/pensions); (3) Mandating generous maternity leave may hurt women (employers discriminate against young women in hiring).

N008 [MUST KNOW] Economic illiteracy is widespread among citizens and leaders. Example: French government in 2000 lowered max workweek from 39 to 35 hours to fix unemployment — logic was "if people work fewer hours, there will be work left for the unemployed." Based on the lump of labor fallacy (fixed number of jobs that must be rationed). The policy failed; Sarkozy government in 2008 passed legislation letting companies negotiate it away.

N009 [MUST KNOW] Ross Perot's "giant sucking sound" warning about NAFTA taking American jobs — the economics were wrong, it didn't happen. International trade creates some losers but doesn't make us collectively worse off. The assertion that trade makes us poorer is "the economic equivalent of warning that the U.S. Navy is at risk of sailing over the edge of the world."

N010 [SHOULD KNOW] Even free-market advocates (Milton Friedman) concede unfettered markets can fail. Example: American automobile externality — we don't pay the full cost of driving (emissions, congestion, road wear, danger to smaller cars). Effect is like being "on the town with Dad's credit card." Solution requires higher taxes on gasoline/cars so cost reflects true social cost. Not laissez-faire — government intervention needed.

N011 [SHOULD KNOW] Glenn Loury's affirmative action thought experiment: 10 applicants (9 white, 1 black), equal merit means black candidate hired. But all 9 white candidates go away angry feeling discriminated against, when only 1 would have gotten the job anyway. Affirmative action can harm the very race relations it seeks to heal by creating perception of unfairness far exceeding actual impact.

N012 [SHOULD KNOW] Insurance mandate example: Clinton's 1998 drive to mandate 2-night hospital stays for new mothers. The hidden cost: insurers raise premiums → some people on the margin can no longer afford any health insurance. Real policy question: "Are we willing to pass a law that will make many women more comfortable if it means a much smaller number of men and women will lose coverage for basic care?"

N013 [MUST KNOW] The 2008 financial crisis was predictable from incentives: mortgage brokers paid on commission (not their money at risk) → banks sell loans to third parties (hot potato — doesn't matter how bad the loan if you pass it on before default) → Wall Street creates CDOs and credit-default swaps → system collapses. Chapter 2 will explain the full chain.

N014 [SHOULD KNOW] Behavioral economics is relatively new to mainstream: humans underestimate some risks (obesity) and overestimate others (flying), let emotion cloud judgment, overreact to both good and bad news. David Brooks: "Economic behavior can be accurately predicted through elegant models. This view explains a lot, but not the current financial crisis."

N015 [SHOULD KNOW] Ben Bernanke was a scholar of the Great Depression before becoming Fed chair. His creative and aggressive interventions at the Fed, inspired by 1930s lessons, prevented the crisis from getting much worse.

N016 [SHOULD KNOW] Blind orchestra auditions study (Claudia Goldin, Cecilia Rouse): When 1950s American orchestras began blind auditions behind screens, women were ~50% more likely to make it past first round and several times more likely to make the final cut. Economics as tool for studying discrimination.

### CHAPTER 1: The Power of Markets — Who Feeds Paris? (pp. 41-68)

N017 [MUST KNOW] Coca-Cola and the Berlin Wall (1989): Douglas Ivester (Coca-Cola Europe head, later CEO) sent sales force to pass out free Coke through holes in the Wall. East German currency was worthless so it was money-losing short term. By 1995, per capita Coke consumption in former East Germany matched West Germany. "Adam Smith's invisible hand passing Coca-Cola through the Berlin Wall" — self-interest (expanding market, boosting profits) served consumers without humanitarian intent. This is the punch line of capitalism: markets align incentives so self-interest leads to improving standard of living.

N018 [MUST KNOW] "Who feeds Paris?" — rhetorical question highlighting that billions of transactions happen daily without central direction. Fresh tuna travels from South Pacific fishing fleet to a Rue de Rivoli restaurant, fruit vendor has exactly what customers want from 10-15 countries. No government directing it. Progress metric: in 1971 a 25-inch color TV cost average worker 174 hours of wages; now about 23 hours for a better set. Life expectancy doubled in 20th century (47→77), infant mortality dropped 93%.

N019 [MUST KNOW] Soviet pharmacy anecdote: Soviet official visits American pharmacy, impressed by thousands of remedies, asks "How can you make sure every store stocks all of these items?" — reveals total misunderstanding of markets. No central authority tells stores what to stock. Soviet economy failed because government bureaucrats directed everything from soap production in Irkutsk to university enrollment in Moscow — task proved overwhelming.

N020 [SHOULD KNOW] Cuba cigar shopping: Wheelan's Illinois delegation discovers the "whole point of communism" — every cigar costs the same everywhere, no competition, no profit, no comparison shopping possible. Every shopkeeper paid government wage unrelated to sales volume.

N021 [MUST KNOW] Gary Becker (Nobel 1992, borrowing from George Bernard Shaw): "Economy is the art of making the most of life." Economics studies how we allocate finite supply of everything worth having. Stark inequality: Alex Rodriguez paid $275M for baseball while 1 in 5 children is poor; Three Dog Bakery sells $16 birthday cakes for dogs in Chicago while 15,000 people are homeless in the same city. World Bank: half the world survives on less than $2/day.

N022 [MUST KNOW] Utility maximization: Economics' foundational assumption — individuals act to make themselves as well off as possible (maximize utility). Utility ≠ happiness exactly, but broader — includes getting typhoid immunization and paying taxes. Economists don't judge what gives utility; they accept individual "preferences." Key insight: maximizing utility is NOT synonymous with acting selfishly.

N023 [SHOULD KNOW] Oseola McCarty example: 91-year-old laundress from Hattiesburg, Mississippi, gave $150,000 to University of Southern Mississippi (school she never attended) for scholarships. Not irrational — she derived more utility from giving than from spending on herself. Wesley Autrey: jumped onto subway tracks to save a stranger having convulsions, with his two daughters watching. Americans give $200B+ to charity annually. All compatible with utility maximization.

N024 [MUST KNOW] "Luxury good" has a technical economic meaning: goods we buy in increasing quantities as we grow richer (sports cars, French wine). Crucially, concern for the environment is a luxury good — wealthy nations spend more as a fraction of income on environmental protection. "We care about the Bengal tiger because we can." Policy implication: it's bad economics to impose wealthy-nation environmental preferences on developing countries whose people are starving.

N025 [MUST KNOW] Opportunity cost — the real cost of something is what you must give up. "Free" concert tickets aren't free if you stand in rain for 6 hours. Bus for $1.50 may cost more than $7 taxi if you're late for a $50,000 client meeting. Driving 90 miles to save $50 on shoes isn't saving if your time is worth more.

N026 [MUST KNOW] Price sensitivity explains social phenomena: 10% decrease in cocaine street price → 10% growth in adult cocaine users. Proposed tobacco settlement (rejected 1998) would have raised cigarette prices 34% → reduced teenage smokers by 25% → 1.3 million fewer smoking-related premature deaths. Falling birth rates in developed world: not because feeding/clothing children costs more (those costs fell), but because opportunity cost of parents' time skyrocketed as women gained better professional opportunities. "It's expensive to quit being a neurologist." Average American woman: 3.77 children in 1905, 2.07 now — 45% drop.

N027 [MUST KNOW] Firms maximize profits (revenue minus cost of production). They take inputs (land, steel, knowledge) and combine them to add value — like a chef turning $30 of groceries into an $80 meal. Markets direct resources to most productive use: Brad Pitt in movies ($30M/film) not insurance ($30K). He goes where he adds most value.

N028 [MUST KNOW] Superstar phenomenon (Sherwin Rosen, U Chicago): Small differences in talent → huge pay differentials when market is very large. LeBron James needs to be only slightly better than competitors to capture enormous share of a massive audience market. Rush Limbaugh's $400M contract — doesn't need to be much better than next talk-show host, just a tiny bit more interesting to 20 million listeners. Winner-take-all dynamic.

N029 [SHOULD KNOW] Prices as information signals ("giant neon billboards"): When tuna becomes popular at Paris restaurants → restaurateur orders more → wholesale price rises → Pacific fishermen earn more → some salmon fishermen switch to tuna → more tuna caught → supply meets demand. No one coordinated this; prices did all the work.

N030 [SHOULD KNOW] Barriers to entry: (1) Physical/natural — truffles cost $500/lb because they grow wild, found by pigs/dogs. (2) Legal — patents create monopolies (Pfizer/Viagra), which incentivize R&D investment. (3) Political — 1980s US auto industry lobbied for tariffs/quotas on Japanese cars rather than competing on quality. (4) Subtle — airline gate control (American/United hold 80% of O'Hare gates). (5) Network effects — Microsoft Word dominance not from quality but because everyone uses it, making switching costly.

N031 [SHOULD KNOW] Firms choose production methods based on relative input costs: Nepal airport — men cut grass with sickles (labor cheap, mowers expensive). US — ATMs, self-service gas, phone trees (labor expensive, capital cheap).

N032 [MUST KNOW] Price discrimination: selling same item to different people at different prices. Airlines are masters — Saturday night stay requirement separates business travelers ($1,800 willingness) from leisure travelers ($250 max). Advance purchase, last-minute pricing. Al Gore anecdote: his mother and dog took same arthritis medication but paid different prices — same logic as airline seats. Technology enables perfect price discrimination (online pricing based on browsing history, Priceline bidding). Wall Street Journal: "Grocery stores appear to be the model of one price for all. But even today, they post one price, charge another to shoppers willing to clip coupons and a third to those with frequent-shopper cards."

N033 [MUST KNOW] Market economy is a powerful force for making lives better — firms can only profit by delivering goods people want to buy. Competition drives innovation and cost reduction. 1900: 3-minute phone call NY to Chicago cost $5.45 (~$140 today). Now essentially free with mobile. "How many world leaders fly to North Korea when they need open-heart surgery?"

N034 [MUST KNOW] The market is amoral (not immoral). It rewards scarcity, which has no inherent relation to value. Diamonds worth thousands/carat while water (far more essential) is nearly free. "The market does not provide goods that we need; it provides goods that we want to buy." Doctors provide face-lifts for Hollywood stars (who can pay) rather than health insurance for the poor (who can't).

N035 [SHOULD KNOW] Drug trafficker innovation as market force: As customs officials improved at sniffing out border crossings, traffickers switched to small boats across the sea. When Coast Guard tracked fishing boats, traffickers built "go fast" boats. When radar/helicopters deployed, they built homemade submarines — 49-foot sub found in Colombian jungle in 2006 (invisible to radar, carried 4 men + 3 tons of cocaine). 100-foot sub under construction found in 2000 (capacity: 200 tons). Coast Guard Rear Admiral: "Like any business, if you're losing more and more of your product, you try to find a different way." Market is like evolution — "two of the most beautifully adapted species on the planet are the rat and the cockroach."

N036 [MUST KNOW] Prices allocate scarce resources: Who gets Super Bowl tickets? Those willing to pay most. In Soviet Union? Those chosen by Communist Party. If Moscow butcher gets pork shipment, state price is fixed low — he sells to first in line, rest get nothing. "Capitalism and communism both ration goods. We do it with prices; the Soviets did it by waiting in line." (Soviet black markets emerged — butcher likely sold extra pork chops out the back door.)

N037 [MUST KNOW] Markets are self-correcting via price signals. OPEC limits oil production → prices rise → high prices are "both a symptom and a potential cure": (1) consumers drive less, insulate homes, buy smaller cars, (2) non-OPEC producers pump more, OPEC members cheat on quotas, (3) alternative energy R&D increases. Eventually supply rises + demand falls → prices drift back down. 2008 example: gas at $4/gallon → SUV sales plunged, subcompact sales rose, Americans drove fewer total miles (first monthly drop in 30 years), transit ridership highest since interstate highway system creation. Unintended: some switched to motorcycles — every $1 gas price increase associated with 1,500 additional motorcycle deaths annually.

N038 [MUST KNOW] When prices are fixed, firms compete on other dimensions. Pre-1978 airline regulation: all flights same price, so airlines competed on quality (bigger seats, better food, dressed-up passengers). After deregulation: price competition → quality fell but inflation-adjusted fares fell by nearly half. South Africa gas stations (price fixed by government): attendants in sharp uniforms with bow ties, spotless bathrooms — competing on service because price competition was impossible.

N039 [MUST KNOW] Every voluntary market transaction makes both parties better off. Sweatshop argument: the problem is there aren't enough sweatshops, not too many. Workers take these jobs voluntarily because they're the best available option. Nike pays Vietnamese workers ~$600/year — "pathetic" but twice the average Vietnamese income. Closing sweatshops doesn't create better jobs; it eliminates the best option poor workers have. Sweatshops played important role in development of South Korea and Taiwan.

N040 [MUST KNOW] Behavioral economics challenges strict rationality. Richard Thaler's cashew experiment: guests wolfing down cashews before dinner, Thaler removed the bowl, guests thanked him. This violates core microeconomics — removing options should never make rational people better off. Implication: if humans lack self-discipline to do what's in their long-term interest (save for retirement, quit smoking, lose weight), society could make them better off by "taking the cashew bowl away" — the public policy equivalent.

N041 [SHOULD KNOW] Daniel Kahneman (Nobel 2002, Princeton): studies how human decisions "systematically depart from those predicted by standard economic theory." Concept of "bounded rationality" — we use intuition/rules of thumb (heuristics) rather than careful analysis. Usually works fine, sometimes leads to systematic errors that diminish utility.

N042 [SHOULD KNOW] Humans are bad at assessing risk/probability. Motorcycle example: rider says "You're willing to fly on a plane, aren't you?" but motorcycles are 2,000x more dangerous per kilometer, 14x more likely to end in death per journey. Financial literacy: Lusardi/Mitchell (Dartmouth/Wharton) found only 1/3 of Americans over 50 could do simple interest rate calculations; most didn't understand investment diversification. "Financial illiteracy is widespread."

N043 [SHOULD KNOW] Pattern-seeking bias and the "hot hand" fallacy: cognitive psychologists disproved the basketball "hot hand" using NBA data (76ers) and controlled experiments (Cornell teams). 91% of fans believe a player making last 2-3 shots has better chance on next shot — no evidence supports this. Same bias drove the housing bubble: "what goes up must keep going up."

N044 [MUST KNOW] Markets are "what democracy is to government: a decent, if flawed, choice among many bad alternatives." Markets are consistent with individual liberty — the state shouldn't tell us where to live, what to do, how to spend. Communist governments of 20th century killed ~100 million of their own people in peacetime. Markets motivate us because we benefit directly from our own hard work. But "we can and should use government to modify markets in all kinds of ways" — the 21st century debate is over how unfettered markets should be, not capitalism vs. communism.

### CHAPTER 2: Incentives Matter (pp. 69-93)

N045 [MUST KNOW] Black rhinoceros case study — perfect illustration of incentive analysis. Population fell from 65,000 (1970) to 4,000. Rhino horn fetches $30,000 on black market (aphrodisiac/fever reducer in Asia, Yemenese dagger handles) in countries where income is ~$1,000/year. "The black rhino is worth far more dead than alive to the people of impoverished southern Africa."

N046 [MUST KNOW] Tragedy of the commons applied to rhinos: communal property creates perverse incentives. If a single rancher owned all rhinos, even a greedy, selfish one would breed and protect them (steady supply of horns, like cattle). But communal ownership means: (1) local villagers get no benefit from living rhinos (only crop damage), (2) conservation is a free-rider problem — if one tour company invests in conservation, competitors get same benefit without the cost, (3) the only party with clear incentive is the poacher.

N047 [MUST KNOW] Conservation solutions that align incentives: (1) Eco-tourism where local citizens share profits — Costa Rica set aside 25%+ of country as national parks, tourism generates $1B/year (11% of national income). (2) Dehorning rhinos — remove the incentive to poach. Mixed results: poachers sometimes still kill dehorned rhinos to avoid tracking same animal again, and horn stumps have value. (3) Mountain gorilla example (only 620 left): locals preserved habitat not from respect but because tourist money exceeded timber value. Civil wars devastated tourism → incentive collapsed. Local man: "The gorillas are important when they bring in tourists. If not, they are not. If the tourists don't come, we will try our luck in the forest."

N048 [MUST KNOW] Adam Smith's foundational insight: "It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest." Bill Gates didn't leave Harvard to join Peace Corps — he founded Microsoft, becoming one of the richest men while launching the PC revolution. Self-interest makes the world go around. "From each according to his abilities, to each according to his needs" made a wonderful folk song; as an economic system, it led to everything from inefficiency to mass starvation.

N049 [MUST KNOW] East German car factories were destroying value — manufacturing process so inefficient that finished cars were worth less than the inputs. "They took perfectly good steel and ruined it!" Hindustan Fertilizer Corporation (India): government-run plant operated for 12 years with 1,200 employees reporting daily, but never produced any salable fertilizer. No mechanism to force shutdown when government bankrolls the business.

N050 [SHOULD KNOW] North Korea's economy in shambles: cannot feed itself, produces nothing valuable for trade, on brink of famine. 1990s famine killed ~1 million people, left 60% of children malnourished. Journalists described people eating grass and scouring railroad tracks for fallen food.

N051 [MUST KNOW] Teacher pay and adverse selection: American teachers' pay is not linked to performance (unions oppose merit pay). Uniform pay scale based on experience + years of schooling → creates adverse selection: brightest students least likely to choose education major; among education majors, those with highest test scores least likely to become teachers; among teachers, those with highest scores most likely to leave early. "Any system that pays all teachers the same provides a strong incentive for the most talented among them to look for work elsewhere."

N052 [MUST KNOW] Perverse incentives / law of unintended consequences: (1) Airplane car seat mandate: requiring car seats for children on planes → families must buy extra seat → some drive instead → driving is far more dangerous → net result: more injuries and deaths. (2) Mexico City pollution program (1989): banned cars one day per week by license plate number → families bought second (older, dirtier) cars → more polluting cars on road → gas consumption increased, air quality didn't improve → policy replaced by mandatory emissions testing.

N053 [MUST KNOW] London congestion charge — incentives used correctly: 2003, £5 ($8) fee for driving into central London during peak hours (7am-6:30pm). Results within one month: traffic fell 20% (settled at 15%), average speed doubled, bus delays cut in half, bus ridership up 14%. Revenue used for transit. Surprising outcome: fee deterred so many drivers that revenue was lower than expected. Retailers opposed it but results were dramatic.

N054 [SHOULD KNOW] Energy/environment incentive solution: make carbon-based energy more expensive → people use less → less pollution. Simple market logic. Wheelan's father: "no great affection for the environment but could squeeze a nickel out of a stone" — stalked around closing closet doors complaining about air-conditioning costs.

### CHAPTER 2: Incentives Matter (pp. 69-93) — continued

N055 [MUST KNOW] Principal-agent problem: Burger King's "Your meal is free if you don't get a receipt" sign is an ingenious management tool. The problem: employees (agents) can steal by selling food without ringing it up on the cash register and pocketing the cash. Rather than spend money monitoring employees, Burger King enlists customers to do the monitoring. This is what economists call a principal-agent problem — the principal (Burger King) employs an agent (the cashier) who has incentives that are not necessarily aligned with the firm's interests.

N056 [MUST KNOW] CEO principal-agent problem: Corporate America is rife with misaligned incentives between shareholders (principals) and executives (agents). Two-thirds of all corporate mergers do not add value to the merged firms, and a third leave shareholders worse off. Why do CEOs pursue them? Because CEOs benefit from mergers even when shareholders don't — running a bigger company means bigger offices, bigger salaries, bigger airplanes. The shareholder who owns Starbucks stock can't even name the CEO, let alone monitor their behavior.

N057 [MUST KNOW] Stock options as flawed incentive alignment: Stock options were supposed to be the CEO equivalent of the Burger King receipt sign — align CEO interests with shareholders by tying compensation to stock price. If stock rises from $10 to $57, the CEO's options are very valuable. But Paul Volcker warned Wheelan that options are "an instrument of the devil." He was right: executives can "goose" the stock short-term while doing long-term damage. Michael Jensen (Harvard Business School) calls options "managerial heroin" — they create incentive for managers to seek short-term highs while doing enormous long-term damage. Studies show companies with large options grants are more likely to engage in accounting fraud and default on debt.

N058 [MUST KNOW] Wall Street principal-agent problem and the 2008 financial crisis: Investment banks like Lehman Brothers and Bear Stearns were literally destroyed by employees who took huge risks at the firm's expense. Banks could afford to feed the real estate bubble with reckless loans because they could quickly bundle those loans, "securitize" them, and sell them to investors. The problem: bankers making huge commissions on buying/selling "toxic assets" did not bear the full risk. "Heads they win, tails the firm loses." At Lehman Brothers, those most responsible for the collapse didn't have to give back their huge bonuses from the good years. Simon Johnson (IMF): profits were "fed by an ever-increasing volume of transactions founded on a relatively small base of actual physical assets."

N059 [SHOULD KNOW] Credit rating agency conflict of interest: Standard & Poor's, Moody's, and others gave stellar ratings to what later became "toxic assets." The conflict: credit rating agencies are paid by the firms selling the bonds/securities being rated. "That's a little like a used car salesman paying an appraiser to stand around the lot and provide helpful advice to customers." The fundamental corporate incentive problem remains unresolved — firms must reward innovation and risk-taking, but the employees doing the innovating always have more information than their superiors (and superiors have more than shareholders).

N060 [MUST KNOW] Real estate agent incentive misalignment: On the buy side, your agent gets paid a percentage of the purchase price — the more you pay, the more they make, and the less time the process takes. On the sell side, a 3% commission on a $300,000 house means the agent can make $8,400 for listing at $280,000 and selling quickly, or $9,600 for listing at $320,000 but doing many weeks of open houses and work. The agent's strongest incentive is to get a deal done, whether or not the price is favorable to you.

N061 [MUST KNOW] Prisoner's dilemma as model for self-destructive rational behavior: Two men arrested for murder, separated for interrogation. If neither confesses: 5 years (weapons charge). If both confess: 25 years each. If one rats out the other: snitch gets 3 years, other gets life. Each prisoner's best strategy is to confess regardless of what their partner does — yet both confessing produces a worse outcome (25 years) than both staying quiet (5 years). Neither prisoner has done anything irrational, yet the outcome is terrible for both.

N062 [MUST KNOW] Tragedy of the commons applied to fisheries (prisoner's dilemma in practice): Atlantic swordfish, cod, and lobster are being fished to collapse. Rhode Island fisherman John Sorlien told the NYT: "Right now, my only incentive is to go out and kill as many fish as I can. I have no incentive to conserve the fishery, because any fish I leave is just going to be picked up by the next guy." Politicians worsen the problem by subsidizing struggling fishermen — keeping boats in the water that would otherwise quit.

N063 [SHOULD KNOW] Port Lincoln, Australia lobster licenses — property rights solving the commons problem: In the 1960s, the community capped the number of traps and sold licenses. A license purchased in 1984 for $2,000 now fetches ~$35,000. Lobsterman Daryl Spencer: "Why hurt the fishery? It's my retirement fund. No one's going to pay me $35,000 a pot if there are no lobsters left." A 2008 Science study found fisheries managed with individual transferable quotas were half as likely to collapse as those using traditional methods. The solution: creating private property rights over a communal resource aligns individual incentives with conservation.

N064 [MUST KNOW] Creative destruction (Joseph Schumpeter): Markets reward winners but also crush losers. The 1990s were great for Internet companies but bad for electric typewriter makers. Wal-Mart is efficient and offers low prices (buying cheaper goods = having more income), but it devastates mom-and-pop shops everywhere — Al's Glass and Hardware in Pekin, Illinois. At the start of the 20th century, half of Americans worked in farming; now about one in a hundred. Two things have NOT happened: (1) we haven't starved, (2) unemployment isn't 49%. Creative destruction is painful short-term but essential for progress. "Imagine our collective loss of utility if Steve Jobs, Steven Spielberg, and Oprah Winfrey were corn farmers."

N065 [SHOULD KNOW] Creative destruction's human cost: When a plant closes or industry is wiped out, it can be years or an entire generation before workers and communities recover. Abandoned mills in New England, rusting steel plants in Gary, Indiana. Michigan congressman wouldn't let staff drive Japanese cars to work — but Wheelan argues US automakers would have been much stronger if they'd faced Japanese competition head-on in the 1970s-80s instead of seeking political protection. English weavers burned textile mills during the Industrial Revolution to fend off mechanization.

N066 [MUST KNOW] Tax incentives and behavioral responses: It is not easy to transfer money from rich to poor because wealthy taxpayers change their behavior. When Bjorn Borg was taxed at extremely high rates in Sweden, he didn't lobby or write op-eds — he just moved to Monaco. Virginia Postrel (NYT): high marginal tax rates on second earners (often women) function as a "marriage tax" — families with high household incomes see second earners pay ~50 cents in taxes per dollar earned, profoundly distorting the work/stay-home decision. After the 1986 tax reform cut marginal rates for high-income women, their workforce participation jumped three times as much as women who didn't get the same rate cut.

N067 [SHOULD KNOW] Underground economy and tax evasion spiral: If tax rates get high enough, individuals and firms slip into the "underground economy." Norway's underground economy grew from 1.5% of GNP in 1960 to 18% by mid-1990s. This creates a vicious circle: more evasion → government must raise rates on remaining taxpayers → more flight to underground → further rate increases needed. Scandinavian countries with generous social programs funded by high marginal rates have seen large growth in black markets.

N068 [SHOULD KNOW] Government benefits create perverse incentives too: Pre-1996 welfare offered cash payments only to unemployed single mothers, implicitly punishing poor women who were married or employed — "two things that the government generally does not try to discourage." Social Security and Medicare may discourage personal savings (debate among economists is unresolved). By guaranteeing old-age benefits, people may save less for retirement, lowering the national savings rate.

N069 [MUST KNOW] Tax design principles — deadweight loss: Economists favor taxes that are broad, simple, and fair. A tax on red sports cars would be narrow and easily avoided (buy another color), collecting no revenue while preventing enthusiasts from driving their preferred color — making everybody worse off. This phenomenon is called "deadweight loss." A gasoline tax is better: broad base, provides revenue AND incentivizes conservation. "Green taxes" (carbon fuels) and "sin taxes" (cigarettes, alcohol) collect revenue while discouraging harmful activities. Carbon tax: raises revenue from a broad base while creating incentive to conserve nonrenewable resources and curtail CO2 emissions.

N070 [SHOULD KNOW] Regressive vs. progressive taxation trade-off: The most economically efficient tax (broad, simple, fair in the narrow sense) is a lump-sum tax — same amount per person. Margaret Thatcher tried this in 1989 as the "community charge" or "poll tax." Result: Britons rioted in the streets. The tax was efficient but deeply regressive — the poor paid the same as the rich. "Obviously good economics is not always good politics."

N071 [SHOULD KNOW] Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) — smart incentive design for poverty: Unlike traditional welfare that pays people for not working, EITC subsidizes low-wage workers through the tax system, raising their total income above the poverty line. A worker earning $11,000 supporting a family of four might get an additional $8,000 through EITC and matching state programs. The design makes work pay, providing incentive to enter the labor force and develop skills. Obvious limitation: doesn't help people who cannot find work at all — "those are the folks who are likely to be most desperate."

N072 [SHOULD KNOW] Wheelan's closing metaphor: Rockets obey unchanging laws of physics — get the equations right and the rocket lands where it should. "Human beings are more complex than that. A recovering drug addict does not behave as predictably as a rocket in orbit." But we have one powerful tool: people seek to make themselves better off. "Programs, organizations, and systems work better when they get the incentives right. It is like rowing downstream."

### CHAPTER 3: Government and the Economy (pp. 94-119)

N073 [MUST KNOW] Externalities defined via SUV example: Wheelan traded his Honda Civic for a Ford Explorer when his wife got pregnant. The decision to buy an SUV affects everyone else on the road (danger to smaller cars, exhaust, CO2 emissions, melting polar ice caps), yet none of those people had a say in the decision. An externality exists when private costs of behavior differ from social costs. At the Ford dealership, the tally sheet included gas, insurance, and payments — but nothing about asthmatic children, pregnant women in Mini Coopers, or Pacific island nations going underwater.

N074 [MUST KNOW] When externalities exist, the market fails — and the market IS the problem: The movie Michael Clayton illustrates this perfectly. A polluting agribusiness company maximizes profits by selling a pesticide that poisons local water supplies and causes cancer. Farmers unaware of the pollution actually reward the company by buying its cheaper/more effective product. Competitors who invest in making their products nontoxic are punished by the market. The only redress comes through non-market, government-supported mechanisms: the courts. "There is no market solution in this case; the market is the problem."

N075 [NICE TO KNOW] Paris dog waste externality: 650 people a year break bones or are hospitalized in Paris after slipping in dog waste (per NYT). A dog owner weighs costs and benefits of scooping — but nobody speaks for the woman running to catch the bus who makes one misstep. This is why cities have laws requiring pet owners to pick up after their animals — government correcting a small-scale externality.

N076 [SHOULD KNOW] Externalities range from mundane to planet-threatening: The Economist suggested families with small children should fly at the back of the plane ("child-free zone"), noting "Children, just like cigarettes or mobile phones, clearly impose a negative externality on people who are near them." Wheelan's neighbor Stuart bought bongo drums and possibly a high-tech amplifier — both Stuart and the music shop owner were pleased with the transaction, "But I was not happy about that transaction." Third parties affected by transactions they weren't part of.

N077 [SHOULD KNOW] Externality examples — obesity and global warming: Chicago Mayor Richard Daley tried a 1-cent "litter tax" on every $2 of take-out food (classic externality logic), but a judge struck it down as "vague and lacking in uniformity." Now there's talk of a federal "soda tax" to address obesity — health care costs from obesity are now roughly as high as those from smoking. Society picks up the tab through government health programs and higher insurance premiums. Global warming is the ultimate international externality: a steel plant in Pennsylvania emits CO2 that may flood Bangladesh. U.S. emissions cause acid rain killing Canadian forests. Any solution requires raising the cost of greenhouse gas emissions for ALL polluters worldwide.

N078 [SHOULD KNOW] Positive externalities: Wheelan enjoyed a view of the Wrigley Building and Tribune Tower for five years from his office without paying for it — the buildings' owners created beauty that benefited passersby for free. A business investing in a downtrodden neighborhood may attract other investment but isn't compensated for being the anchor — which is why local governments offer subsidies for revitalization. Positive externalities mean the market under-provides desirable things.

N079 [SHOULD KNOW] Smoking as dual externality: Negative — smokers impose health care costs on society ($28 million/year in Czech Republic pension savings from premature deaths offset by public health costs). All 50 states sued tobacco companies for extra health care costs borne by taxpayers. Positive — smokers die seven years earlier on average (American Lung Association), so nonsmokers get more back from Social Security and pensions relative to what they paid in. Philip Morris actually commissioned a report for the Czech Republic showing the net benefit of smoking to government was $148 million when including taxes and subtracting health costs.

N080 [MUST KNOW] Dealing with externalities — regulation vs. taxation: Government can regulate (ban it, like zoning laws) or tax it (raise the private cost to match the social cost). Economists often prefer taxation: Robert Frank (Cornell) argues we're in an SUV arms race — families buy bigger vehicles because everyone else does. Don't ban the Hummer; raise the private cost of driving it. If it costs 75 cents/mile instead of 50 cents/mile, fewer people will drive SUVs, AND those who still drive them will be those who value them most (hauling, off-road). A gas-guzzler tax also raises revenue (unlike a ban), encourages Detroit to build fuel-efficient cars (carrot vs. stick), and can offset regressive taxes like the payroll tax.

N081 [MUST KNOW] Limitations of taxing externalities: (1) Getting the tax right is nearly impossible — is the correct per-mile surcharge $0.75, $2.21, or $3.07? Scientists can't agree on climate costs. (2) Equity problem: willingness to pay measures wealth, not moral worth. At $9/gallon, Nantucket beach-goers keep driving while a Chicago contractor who needs a pickup truck for work can't afford it. (3) Slippery slope: every activity generates some externality — wearing spandex in public? The process of finding and taxing externalities can get out of control.

N082 [MUST KNOW] Coase Theorem (Ronald Coase, Nobel 1991): When transaction costs are low and property rights are clearly defined, private parties can resolve externalities on their own without government intervention. Bongo drum example: if Wheelan values silence at $100/hour and Stuart values bongo playing at $50/hour, Wheelan can pay Stuart $75 to stop — both are better off. Key insight: the efficient outcome is the same regardless of who starts with the property right. If Stuart has the right to make noise, Wheelan pays him to stop. If Wheelan has the right to silence, Stuart won't pay enough to override it. Either way, the bongos stop because silence is more valuable than noise.

N083 [SHOULD KNOW] Coase in practice — Ohio power company buying a town: American Electric Power company was emitting a "bizarre blue plume" damaging property and health in a small Ohio town. The Clean Air Act gave 221 residents the right to sue. Rather than stop polluting or face lawsuits, the company bought the entire town — paying residents roughly 3x their home values in exchange for signed agreements never to sue. For $20 million, the utility's problems vanished. NYT headline: "Utility Buys Town It Choked, Lock, Stock and Blue Plume."

N084 [SHOULD KNOW] Coase's limitation — transaction costs: Private resolution only works when transaction costs (finding parties, negotiating, legal fees) are low. Stuart and Wheelan can haggle over the fence. American Electric Power can strike a deal with 221 homeowners. But CO2 emissions affect 6 billion people — "It takes a long time to write checks to six billion people, particularly when you are already late for work." Property rights for greenhouse gases are also ambiguous: Do I have the right to emit unlimited CO2, or does a Pacific island nation have the right to stop me from submerging their country?

N085 [MUST KNOW] Government doesn't just fix market failures — it makes markets possible in the first place. "Good government makes a market economy possible. Period." Countries without functioning governments (Nigeria, Angola, Somalia) are not oases of free market prosperity — they are places where it's expensive and difficult to conduct even the simplest business. Nigeria: firms face BYOI — "bring your own infrastructure." Angola: spent $900 million in oil revenue on weapons in 1999 while 1 in 3 children died before age 5 and life expectancy was 42. A UN report placed much of the blame for world poverty on bad government.

N086 [MUST KNOW] Government's essential roles for a functioning market: (1) Defines and protects property rights — you own your home, car, business; you can sell it, rent it, use it as collateral. Without this, imagine tending crops all summer only to have your neighbor harvest them (which is essentially what Napster did to musicians). (2) Copyright and patents — Pfizer's Viagra patent lets it charge $7/pill when ingredients cost pennies. Without patent protection, Pfizer never would have invested in developing the drug. When drugs come off patent, generics drop the price 80-90%. Average cost of bringing a new drug to market: ~$600 million. Proposed solution: government could buy out patents at fair value and charge whatever price it deems appropriate.

N087 [SHOULD KNOW] Ambiguous property rights stifle development — Native American reservation example: While reporting for The Economist, Wheelan noticed very little private housing on reservations. The reason: tribal land is owned communally by the Indian Nation, not by individual members. Banks won't issue conventional mortgages because they can't foreclose on communal land — no effective collateral. Result: tribal members live in government-financed houses or trailers (which CAN be repossessed). Trailers are assembled thousands of miles away, providing no local construction jobs — exactly what reservations need most.

N088 [MUST KNOW] Government provides infrastructure and institutional framework: Contract law, fraud prevention, sound currency, roads, bridges, ports, dams — all lower the cost of doing business. The 1950s-60s interstate highway system accounted for a significant fraction of new capital created in the US and is associated with large productivity increases in vehicle-intensive industries. The SEC makes stock markets credible. Jerry Jordan (former president, Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland): "Our sophisticated institutions, both public and private, make it possible to undertake complex transactions with total strangers." We send money to banks run by strangers, buy from websites in other states — all because government infrastructure creates trust.

N089 [SHOULD KNOW] India's court system as counterexample: Abdul Waheed filed a lawsuit against his neighbor Mohammad Nanhe over drainage. The case came to trial in June 2000 in Moradabad, near New Delhi — 39 years after it was filed. Both parties were dead; their relatives inherited the case. By one calculation, if no new cases were filed in India, it would take 324 years to clear the existing docket. A 75-year-old man was released from a Calcutta jail after waiting 37 years to be tried for murder — all witnesses and the investigating officer were dead. "Bear in mind that by developing world standards, India has relatively good government institutions."

N090 [MUST KNOW] Public goods — why government must provide certain things: Public goods have two characteristics: (1) the cost of offering them to additional users is very low or zero (a lighthouse, once built, protects all ships), and (2) it's very hard to exclude people who haven't paid (how do you tell a ship's captain to close his eyes as he sails past?). Because of the free rider problem, private enterprise chronically underprovides public goods. Examples: national defense (neighbor Etienne won't pay for missile defense because he gets the benefit free anyway), basic research (discoveries must be shared freely to maximize value — you won't get rich from basic science), law enforcement (private security guards won't track down Mexican drug kingpins), parks/open space (many beneficiaries of Chicago's lakefront never set foot on it — they enjoy the view from high-rises or Lake Shore Drive).

N091 [SHOULD KNOW] Stephen King's "The Plant" as free rider experiment: King offered monthly installments of his novel directly to readers via the Internet for $1 per download, on the honor system. He warned the story would fold if fewer than 75% paid. Outcome was "sadly predictable to economists who have studied these kinds of problems" — only 46% paid, and the story folded. This is the basic problem with public goods left to private enterprise: firms cannot force consumers to pay, no matter how much utility they derive.

N092 [MUST KNOW] Government redistributes wealth — but economics can't say how much is "right": Most government benefits go to the middle class (Medicare, Social Security), not the poor. The most important redistribution questions are philosophical, not economic. Amartya Sen's thought experiment: three men need a job. The first is the poorest. The second is the unhappiest (recently became poor, not accustomed to deprivation). The third has a chronic health problem that wages could cure. Who should get the job? There is no "right answer" — and the same is true of wealth redistribution in a modern economy. Economists can't even prove that taking $1 from Steve Jobs and giving it to a starving child improves overall social welfare, because utility can't be measured or compared across individuals.

N093 [SHOULD KNOW] Equality vs. growth tension: A world where everyone earns $25,000 has a smaller total pie but more equal distribution than the status quo (average ~$48,000 but wide inequality). Liberals ignore that a growing pie "will almost always make even the small pieces larger." Conservatives ignore that there are legitimate grounds for policies that shrink the pie (environmental protection, redistribution) and that relative wealth matters for well-being — "we derive utility not just from having a big television but from having a television that is as big as or bigger than the neighbors'."

N094 [MUST KNOW] Libertarian paternalism and "nudge" theory (Thaler & Sunstein): Middle ground between pure libertarianism (let people do whatever they want) and heavy-handed paternalism (government decides for you). The idea: people make systematic errors of judgment, but government shouldn't force you to change — it should just point you in the right direction. Key insight: decisions are driven by inertia/defaults. If your employer auto-enrolls you in a retirement savings plan, most people stick with it. If they don't auto-enroll, many people never sign up even when other plans are available. The "nudge" is to make the default option the one that's best for most people. Anyone can opt out at any time.

N095 [SHOULD KNOW] Organ donation and default effects: Spain, France, Norway, Israel, and many other countries have "opt-out" (presumed consent) laws — you are an organ donor unless you indicate otherwise. The US has "opt-in" — you're NOT a donor unless you sign up. Spain has the highest cadaveric organ donation rate in the world, 50% higher than the US. Economists have found presumed consent laws have significant positive effects on organ donation, controlling for religion and health expenditures. True libertarians reject presumed consent because it implies the government "owns" your organs until you make an effort to get them back.

N096 [SHOULD KNOW] Good government becomes more important as the economy grows more sophisticated: The Internet is driven by the private sector, but government roots out fraud, makes online transactions legally binding, sorts out property rights (domain names), settles disputes, and handles issues not yet imagined. "One sad irony of September 11 was that one simple-minded view of government — that 'taxpayers know better what to do with their money than the government does' — was exposed for its hollowness." Individual taxpayers cannot gather intelligence, track fugitives in Afghanistan, research bioterrorism, or protect airports. Government enables us to do collectively what we cannot do individually.

### CHAPTER 4: Government and the Economy II (pp. 120-143)

N097 [MUST KNOW] Government is a double-edged sword: "Government is good at doing some things and tragically bad at doing others." It can deal with externalities OR regulate an economy to ruin. It can provide essential public goods OR squander tax revenues on pet projects. It can transfer money from wealthy to disadvantaged OR from common folk to the politically well-connected. Soviet Lada joke illustrates: woman orders a car, told to come back in two years on March 17th. "Morning or afternoon?" she asks. "The plumber is coming that day." If the USSR taught us anything, it's that monopoly stifles innovation — and government is one very large monopoly.

N098 [MUST KNOW] Government as monopoly — DMV example: The Department of Motor Vehicles has a monopoly on driver's licenses. Why would they be friendly, stay open longer, or keep the office clean? "None of these things will produce even one more customer!" Every person who needs a license already comes to the DMV no matter how unpleasant the experience. Contrast with the private sector: if a rat runs across the counter at your Chinese take-out restaurant, you stop ordering there — end of problem. But if you stop going to the DMV, you go to jail.

N099 [SHOULD KNOW] Fidelity vs. Post Office contrast: Wheelan waited weeks for a check that never arrived. Called Fidelity — a friendly assistant immediately canceled the check, issued a new one, and apologized profusely for a problem they didn't cause. Called the post office — the local postmaster doesn't accept phone complaints. The USPS has a monopoly on first-class mail delivery, "and it shows." Government should not be the sole provider of goods/services unless there's a compelling reason the private sector would fail. FedEx and UPS proved private firms can build worldwide delivery infrastructure.

N100 [MUST KNOW] Government should finance, not necessarily produce: Even where government has an important role (roads, bridges), government employees don't have to pour the cement. Government can plan, finance, and solicit competitive bids from private contractors. Barack Obama during health care town hall: a woman said "I don't want government-run health care. I don't want socialized medicine. And don't touch my Medicare." The irony: Medicare IS government-run health care — it allows Americans over 65 to see private doctors and get reimbursed by the federal government. Even the CIA opened its own venture capital firm (In-Q-It, named after Q from James Bond) because government procurement processes were too slow to capture cutting-edge technology.

N101 [MUST KNOW] Government misallocates resources via politics, not markets: Margarita Space Pak example — at a White Sox game, Wheelan saw a vendor with a backpack that made frozen margaritas on the spot. Top engineering minds devoted their scarce talent to baseball beverage delivery rather than cheaper energy or nutrient delivery for malnourished children. But "that's my opinion and I don't run the world." In a market, resources flow where they're valued most. Under government control, resources flow where politicians direct them. Soviet Union churned out massive steel plants but couldn't produce soap or decent cigarettes. Soviet central planners didn't consider birth control an economic priority — result: roughly two abortions for every live birth. After the collapse, Western contraceptives became available and the abortion rate fell by half.

N102 [SHOULD KNOW] Political resource allocation — particle accelerator example: Two leading sites for a high-speed particle accelerator in the early 1990s were northern Illinois (already had a particle accelerator + major federal lab, infrastructure in place) and somewhere in Texas. Wheelan asked a technology expert why Texas was chosen: "Because George [H.W.] Bush was president." Government spent ~$1 billion on the Texas project and then abandoned it. Wall Street Journal headline: "Industries That Backed Bush Are Now Seeking Return on Investment." Jamie Dimon (JPMorgan Chase) called government relations his company's "seventh line of business."

N103 [MUST KNOW] Crony capitalism: When politicians decide who gets bank credit and who doesn't, the result is crony capitalism. In autocratic nations like China and democratic ones like Indonesia, politically connected projects get financing while potentially profitable ventures don't. Consumers lose twice: (1) tax money squandered on projects that go bust (or banking system collapses from politically motivated bad loans), (2) the economy underperforms because credit (a finite resource) is channeled away from worthy projects — car plants don't get built, students don't get loans, entrepreneurs don't get funding.

N104 [MUST KNOW] Regulation trade-offs and regulatory capture: Every regulation carries a cost. Milton Friedman recounts an exchange at the American Bar Association where an economist argued for more permissive bar admission to lower legal costs. A lawyer stood up: "The country cannot afford anything but Cadillac lawyers!" But if only Cadillacs were available, most people couldn't afford any transportation. Sometimes Toyota Corollas are fine. George Stigler (Nobel 1982): firms and professional associations often SEEK regulation as a way to hobble competitors. Illinois Cosmetology Association lobbied for stricter manicurist licensing — not to protect consumers, but to block immigrant-run discount nail salons ($6 vs. $25 manicures). After Hitler came to power in 1933, US professions erected "good citizenship" requirements and language exams to block fleeing European professionals — Friedman showed the number of foreign-trained physicians licensed in the 5 years after 1933 was the same as the 5 years before.

N105 [SHOULD KNOW] "Helping hand" vs. "grabbing hand" regulation: Study of 75 countries found enormous variation in cost/time to start a business. Canada: 2 procedures, 2 days. Bolivia: 20 procedures. Mozambique: 6 months. Vietnam, Mozambique, Egypt, Bolivia: entrepreneurs must give up 1-2x their annual salary just for licensing (not counting bribes). Countries with MORE regulation had LOWER compliance with international quality standards, no improvement in pollution or health, and higher corruption. Excessive regulation pushes entrepreneurs underground where there is no regulation at all.

N106 [SHOULD KNOW] Absurd regulation examples: EU banned the sale of "overly curved, extra knobbly or oddly shaped" fruits and vegetables until November 2008. Representatives from 16 of 27 member nations tried to block the deregulation. Indian government mandates 168 square feet of building space per technical college student — K.M. Kundnani College of Pharmacy in Mumbai has padlocked, empty lecture halls on the top floor but is legally barred from teaching more than 300 students.

N107 [MUST KNOW] DDT and regulatory trade-offs — the lives vs. environment dilemma: DDT is a "persistent organic pollutant" that wreaks havoc on the food chain. Should it be banned? Much of the developing world is ravaged by malaria — 300 million suffer, 1 million+ die annually. DDT is the most cost-effective way to control mosquitoes; the next best alternative is 4x more expensive and less effective. Tanzanian researcher Wen Kilama: if seven Boeing 747s full of children crashed into Mt. Kilimanjaro every day, the world would notice — that's the scale of malaria deaths. Jeffrey Sachs estimates sub-Saharan Africa would be a third richer if malaria had been eradicated in 1965. Even the Sierra Club, Endangered Wildlife Trust, Environmental Defense Fund, and WHO embraced DDT for fighting malaria. UN delegates from 120 countries meeting to ban persistent organic pollutants specifically exempted DDT for malaria use.

N108 [SHOULD KNOW] Gary Becker on regulation design — aggregate vs. individual quotas: Becker (Nobel laureate, Cape Cod striped bass enthusiast) argued in Business Week that aggregate fishing quotas are poorly designed — they encourage each boat to catch as much as possible early in the season before the aggregate limit is reached. Result: fishermen get low prices (seasonal glut), then consumers can't get any fish at all. Massachusetts later switched to individual quotas — total catch still limited but each fisherman can fulfill their quota anytime during the season. Better regulation design preserves the goal (conservation) while minimizing market distortion.

N109 [MUST KNOW] Fiscal drag — taxes reduce economic well-being even when spent well: "Fiscal drag" (Burton Malkiel's term) operates two ways. First, taxes directly reduce purchasing power — government spending on jet fighters means citizens buy fewer televisions, give less to charity, take fewer vacations. Government may not be creating jobs, just moving them around. Second, taxes change behavior in ways that make everyone worse off (deadweight loss). At 50% marginal tax rates, some workers quit — the worker loses their job, and the government raises no revenue. Deadweight loss is like a burglar stealing your family photo album — the thief gets nothing from it, yet you lose something irreplaceable. Taxes also discourage investment: an entrepreneur may invest when expected return is $100M but not when after-tax return is $60M. A graduate degree that raises income 10% may not be worth it if after-tax income only rises 5%.

N110 [MUST KNOW] Laffer Curve — true in theory, misused in practice: Arthur Laffer theorized in 1974 (on a restaurant napkin — Dick Cheney's napkin) that at very high tax rates, cutting taxes can actually increase revenue by stimulating so much economic activity. At 95% tax rates, this is certainly true. But both the Reagan and George W. Bush tax cuts did NOT boost total government revenue — they led to large budget deficits. The wealthiest Americans paid more after the Reagan cuts, but this may be because their incomes rose sharply (economy demanded more brains), not because they worked harder due to lower rates. Bruce Bartlett (Reagan/Bush official) lamented that "supply-side economics" morphed from a defensible idea (lower marginal rates stimulate activity) into the "implausible notion that all tax cuts raise revenue." Greg Mankiw (Bush's Council of Economic Advisers chair) asked on his blog: "If you think tax cuts increase revenue, why advocate spending restraint?"

N111 [SHOULD KNOW] Tax cuts: the real trade-off: A $1 tax cut may only cost the government $0.80 or $0.50 in revenue (because the economy grows), but it does NOT pay for itself. "In all but the most extraordinary of circumstances, there is no free lunch." The bastardization of supply-side economics has transformed an important intellectual debate (should we pay more in taxes for more government services, or pay less and get less?) into the intellectually dishonest premise that "we can pay less and get more." Growth matters: when the economy slows, it's steelworkers and busboys who get laid off, not brain surgeons and professors. In 2009, the poverty rate exceeded 13% — highest in a decade. Rebecca Blank (Michigan economist, Clinton CEA member) noted the 1990s expansion was remarkably good for those at the bottom of the economic ladder.

### CHAPTER 4: Government and the Economy II (pp. 120-143) — continued

N112 [MUST KNOW] Wheelan's framework for government's role — four categories: (1) Government enhances the productive capacity of the economy (legal framework, public goods, externality correction) — "the notion that smaller government is always better government is simply wrong." (2) Some government activity shrinks the pie but is still socially desirable — transferring $1 to a poor family may cost the economy $1.25 when deadweight costs of taxation are included. France: good place to be born poor, bad place to be an Internet entrepreneur. (3) Reasonable people disagree over the appropriate level of social spending — the US is richer but more unequal than Europe; the trade-off between equality and growth is a matter of values, not economics. (4) Some government involvement is purely destructive — regulations whose costs grossly outweigh benefits, laws serving special interests or corrupt politicians.

N113 [SHOULD KNOW] Minimum wage as factual debate: Theory suggests a higher minimum wage helps workers who keep their jobs but hurts those who lose them (or never get hired). Economists present competing research on how many jobs are actually lost. This is a factual question that data can eventually answer, unlike the philosophical questions about redistribution. "As one policy analyst once pointed out to me, it may be easy to lie with statistics, but it's a lot easier to lie without them."

N114 [MUST KNOW] Government as surgeon's scalpel: Jerry Jordan (former president, Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland): "What separates the economic 'haves' from the 'have-nots' is whether the role of an economy's institutions — particularly its public institutions — is to facilitate production or to confiscate it." Government is "like a surgeon's scalpel: It is an intrusive tool that can be used for good or for ill. Wielded carefully and judiciously, it will facilitate the body's remarkable ability to heal itself. In the wrong hands, or wielded overzealously with even the best of intentions, it can cause great harm."

### CHAPTER 5: Economics of Information (pp. 144-166)

N115 [MUST KNOW] Hope Scholarships — adverse selection kills a well-designed program: Clinton's 1992 proposal (based on a Yale experiment) would let students borrow for college and repay as a percentage of post-graduation income (e.g. 1.5% for 15 years) instead of fixed payments. Brain surgeons would pay back more than kindergarten teachers. In theory it would be self-financing. Fatal flaw: adverse selection. Aspiring Wall Street bankers (who know they'll earn high incomes) would avoid the program because a conventional loan would be cheaper. Future kindergarten teachers and Peace Corps volunteers would flock to it. The program attracts only low earners, making it unable to recover costs. The Yale experiment was "quietly canceled after five years" because repayments fell short and administrative costs were prohibitive.

N116 [MUST KNOW] Nobel Prize for information economics (2001): George Akerlof, Michael Spence, and Joseph Stiglitz shared the Nobel for work on asymmetric information — problems that arise when one party to a transaction knows more than the other. Akerlof's "The Market for Lemons" (1970) used the used-car market: sellers know more about car quality than buyers. This creates adverse selection — owners of good used cars are less likely to sell (they know the car's true value), so the market fills with "lemons." Buyers anticipate this and demand a discount, which drives out even more good cars. In theory, the market for high-quality used cars should not function at all.

N117 [MUST KNOW] Statistical discrimination — rational but unfair: A law firm interviewing two equally qualified Harvard Law grads (one male, one female) may rationally hire the man because women still bear most child-rearing responsibility — the female candidate is more likely to take maternity leave and possibly not return. "Yet the firm's logic makes sense" — Gary Becker pointed out in The Economics of Discrimination that employers with a "taste for discrimination" sacrifice profits. But statistical discrimination is different: a law firm minimizing turnover by playing statistical averages may offend sensibilities and violate federal law, "but it is not foolish." Solution: refundable maternity benefits — keep the benefit if you come back to work, return it if you don't. This eliminates the information asymmetry and makes firms willing to offer generous benefits.

N118 [SHOULD KNOW] Criminal background checks and racial discrimination paradox: An employer with no racial prejudice but an aversion to hiring criminals will, without access to criminal records, discriminate against black male applicants — who are statistically far more likely to have served time (28% vs. 4% for white males). Study found that employers who DO check criminal backgrounds are MORE likely to hire African-American workers. Suppressing criminal record information to help ex-offenders may actually increase racial discrimination against the broader black male population. "Curtailing access to criminal history records may actually harm more people than it helps and aggravate racial differences in labor market outcomes."

N119 [MUST KNOW] Health care as the ultimate information problem: Five concurrent issues make health care fundamentally different from buying a TV: (1) patients who don't pay the bill demand as much care as possible, (2) doctors maximize income and minimize lawsuits by delivering as much care as possible, (3) insurance companies maximize profits by paying for as little care as possible, (4) technology introduces massively expensive options (some miraculous, some wasteful), (5) it's very costly for either side to prove the "right" course of treatment. Prostate cancer example: "watchful waiting" costs essentially nothing; proton radiation therapy (requiring a football-field-sized accelerator) costs ~$100,000. RAND Corporation analysis: "No therapy has been shown superior to another." David Leonhardt (NYT): the prostate cancer test is "his personal litmus test" for health care reform.

N120 [MUST KNOW] HMOs — swapping one information problem for another: Fee-for-service medicine creates incentives for over-treatment (doctors do more, earn more, avoid lawsuits). HMOs flip the incentives: doctors are paid a fixed fee per patient per year regardless of services provided, may be restricted in what they can prescribe, and may even get bonuses for NOT referring to specialists. Now the person who knows most about your condition has an economic incentive to deny you care. "Complaints about too much spending are replaced by complaints about too little spending."

N121 [MUST KNOW] Adverse selection death spiral in health insurance: Insurance works by pooling risk. Aetna insures 20,000 fifty-year-old men at $1,300/year (average cost: $1,250). Problem: healthiest men know they're overpaying and opt out. Pool becomes sicker on average → premium rises to $1,800 → more healthy men leave → premium rises further → eventually only the sickest remain and the market collapses. Group policies (all GM employees must be covered) solve this because individuals can't opt out. Individual policies are "a much scarier undertaking" — the people who most want insurance are those who most need it, "no matter how much an insurance company charges." Contrived but illuminating dialogue: patient learns he needs open-heart surgery, responds "Then I should probably buy some health insurance first."

N122 [MUST KNOW] Genetic testing as the ultimate adverse selection challenge: A single strand of hair contains your entire genetic code — predispositions to heart disease, cancer, depression, Alzheimer's (25 years before onset). If insurers access genetic information, those most prone to illness become uninsurable. If insurers are barred from using it, individuals who know they're high-risk load up on generous policies → devastating adverse selection. The Economist (not a left-wing publication): "Genetic testing may become the most potent argument for state-financed universal health care." People with family history of Huntington's disease already find it hard or impossible to get life insurance.

N123 [MUST KNOW] Health insurance mandate as solution to adverse selection: If you guarantee affordable insurance regardless of health status, the optimal time to buy is in the ambulance on the way to the hospital. The only fix is a "personal mandate" — everyone must buy insurance. Losses from insuring sick people are offset by profits from healthy people forced to participate. Massachusetts adopted this approach; it's effectively what every country with universal health care does. The relevant economics are clear: (1) we know who is sick, (2) we increasingly know who will become sick, (3) sick people can be very expensive, (4) private insurance doesn't work well under these circumstances. The tough part is philosophical — should we share health care costs, and how?

N124 [MUST KNOW] McDonald's as information solution — branding: "McDonald's didn't create a better hamburger." Their genius is information. Every McDonald's tastes the same in Moscow, Mexico City, or Cincinnati. Driving through Nebraska at 9pm, you see McDonald's and "Chuck's Big Burger." You know exactly what McDonald's will deliver — clean, safe, inexpensive, working bathroom, predictable. Chuck's might be amazing or might give you E. coli. "You know all of these things before you get out of your car in a state you've never been in." Branding solves the consumer's information problem for products whose quality can only be determined after purchase (or not at all). Same principle applies to cruises, fashion, tires — Michelin shows babies inside tires: "Because so much is riding on your tires."

### CHAPTER 5: Economics of Information (pp. 144-166) — continued

N125 [SHOULD KNOW] Branding as trust mechanism, not manipulation: Critics attack branding as a tool for extorting premiums on products we don't need. Economics tells a different story: branding provides an element of trust necessary for a complex economy. We conduct transactions with people we've never met — Wheelan mails checks to Fidelity without knowing a single person there. Government regulators can only protect from the most egregious fraud; brands fill the gap. Sara Lee (1997) decided to sell off all manufacturing — no more turkey farms or textile mills. The company would only attach its brand names (Champion, Hanes, Coach, Jimmy Dean) to products manufactured by others. "Sara Lee's strategy for growth and profits is to produce nothing."

N126 [MUST KNOW] Commodity hell vs. branding: In competitive markets, prices are driven toward production cost — "commodity hell" for producers. A soybean is a soybean; Iowa farmers can't charge a penny above market price. How do firms escape? By convincing the world their product is different. "Coca-Cola is not soda; it's Coke." Nike clothes are not fabric sewn in Vietnam; they're Tiger Woods's clothes. Even farmers have adopted branding — Sunkist oranges, Angus beef, Tyson chickens command premium prices.

N127 [SHOULD KNOW] Signaling quality (Michael Spence, Nobel 2001): Firms signal quality through the trappings of success — marble lobbies, Impressionist paintings, Italian suits. These have no inherent relation to professional competence, but we interpret them as signals the firm is top-notch. "They are to markets what a peacock's bright feathers are to a prospective mate: a good sign in a world of imperfect information." In parts of Asia, offices are kept ridiculously cold to signal the firm can afford massive air conditioning. WSJ: "Frosty air conditioning is a way for businesses and building owners to show that they're ahead of the curve on comfort."

N128 [MUST KNOW] Screening mechanisms (Joseph Stiglitz, Nobel 2001): Insurance companies use deductibles to screen customers. Healthy people (who privately know they'll have few claims) choose high-deductible, low-premium policies. Sick people (who know they'll hit the deductible) choose low-deductible, high-premium policies. "The deductible is a tool for teasing out private information; it forces customers to sort themselves." Same principle applies to car insurance — if your 16-year-old son is an even worse driver than most 16-year-olds, you have a sneaking suspicion the deductible matters.

N129 [MUST KNOW] Harvard as signaling mechanism: Krueger and Dale (Princeton/Mellon Foundation) study — "Children Smart Enough to Get into Elite Schools May Not Need to Bother." Students admitted to BOTH a highly selective school and a moderately selective one earned roughly the same average salary regardless of which they attended. The quality of the student matters more than the quality of the school. Exception: students from lower-income families did benefit significantly from attending a more selective school. Krueger's advice: "Don't believe that the only school worth attending is one that would not admit you.... Recognize that your own motivation, ambition and talents will determine your success more than the college name on your diploma."

N130 [SHOULD KNOW] School report cards as information problem: Illinois evaluates schools by standardized test scores. Media labels suburban schools "best." But Eric Hanushek (University of Rochester): "In many suburban communities, students would do well on standardized tests even if they went to school and sat in a closet every day for four years." The missing information: how much value is the school adding? Are they exceptional teachers, or just repositories for privileged kids who'd succeed anywhere? It's the Harvard question again.

N131 [MUST KNOW] Racial profiling as information economics: Does race/ethnicity convey meaningful information related to criminal activity? Yes, in some circumstances — Clinton declared racial profiling "morally indefensible" while his drug czar's website profiled heroin dealers as Mexican nationals (Denver), crack dealers as African-American males (Trenton), powdered cocaine dealers as Latino (also Trenton). Wheelan's own experience: wearing a suit on a westbound bus through segregated Chicago, an older black woman asked "Oh, are the Bulls playing tonight?" — the only reason a white guy in a suit would be on that bus heading west at 7pm. Race can convey information, but the policy question is whether we should act on it. The costs of harassing 10-20 or 100 law-abiding people to catch one more drug dealer are usually not worth it. Terrorism is trickier because costs of missing one person are catastrophically high.

### CHAPTER 6: Productivity and Human Capital (pp. 167-189)

N132 [MUST KNOW] Human capital defined: The sum total of skills embodied in an individual — education, intelligence, charisma, creativity, work experience, entrepreneurial vigor, even the ability to throw a baseball fast. It's what you'd be left with if someone stripped away all your assets and left you on a street corner with only the clothes on your back. Bill Gates would do fine — companies would snap him up as consultant, board member, CEO, motivational speaker. Bubba who dropped out in 10th grade with a meth addiction? Not so well. The sultan of Brunei might not do well either — he's rich because his kingdom sits on oil, not because of personal skills.

N133 [MUST KNOW] Skill scarcity determines compensation, not social value: Robert Solow (Nobel 1987, avid baseball fan) was asked if it bothered him that Roger Clemens earned more for a single Red Sox season than Solow earned for winning the Nobel Prize. "No. There are a lot of good economists, but there is only one Roger Clemens." Alex Rodriguez: $275 million over 10 years from the Yankees because virtually no one else can hit a round ball traveling 90+ mph as consistently. The price of a skill has no inherent relation to its social value, only its scarcity.

N134 [MUST KNOW] Human capital as economic passport — Palestinian family anecdote: Gamal Abouali's family insisted he finish his engineering degree in three years instead of four, taking extra classes each quarter and attending school every summer. His father, an accountant, explained: the Palestinian existence was itinerant and precarious. An accountant "could practice nearly anywhere in the world — because, he explained, that is where he might end up." The family had lived in Canada before Kuwait; they might be somewhere else in five years. After Saddam Hussein's 1990 retreat, Palestinians were expelled from Kuwait — the Abouali family's human capital investment proved prescient.

N135 [MUST KNOW] Poverty as a dearth of human capital: "The most insightful way to think about poverty, in this country or anywhere else in the world, is as a dearth of human capital." People are poor because they can't find good jobs — but that's the symptom, not the illness. The underlying problem is lack of skills. Poverty rate for high school dropouts is 12x that of college graduates. India is poor primarily because 35% of the population is illiterate. A high proportion of America's homeless suffer from substance abuse, disability, or mental illness — conditions that render human capital less useful.

N136 [SHOULD KNOW] Two thought experiments on human capital: (1) Drop 100,000 high school dropouts on State Street and Madison in Chicago — social calamity, crime soars, government overwhelmed. Sacramento offered one-way bus tickets out of town to the homeless before the 1996 Olympics. (2) Drop 100,000 graduates from top universities on the same corner — many find jobs immediately, some start businesses, firms relocate to exploit the talent glut, economists call it a boon like waves of immigration.

N137 [SHOULD KNOW] NAWC/HAWC — human capital is the real asset: Naval Air Warfare Center in Indianapolis was closing (2,600 workers). Unlike typical plant closings where workers have narrow skills, 40% of NAWC's workforce were scientists and engineers. Mayor Stephen Goldsmith sold the plant to Hughes Electronics. On Friday, employees went home as government workers; Monday, 98% came back as Hughes employees. Hughes executives said the value lay in the people, not the bricks and mortar. Robert Topel (labor economist): experienced workers lose 25% of earnings capacity when forced to change jobs by plant closings.

N138 [MUST KNOW] Lump of labor fallacy: The mistaken belief that there's a fixed amount of work in an economy, so every new job must come at someone else's expense. This is how the French government justified the 35-hour workweek. It's wrong. Jobs are created anytime someone provides a new good/service or finds a better/cheaper way to provide an old one. The US created tens of millions of new jobs over three decades, including the entire Internet sector. Millions of women entered the labor force without causing permanent unemployment increases. Massive immigration waves didn't produce permanent unemployment either. New workers spend their earnings, creating demand for other products — the pie gets bigger, not merely resliced.

N139 [MUST KNOW] Farming town parable — how human capital creates jobs: Farming community where each family produces just enough to feed itself. Stranger arrives with no skills — town has "no jobs," they give him a bus ticket. But if a Ph.D. agronomist arrives with a new plow that improves corn yields, suddenly everyone is better off. The agronomist can support himself, farmers have more food even after paying for plows, and the community has created a new job: plow salesman. Then a carpenter arrives, then a teacher, then novelists, firefighters, engineers. "This is the one-page economic history of the United States."

N140 [MUST KNOW] Gary Becker on human capital as the dominant form of wealth: Becker (Nobel laureate) reckons that the stock of education, training, skills, and health constitutes about 75% of the wealth of a modern economy. "Not diamonds, buildings, oil, or fancy purses — but things that we carry around in our heads." He called it a "human capitalist economy." Japan and Switzerland are among the richest nations despite poor natural resource endowments. Nigeria has enormous oil wealth that has done relatively little for living standards — in some cases, mineral wealth financed bloody civil wars. Israel has higher per capita income than Saudi Arabia despite having virtually no natural resources.

N141 [SHOULD KNOW] Human capital creates virtuous and vicious cycles: Well-educated parents invest heavily in their children's human capital; disadvantaged parents have the opposite effect. Becker: "Even small differences among children in the preparation provided by their families are frequently multiplied over time into large differences when they are teenagers." This is why the labor market cannot do much for dropouts who can barely read and never developed good work habits.

N142 [MUST KNOW] Productivity is everything: Productivity = efficiency of converting inputs to outputs. "The more productive we are, the richer we are." In 1870, a typical household required 1,800 hours of labor for its annual food supply; today about 260 hours. Average work year fell from 3,100 to 1,730 hours. Real GDP per capita rose from $4,800 to $40,000+. "The poverty line is now at a level of real income that was attained only by those in the top 10 percent of the income distribution a century ago." Keynes: "In the long run, productivity is everything."

N143 [MUST KNOW] Productivity takes the suck out of Perot's "giant sucking sound": Can American workers compete against Mexican workers earning a fraction of US wages? Yes — because American workers produce much more. We're better-educated, healthier, have better access to capital/technology, and have more efficient government institutions. A worker who costs a tenth as much but produces a tenth as much is no bargain. A worker who costs a tenth and produces half as much probably is — which is why unskilled manufacturing (textiles, shoes, basketball shoes) does move abroad. "Can a Vietnamese peasant sew basketball shoes together? Yes — and for a lot less than the American minimum wage."

### CHAPTER 6: Productivity and Human Capital (pp. 167-189) — continued

N144 [MUST KNOW] Rule of 72 and productivity growth rates: Divide 72 by a growth rate to find doubling time. At 2.7% productivity growth (1947-1975), living standards double every 27 years. At 1.4% (1975-mid-1990s), it takes 51 years. The difference seems trivial but is profound. 1947-1975: 2.7% growth. 1975-mid-1990s: 1.4% (reasons still not fully understood). 2000-2008: back to ~2.5%. Debate continues: Greenspan argued IT investments permanently raised productivity growth; Robert Gordon (Northwestern) argued data don't support this when properly interpreted.

N145 [SHOULD KNOW] Productivity growth is not zero-sum: If Japan grows at 4% and the US at 2%, both countries are getting richer. This may cause envy and political friction over time, but the important point is that both are growing. If 500 million people in India became more productive and moved to the middle class, Americans would become richer too — those people would buy our software, cars, music, agricultural exports. "One of those newly educated peasants might be the person who discovers an AIDS vaccine."

N146 [SHOULD KNOW] Factors affecting productivity growth: Investment in physical capital, human capital, R&D, and effective government institutions. Legal/regulatory/tax structures matter — high taxes, bad government, poorly defined property rights, or excessive regulation diminish incentives for productive investment. Collective farms are terrible for agriculture. Discrimination wastes resources — societies that don't educate women or deny opportunities by race/caste leave vast resources fallow. Innovation and technology matter, but their progress is not fully understood.

N147 [MUST KNOW] Malthus refuted by human capital theory: Thomas Malthus predicted population growth would outstrip food production, keeping humanity at subsistence. For 55 of the last 57 centuries, he was right — the human condition didn't change. But the Industrial Revolution changed everything. As Becker explains: "Parents did spend more on children when their incomes rose — as Malthus predicted — but they spent a lot more on each child and had fewer children, as human capital theory predicts." Productivity gains made parents' time more expensive → having more children became costlier → families invested in quality over quantity. One of the most potent weapons against population growth is educating girls — Taiwan doubled female high school graduation between 1966-1975 and fertility dropped by half.

N148 [MUST KNOW] Growing inequality in America — the data: Congressional Budget Office analysis: from 1979-2004, bottom fifth of households saw only 2% income growth (inflation-adjusted) — essentially zero in 25 years. Middle saw 15% growth. Top 20% saw 63% growth. The wealthiest 1% went from earning 9% of national income in 1979 to 16%. Male high school dropout wages have fallen roughly 25% compared to their fathers' earnings. The 2007 recession narrowed the gap slightly — by destroying wealth at the top, not by making workers better off.

N149 [MUST KNOW] Why inequality is growing — "Revenge of the Nerds": Human capital has become more important and better rewarded. College graduates earned 40% more than high school graduates in the early 1980s; now they earn 80% more. Technology makes smart workers more productive while making low-skilled workers redundant (ATMs, self-serve pumps, automated assembly lines, robots). International trade creates new markets for high-skilled exports (Boeing sells aircraft to India) but puts low-skilled workers in competition with Vietnamese workers earning $1/day. Unions have weakened, reducing blue-collar bargaining power. High-wage workers log more hours than low-wage counterparts.

N150 [SHOULD KNOW] Does inequality matter? Two traditional economist arguments for "no": (1) Inequality sends important signals — growing wage gap motivates college enrollment and entrepreneurial risk-taking; (2) We should care about absolute well-being, not relative — Robert Fogel (Nobel, AEA president address 1999): poorest Americans have amenities "unknown even to royalty a hundred years ago" (90%+ of public housing residents have color TVs). But common sense and research suggest relative wealth matters: H.L. Mencken: "A wealthy man is a man who earns $100 a year more than his wife's sister's husband." Neumark/Postlewaite study: women are significantly more likely to seek employment if their sister's husband earns more than their own.

N151 [MUST KNOW] Robert Frank's relative income experiment: Choose between (A) you earn $110,000, everyone else earns $200,000, or (B) you earn $100,000, everyone else earns $85,000. In World A you're richer but relatively poorer. A majority of Americans chose World B — accepting $10,000 less absolute income for the satisfaction of being relatively wealthier. Relative income matters for utility. Frank argues that in complex social environments, relative wealth is how we evaluate our own performance.

N152 [SHOULD KNOW] Can inequality become counterproductive? Possibly: the poor may become disenfranchised and reject institutions (property rights, rule of law). The rich may squander resources on frivolous luxuries (doggy birthday cakes) when human capital investment for the poor would yield higher returns. Class warfare may lead to punishing the rich without making the poor better off. Some studies find negative correlation between inequality and growth; others find the opposite.

N153 [MUST KNOW] Poverty is not zero-sum: "Families who live in public housing on the South Side of Chicago are not poor because Bill Gates lives in a big house. They are poor despite the fact that Bill Gates lives in a big house." Gates didn't take their pie or stand in their way. He created enormous wealth that not everyone got to share. There's a crucial distinction between getting rich by stealing crops (zero-sum) and getting rich by growing a bigger food supply to share (positive-sum). In theory, a world where every individual was educated, healthy, and productive would be one where everyone lived comfortably — "there is no theoretical limit to how well we can live or how widely our wealth can be spread."

N154 [SHOULD KNOW] If everyone had Ph.D.s, who passes out towels at the Four Seasons? Probably no one — as populations become more productive, technology substitutes for labor (voice mail, ATMs, washing machines, vending machines, backhoes). Highly skilled individuals have high opportunity costs, making domestic labor expensive. India has far more domestic servants than the US because India has a glut of low-skilled workers with few options. In a hypothetical world where everyone has a Harvard degree and an essential but horrible job (hauling sludge) must be done, the sludge hauler would be one of the best-paid workers — wage bid up until someone leaves a pleasant job to do it.

### CHAPTER 7: Financial Markets (pp. 190-216)

N155 [MUST KNOW] Investing is like dieting — simple rules, hard discipline: The grapefruit and ice cream diet swept Wheelan's college campus — eat grapefruit and ice cream to lose weight. It didn't work. His neighbor, a doctor and university professor, lost money in the tech crash and then started studying chart patterns to predict market moves. Wheelan's reaction: "Grapefruit and ice cream!" When it comes to personal finance, intelligent people abandon good sense faster than you can say "miracle diet." The rules for investing are strikingly simple but require discipline and short-term sacrifice — slow, steady wealth accumulation, not quick windfalls.

N156 [MUST KNOW] Four functions of financial markets: (1) Raising capital — borrowing makes investment possible (college, homes, businesses). Microcredit example: Esther Gelabuzi, a widow in Uganda with 6 children, used a tiny loan ($195 average) to set up a midwifery clinic without electricity; she's since delivered ~1,400 babies charging $6-14 each. Opportunity International made 325,000 micro-loans in 24 developing countries; repayment rate: 96%. (2) Storing/protecting/growing excess capital — the interest rate (r) is the "rental rate" on capital. Sultan of Brunei's billions would lose to inflation under a mattress ($1B in 1970 = $180M today). Harvard's $25B endowment employs ~200 professionals; earned average 16% annual return from 1995-2005 (lost 30% in the financial crisis). (3) Insuring against risk — health, life, auto insurance, plus exotic products like marine insurance (French tanker Limberg rammed by suicide bomber off Yemen in 2002, insurer paid $70M), catastrophe bonds (USAA issued East Coast hurricane bonds paying 12% if no major hurricane), and the World Cup "cancellation bonds" ($260M against terrorism disruption). (4) Speculation.

N157 [SHOULD KNOW] Futures contracts as insurance: Futures lock in a sale price for commodities at a future date. Corn farmers can sell their crop before it's planted — they pay for certainty. Airlines use futures for jet fuel. Starbucks hedges coffee bean prices. Fila should have bought "win insurance" before the 2009 US Open — Kim Clijsters, a 40-1 long shot returning from having a baby, won, and Fila owed ~$300,000 in bonus money.

N158 [SHOULD KNOW] Catastrophe bonds and spreading risk: Wall Street created catastrophe bonds to help insurance companies hedge natural disaster risk. Higher interest rates than regular bonds, BUT if a major hurricane/earthquake hits a specified area, investors lose some or all of their principal (which goes to the insurance company). Spreads catastrophic risk across a broad group of diversified investors — each takes a small hit even if something terrible happens. Same concept applied to terrorism: 2006 World Cup insured via $260M in cancellation bonds.

### CHAPTER 7: Financial Markets (pp. 190-216) — continued

N159 [MUST KNOW] Credit default swaps — from insurance to weapon of mass destruction: A CDS is simply an insurance policy on whether a third party will repay its debt. Legitimate use: you loan $25,000 to your ne'er-do-well brother-in-law and buy a CDS from someone with a more favorable view of his creditworthiness. If he defaults, the counterparty pays you $25,000. But CDSs went wrong because: (1) anyone can buy one, even without owning the underlying debt — that's pure speculation, not hedging; (2) for any single debt, there may be hundreds or thousands of CDS contracts; (3) when your brother-in-law defaults on $25,000, the losses are magnified thousands of times over. AIG was at the heart of the debacle — its Financial Products division made $2.5B in pretax profits in 2005 by selling "underpriced insurance on complex, poorly understood securities." Simon Johnson (former IMF chief economist): the strategy was "picking up nickels in front of a steamroller — profitable in ordinary years, and catastrophic in bad ones." AIG had $400B+ in outstanding insurance; the US government committed ~$180B to rescue it.

N160 [MUST KNOW] Wall Street vs. Las Vegas — positive-sum vs. zero-sum: Las Vegas is pure zero-sum (house wins, you lose) and serves no broader social purpose beyond entertainment. Wall Street is mostly positive-sum: things get built, companies launch, risk is managed. Capital flows to its highest return — not into businesses run by communist officials or friends of the king. But capital markets are imperfect: billions went into failed dot-coms, the housing bubble, toxic assets. Some profitable enterprises are starved for capital because they lack collateral (e.g. poor families wanting to invest in a college education). The financial regulatory system must protect what markets do best while curtailing excesses that "enrich the folks making them before eventually leaving a mess for the rest of us to clean up."

N161 [MUST KNOW] Efficient markets theory — the Lincoln Park brownstone: A brownstone in Lincoln Park (Chicago) normally costs ~$500,000. You find one listed at $250,000 that appears identical. Should you buy it? Three problems: (1) Why would anyone sell a $500K house for $250K? (2) If it's such a sure thing, why hasn't your real estate agent bought it? (3) If it's been advertised in Crain's Chicago Business, why hasn't a bidding war pushed the price back to $500K? There is virtually no chance of finding a $500K brownstone for $250K — because everyone else is looking too. "Yet people assume the stock market works like this all the time." Every time you buy a "hot stock," someone is selling it — half the investors in that stock are trying to get rid of it.

N162 [MUST KNOW] Efficient markets — the grocery store checkout analogy: Picking stocks is like picking the shortest checkout line. Some lines do move faster, and you can see obvious clues (guy with two full carts, coupon lady). But everyone else sees the same clues and acts on them. Even when you dodge the coupon lady, the guy ahead of you forgot to weigh his avocados — "Price check on avocados at register three!" Unpredictable events drive outcomes. MicroStrategy stock fell $140 in one day (62% plunge) when it restated earnings. "It's the things you can't predict that matter." Mantra for concentrated stock positions: "Enron, Enron, Enron. Or Lehman, Lehman, Lehman."

N163 [MUST KNOW] Index funds outperform most active managers: If markets are efficient, a monkey throwing darts at the stock pages should match professional stock pickers. Malkiel noted the monkey should throw a wet towel for diversification. Index funds ARE that towel. Data: slightly fewer than half of actively managed funds beat the S&P 500 in any given year. Over 5 years, 66% beat it. But over 20 years (the relevant timeframe for retirement savers), only 45% beat the index. "55 percent of the mutual funds that claim to have some special stock-picking ability did worse over two decades than a simple index fund, our modern equivalent of a monkey throwing a towel at the stock pages." $10,000 invested in the average active fund in 1973 = $355,091; same amount in an S&P 500 index fund = $364,066.

N164 [SHOULD KNOW] Behavioral finance challenges efficient markets: Behavioral economists document herd behavior, overconfidence, excessive weight on past trends, overreaction to news. If individuals systematically err, markets (collections of individuals) can too — producing bubbles and busts. Neuroeconomics: Carnegie Mellon/Stanford/Iowa study found brain-damaged patients (impaired emotional responses but intact logic) made 13% more money in investment games than controls — because they took more risks when payoffs were high and were less emotional about losses. Robert Shiller (Yale) argued in 2000 (Irrational Exuberance) that stocks were overvalued; five years later, he correctly called the housing bubble.

N165 [SHOULD KNOW] Richard Thaler's behavioral growth fund: Thaler (the cashew guy from Ch. 1) created a mutual fund to exploit human irrationality. It returned 4.5% annually since inception vs. 2.3% for the S&P 500. Even so, even EMT critics agree: (1) market anomalies self-correct as investors exploit them (like the fast checkout line attracting too many people), (2) average investors shouldn't try to beat the market. Andrew Lo and Craig MacKinlay (A Non-Random Walk Down Wall Street) argue experts with supercomputers can find anomalies, but "Surprisingly, Lo and MacKinlay actually agree with Malkiel's advice to the average investor... go ahead and purchase index funds." Warren Buffett and even Thaler himself put most of their retirement savings in index funds.

N166 [MUST KNOW] Four rules of investing — the economics "sniff test": (1) Save. Invest. Repeat. — Capital is scarce; the only way to generate it is to spend less than you earn. Compound interest is "the greatest invention of all time" (attributed to Einstein). "The cost of living better in the present is living less well in the future." (2) Take risk, earn reward — riskier investments must offer higher expected returns to attract capital. Government bond at 6% vs. neighbor Lance's sunflower combustion engine at 6%? Buy the bond. Stocks averaged 12.9% (1945-1997) but lost 26.5% in their worst year; bonds averaged 5.8% but never lost more than 5%. WSJ headline: "Bonds Let You Sleep at Night but at a Price." Harvard/Yale endowments can tolerate huge losses because their time horizon is infinite. (3) Diversify — coin flip exercise: one coin = 50% chance of losing everything; spread across 10 coins = same expected return with near-zero chance of total loss. But assets must be truly independent — Microsoft and Intel aren't. The MBS crisis: bundling thousands of mortgages was supposed to be like flipping 100 coins, but when the bubble popped, all the coins came up tails simultaneously. (4) Invest for the long run — the casino always wins over time; so does a diversified portfolio with positive expected return. Day trading is idiotic — all the costs of trading, none of the benefits of long-term holding.

N167 [SHOULD KNOW] The sniff test for investment advice: When an adviser promises 20-40% returns, one of three things must be true: (1) it's very risky (think Harvard endowment), (2) your adviser has discovered something all the world's sophisticated investors missed — please call Wheelan, or (3) your adviser is incompetent and/or dishonest — think Bernie Madoff. "All too often the answer is (3)."

### CHAPTER 8: The Power of Organized Interests (pp. 217-233)

N168 [MUST KNOW] The mohair subsidy — poster child for pork-barrel politics: Federal government paid cash to mohair farmers for nearly 40 years. Created in 1955 for military uniforms (mohair = Angora goat wool substitute). But the military switched to synthetic fibers around 1960 — and the subsidy continued for another 35 years. Eventually eliminated when it became too absurd to defend. Then it came back — 2008 farm bill includes wool and mohair subsidies for 2008-2012. "The process that has generated decades of cash payments for mohair farmers is alive and well."

N169 [MUST KNOW] Gary Becker's economics of regulation: "When it comes to interest group politics, it pays to be small." Small, well-organized groups are most successful in the political process because the costs of whatever favors they extract are spread over a large, unorganized population. 1,000 mohair farmers each getting $100K care intensely about the subsidy; 300 million taxpayers each paying pennies don't care at all. Any politician can calculate that supporting the subsidy earns strong support from farmers while costing nothing with other voters.

N170 [MUST KNOW] The ethanol subsidy as textbook rent-seeking: Gasoline blended with ethanol is taxed 5.4 cents less per gallon. GAO (1997) found ethanol had little effect on environment or foreign oil dependence, cost Treasury $7.1B in forgone revenue, and may actually worsen some air pollution (evaporates faster, contributes to ozone). But ethanol is crucial to Iowa corn farmers, and Iowa is crucial to presidential races. Every mainstream presidential candidate except John McCain has supported the ethanol subsidy. Bill Bradley opposed it as NJ senator but "suddenly found it in his heart" to support tax breaks for ethanol when he arrived in Iowa as a presidential candidate. Science is irrelevant — "Regardless of whether ethanol is a great fuel for cars, it certainly works wonders in Iowa campaigns."

N171 [MUST KNOW] Farm subsidy paradox across countries: In countries where farmers are a small minority (US, Europe), government provides large agricultural subsidies. In countries where farmers are a large portion of the population (China, India), farmers are forced to sell crops below market price so urban dwellers get cheap food. Both cases: the large group subsidizes the small group. Logically consistent with Becker's theory.

N172 [MUST KNOW] "Death by a thousand subsidies": Each individual subsidy (mohair, ethanol, sugar protection, pharma tax breaks, dairy price supports) is insignificant in a $14T economy. But collectively, "tens of thousands of others like them are significant. Little inefficiencies begin to disrupt the most basic function of a market economy." Best definition of a tax shelter: "some kind of investment or behavior that would not make sense in the absence of tax considerations." Government shouldn't incentivize people to do things that wouldn't otherwise make sense.

N173 [MUST KNOW] George Stigler's regulatory capture theory (Nobel Prize 1982): Firms and industries often benefit from regulation — they use the political process to generate regulation that helps them or hobbles competitors. Teacher certification example: unions always support more rigorous requirements for new teachers but exempt current teachers. Private school teachers with decades of experience can't teach in public schools. Albert Einstein couldn't legally teach high school physics in Princeton. Hamilton Project study (150,000 students, 3 years): (1) good teachers matter hugely (top quarter = 10 percentile points above bottom quarter), (2) certification has zero correlation with classroom performance. "Groups seek to get themselves licensed."

N174 [SHOULD KNOW] Occupational licensure paradox: Illinois requires barbers and manicurists to be licensed but not electricians. A bad electrical job could burn down a neighborhood; a bad haircut is relatively benign. The best predictor of whether a profession is licensed is the size and budget of its professional association, not the danger to the public. Political organization is a better predictor of licensure than liability premium.

N175 [MUST KNOW] Creative destruction vs. status quo: Joseph Schumpeter coined "creative destruction" — capitalism incessantly destroys old structures and creates new ones. Good for the world, bad for those in the old structure. Those standing in capitalism's path use politics to block progress. Roger Ferguson Jr. (Fed vice chairman): "Policymakers who fail to appreciate the relationship between the relentless churning of the competitive environment and wealth creation will end up focusing their efforts on methods and skills that are in decline... protecting weak, outdated technologies, and in the end, they slow the economy's march forward."

N176 [MUST KNOW] Safety net vs. stopping progress — the telegraph/Pony Express distinction: It's one thing to retrain displaced Pony Express workers as telegraph operators (safety net); it's quite another to ban the telegraph to protect them (blocking progress). NAFTA included compensation for workers whose jobs moved to Mexico. Tobacco settlement money compensates tobacco farmers. But sometimes politics does the equivalent of banning the telegraph. Benefits of competition are huge but spread over a large group; costs are smaller but concentrated — so losers chain themselves to the congressman's door.

N177 [SHOULD KNOW] "We are ALL the special interests": Wheelan's townhouse association fought CTA expansion that would move train tracks 30 feet closer to their homes — good for Chicago transit, bad for their neighborhood. He ran for Congress in 2009 (IL-5 special election, 20+ candidates). At candidates' forum, all excoriated earmarks — until moderator asked "So you'd oppose an earmark for Children's Memorial Hospital?" (300 yards away). Every candidate backpedaled. "Everyone despises earmarks, except for their own."

N178 [SHOULD KNOW] Bob Kerrey (former senator) on campaign finance reform: "The most important corruption that happens in politics doesn't go away even if you had full public financing of campaigns. And that is: I don't want to tell you something that's going to make you not like me... If I had a choice between getting a round of applause by delivering a twenty-six-second applause line and getting a round of boos by telling you the truth, I'd rather get the round of applause." It's not just money — it's intensity of caring. 2% who care deeply outweigh 98% who are mildly opposed.

### CHAPTER 9: Keeping Score (pp. 234-260)

N179 [SHOULD KNOW] Bush/Quayle joke as economic time capsule: Late 1980s, Bush in coma for 9 months, wakes up, asks Quayle about economy. Unemployment 4%, inflation under control. "How much does a loaf of bread cost?" "About 240 yen." Joke captured anxiety about Japanese economic domination — which never materialized. Japan suffered a decade of stagnation; US entered its longest expansion. Nikkei peaked at 38,916 in late 1989, was still around 10,000 twenty years later.

### CHAPTER 9: Keeping Score (pp. 234-260) — continued

N180 [MUST KNOW] GDP basics: Total value of all goods and services produced in an economy. Real GDP = adjusted for inflation (what matters). Nominal GDP = not adjusted (misleading — 10% GDP growth with 10% inflation means zero real gain, "the economic equivalent of swapping a $10 bill for ten $1 bills"). GDP per capita = GDP divided by population. India GDP $3.3T vs Israel $201B, but Israel far richer — GDP per capita $28,300 vs $2,900. US GDP ~$14T (2008), per capita ~$47,000.

N181 [MUST KNOW] Dallas Fed "work time" measure of progress: How long must the average worker work to buy basic goods? Stockings: 1900 = 1 hour 41 minutes, 2000 = 18 minutes. Chicken (3 lbs): 1919 = 2 hours 37 minutes, 2009 = 13 minutes. Cell phone: mid-1980s = 456 hours of work, now = 9 hours (98% drop). "Making money takes time, so when we shop, we're really spending time. The real cost of living isn't measured in dollars and cents but in the hours and minutes we must work to live."

N182 [SHOULD KNOW] Robert Lucas Jr. (Nobel 1995): sustained growth in living standards is only a few centuries old. GDP per capita in Europe between 500 and 1500 was essentially zero growth. "They don't call it the Dark Ages for nothing."

N183 [MUST KNOW] India leprosy example — what low GDP per capita means in human terms: India has 100,000+ cases of Hansen's disease (leprosy). It's easily cured — $3 for a mild case, $20 for severe. WHO provides drugs free, but India's health infrastructure can't identify and reach the afflicted. "More than 100,000 people in India are horribly disfigured by a disease that costs $3 to cure. That is what it means to have a per capita GDP of $2,900."

N184 [MUST KNOW] GDP limitations: (1) Doesn't count unpaid work (cooking, childcare, housework). Outsourcing these to restaurants/daycare/cleaning services adds to GDP but doesn't necessarily increase welfare. (2) Doesn't account for environmental degradation — clear-cutting a forest counts as GDP but the lost forest doesn't subtract. China's "Green GDP": 10% growth in 2004 was really ~7% after subtracting $64B in pollution costs. 16 of world's 25 most polluted cities are in China. (3) No value judgments — prison construction and disaster cleanup add to GDP. Leisure counts for nothing; spending a day in the park may subtract from GDP. (4) Ignores income distribution — GDP per capita can look great even if a small minority is fabulously rich while most citizens get steadily poorer. (5) GDP ≠ happiness — from 1970-1999, rising real incomes coincided with fewer Americans describing themselves as "very happy" (36% → 29%).

N185 [SHOULD KNOW] Happiness economics: David Blanchflower/Andrew Oswald found a lasting marriage is worth $100,000/year in happiness equivalent. Daily journals show "intimate relations" highest-happiness activity; morning commute is lowest. The Economist: "economic arbiters of taste recommend 'experiences' over commodities, pastimes over knick-knacks, doing over having." Long commutes may not be worth the bigger house and higher salary.

N186 [SHOULD KNOW] Alternative measures to GDP: Marc Miringhoff's social health index (16 indicators including child poverty, crime, infant mortality). William Bennett's "index of leading cultural indicators" (out-of-wedlock births, divorce, drug use, church participation). Sarkozy commissioned Stiglitz and Amartya Sen to develop a measure incorporating art, leisure, and environmental destruction. WSJ: "Chapeaus off... if they can put a number on such spiritual matters — but don't hold your breath."

N187 [MUST KNOW] Business cycles and recessions: Recessions stem from economic shocks — stock/property bubble collapse (US 1929/2007, Japan 1989), oil price spikes (1973), Fed tightening (1980/1990), commodity price crashes (developing countries). Each recession is different enough to be hard to prevent. Recessions spread: coffee price crash of 2001 devastated Central America; 2008 financial crisis spread globally — US GDP contracted 5.4% annualized in Q4 2008, Singapore fell 16%, Japan 12%.

N188 [MUST KNOW] Self-fulfilling expectations in recessions: "If we all believe the economy is likely to get worse, then it will get worse." FDR's "nothing to fear but fear itself" was good economics. Giuliani urging New Yorkers to go holiday shopping after 9/11 wasn't wacky — spending generates confidence that causes recovery. The paradox of thrift: individual caution is rational but collectively makes things worse.

N189 [MUST KNOW] 2008 Great Recession mechanics: Housing bubble burst → overleveraged households couldn't pay mortgages or sell homes → foreclosures → banks stuck with bad loans → credit freeze → Wall Street loaded with MBS/derivatives tied to housing → Lehman Brothers collapse (Sept 2008) → cascade panic. Christina Romer: US household wealth fell 17% between Dec 2007 and Dec 2008 — five times the decline of 1929 (when fewer families owned stocks/houses). Geithner anecdote: "titan of the financial system" called saying he was fine "but his voice was quavering." Geithner called back: "Don't call anyone else... you'll scare the shit out of them."

N190 [MUST KNOW] Fiscal vs monetary policy for recessions: Fiscal policy = government spending + tax cuts (Keynesian). Obama's American Recovery and Reinvestment Act 2009: $500B spending + $300B tax cuts. Problem: fiscal policy is made in Congress, not theory — three requirements rarely met: (1) agree on appropriate remedy, (2) pass it timely, (3) remedy kicks in fast. "In most postwar recessions, Congress did not pass legislation in response to the downturn until after it had ended." May 1977: Congress still passing recovery legislation for recession that ended March 1975. Monetary policy (Fed adjusting interest rates) is faster but has limits — in 2007 Fed pushed rates to near zero but consumers/businesses still wouldn't borrow ("pushing on a wet noodle" per Keynes).

N191 [MUST KNOW] Key economic indicators beyond GDP: (1) Unemployment — fraction of workers who want work but can't find it (excludes retired, "discouraged workers" who gave up). Below 4% in 1990s boom, climbed over 10% in Great Recession. (2) Okun's Law — GDP growth of 3% keeps unemployment unchanged; each 1% above/below moves unemployment by half a point. (3) Poverty rate — ~13% of Americans, no better than 1970s. 1 in 5 children poor, 35% of black children. Only success: elderly poverty fell from 30% to below 10% (Social Security). (4) Gini coefficient — income inequality measure (0 = perfect equality, 100 = one person has everything). US: 45 (2007), France: 28, Sweden: 23, Brazil: 57. US has grown more unequal (36.5 in 1980 → 37.9 in 1950). (5) Government size — US government spending ~30% of GDP (low by developed world standards; UK ~40%, Japan 45%, France/Sweden 50%+). US is only developed country where government doesn't pay for bulk of healthcare. (6) Budget deficit/surplus — deficits OK during recessions (Hoover's attempt to balance budget during Depression was "one of the great fiscal follies"). National debt climbed from 33% of GDP (2001) to projected 68% by 2019. Crowding out: government borrowing leaves less capital for private investment. (7) Current account deficit — US at ~$700B (2008). Not inherently bad (early America ran deficits to build industrial capacity) but dangerous if imports aren't funding productive investment.


N192 [MUST KNOW] National savings crisis: US personal savings rate fell from 9%+ (1960s-70s) to 6% (1980s) to below 5% (mid-1990s) to roughly zero by end of 1990s. Savings finance investment, and investment makes society more productive. If Americans don't save, we must borrow from abroad — at a cost. "Any country with significant exposure to foreign lenders must always worry that when times get tough, the herd of international investors will get spooked and flee with their capital."

N193 [MUST KNOW] Social Security as pyramid scheme: Pay-as-you-go system — current workers pay for current retirees. Not invested anywhere. "Straight from young Peter to old Paul." Works as long as there are enough workers on the bottom. 1960: 5 workers per retiree. Now: 3. By 2032: only 2. Fix is economically simple but politically impossible (Ch 8): raise payroll tax, raise retirement age, cut benefits, or increase worker productivity. "If you think any of these solutions would be politically palatable, please go back and read Chapter 8 again."

N194 [SHOULD KNOW] Are recessions getting milder? US has had 11 recessions since WWII, none close to the Great Depression (1929-33: GDP fell 30%, unemployment hit 25%). Prior to the Depression, financial panics were far worse than today. "We haven't made the economic bumps go away, but they are smaller bumps." Bernanke (Depression scholar) and Romer (academic economist) at the helm during 2008 crisis avoided the worst 1930s mistakes (raising interest rates during a depression, raising taxes).

### CHAPTER 10: The Federal Reserve (pp. 261-285)

N195 [MUST KNOW] The Fed's 9/11 response: Hours after the attacks, the Fed issued a two-sentence statement: "The Federal Reserve System is open and operating. The discount window is available to meet liquidity needs." Those terse, technical words calmed global markets. The following Monday, the Fed cut interest rates by 0.5%. The Fed has "tools with more direct impact on the global economy than any other institution in the world, public or private."

N196 [MUST KNOW] Paul Krugman on Fed power: "If you want a simple model for predicting the unemployment rate in the United States over the next few years, here it is: It will be what Greenspan wants it to be, plus or minus a random error reflecting the fact that he is not quite God." Now true of Bernanke. Fed chairman doesn't manage by committee — he sits at the head of the table when the FOMC makes interest rate decisions.

N197 [MUST KNOW] How the Fed controls the economy — the speed limit analogy: Too much easy money (low rates) → excess demand → can't produce enough → inflation. The economy has a "speed limit" (~3% GDP growth). If the Fed pushes growth above this, excess demand causes prices to rise. William McChesney Martin Jr. (1950s-60s Fed chair): "The Fed's job is to take away the punch bowl just as the party gets going."

N198 [MUST KNOW] Paul Volcker's inflation cure: Inflation climbed from 3% (1972) to 13.5% (1980). Volcker "hit the monetary brakes" — short-term rates peaked above 16% in 1981. Result: severe recession of 1981-82 (GDP shrank 3%, unemployment near 10%). But it worked — by 1983, inflation fell to 3%. "Obviously it would have been easier and less painful if the party had never gone out of control in the first place."

N199 [MUST KNOW] Fed mechanics — how money is actually created: (1) Discount rate = rate at which banks borrow directly from the Fed (carries stigma — like borrowing from parents at 25). (2) Federal funds rate = rate banks charge each other for overnight loans. FOMC sets a target and manipulates money supply to achieve it. Think of money supply as furnace, fed funds rate as thermostat. (3) Open market operations = the actual mechanism. Fed buys government bonds from banks, paying with newly created money. Bank swaps bonds (inert) for cash (lendable). $100M injection can multiply to $1B through the banking system (fractional reserve banking — each bank lends most of what it receives, recipients deposit at other banks, cycle repeats).

N200 [MUST KNOW] Fed challenges — driving with a lagging map: (1) Don't know exact speed limit (is it 3% or higher due to tech productivity?). (2) Both accelerator and brake work with a lag — unpredictable delay from weeks to years. (3) Monetary and fiscal policy can work at cross-purposes (Fed brakes while Congress accelerates). (4) Unexpected shocks (financial collapse, oil spike). Greenspan's "Goldilocks" reputation unraveled — criticized for keeping rates too low too long, abetting housing/stock bubbles. "Cheap money didn't cause inflation by sending everyone to buy PT Cruisers and Caribbean cruises. Instead we bought stocks and real estate, and those rising asset prices didn't show up in the consumer price index."

N201 [MUST KNOW] Money vs. wealth distinction: Wealth = real things (houses, cars, human capital). Money = merely a medium of exchange facilitating trade. Money serves four functions: (1) medium of exchange, (2) unit of account, (3) portable and durable, (4) store of value (must be scarce). "Salary" comes from Roman soldiers paid in sacks of salt. In prisons where cash is banned, cigarettes became currency; when smoking was banned, cans of mackerel ("macks") became the standard unit — a haircut costs two macks at Lompoc Federal Correctional Complex.

N202 [MUST KNOW] Fiat money and confidence: Since 1971 (off gold standard), US dollars are redeemable for... nothing. "A dollar is a piece of paper whose value derives solely from our confidence that we will be able to use it to buy something we need in the future." India torn rupee phenomenon: shopkeepers, taxi drivers refuse torn notes even though the central bank considers them legal tender. Rational people refuse them because they believe others will refuse them — "our faith in paper currency is predicated on the faith that others place in the same paper."

N203 [MUST KNOW] Hyperinflation destruction: German newspaper: 1/3 of a mark in 1921, 70 million marks two years later. The mark became "so much value that it became cheaper for households to burn them than to use them to buy firewood." Latin America in 1980s: some countries' largest import was paper currency. Belarussian ruble ("the bunny") lost 10% of purchasing power in one week (August 1998). Hyperinflation destroys fixed-rate lending (German mortgage from 1921 could be paid off with fewer marks than a newspaper cost in 1923), makes people rush to spend before money loses value, and eliminates long-term financial planning.

N204 [MUST KNOW] Even moderate inflation is destructive: (1) Erodes wealth held in cash or low-interest accounts — "safe" savings accounts/CDs may lose purchasing power to inflation. "It is a sad irony that unsophisticated investors eschew the 'risky' stock market only to have their principal whittled away through the back door." (2) Hurts those on fixed incomes (retirees). (3) Creates misleading comparisons — Hollywood proclaiming box office "records" without adjusting for inflation (Gone with the Wind ticket: 19 cents). (4) Redistributes wealth from lenders to borrowers (unexpected inflation makes loan repayment cheaper in real terms). Real interest rate = nominal rate minus inflation.

N205 [SHOULD KNOW] Iowa farmer tractor story: Farmer showed Wheelan an old tractor ($7,500 new in 1970) next to a new one ($40,000). "Cost me $40,000. Can you explain that?" Answer: inflation. The new tractor wasn't really more expensive in real terms — the farmer had to do the same amount of work to buy it because the prices of his crops had risen proportionally.

### CHAPTER 10: The Federal Reserve (pp. 261-285) — continued

N206 [MUST KNOW] Inflation uncertainty is worse than inflation itself: If inflation were constant and predictable (say always 10%), everyone could adjust — savings accounts would pay real rate + 10%, salaries would rise 10%/year, loans would incorporate it. But inflation is not predictable. Uncertainty forces lenders to build in a "buffer" — the greater the fear of future inflation, the bigger the buffer, the higher long-term interest rates. One major benefit of 1990s low inflation: lenders became less fearful, long-term rates dropped, making homes/big purchases more affordable. Robert Barro (Harvard): studied ~100 countries over several decades, confirmed significant inflation is associated with slower real GDP growth.

N207 [MUST KNOW] Why governments deliberately create inflation — the "inflation tax": (1) Inflation benefits debtors, governments are the biggest debtors. Government owes huge debt → prints money → inflation erodes the real value of what it owes. (2) "Inflation tax" — can't collect conventional taxes? Print money to pay workers/army. You've taxed citizens indirectly by devaluing the money in their wallets. Continental Congress, both sides of Civil War, Weimar Germany, modern Zimbabwe all used this. (3) Politicians can use loose monetary policy for short-term gain before elections — easy money makes everyone temporarily feel richer. Arthur Burns allegedly did this for Nixon in 1972; Bush family blamed Greenspan for not doing it in 1992.

N208 [MUST KNOW] Central bank independence is crucial: Countries with independent central banks have lower average inflation. Fed governors serve 14-year terms (insulated from political pressure). "We have made a democratic decision to create a relatively undemocratic institution." A central bank's credibility becomes self-fulfilling — if firms believe it won't tolerate inflation, they won't raise prices, and there won't be inflation. Volcker dinner anecdote: he grumbled that Greenspan being seated next to Hillary Clinton at a presidential address sent the wrong message about Fed independence.

N209 [MUST KNOW] Deflation is worse than inflation: Japan's two decades of experience. Falling prices → consumers postpone purchases → firms cut prices further → economic death spiral. Krugman: "Prices are falling because the economy is depressed; now we've just learned that the economy is depressed because prices are falling." Deflation also makes real interest rates high even when nominal rates are zero (borrowing $100 when prices fall 5%/year means paying back more purchasing power). TARP (Bush-era bank bailout) aimed to "recapitalize" banks so they could lend again. Fed's Great Depression mistake: money supply fell 28% as bank failures cascaded; the Fed did nothing (and actually raised rates in 1931 to defend the gold standard).

N210 [MUST KNOW] Bernanke's unconventional response to 2008 crisis: "Helicopter Ben" (had advocated Japan drop money from helicopters). Cut fed funds rate 7 times between Sept 2007 and April 2008. When rates hit zero ("pushing on a wet noodle"), went beyond textbook: urged banks to use discount window anonymously, offered longer-term loans, loaned directly to investment banks (first time ever — Bear Stearns), loaned JPMorgan $30B to acquire Bear Stearns, accepted MBS as collateral. Article 13(3) of Federal Reserve Act: gives Fed authority to lend to anyone — "Ben Bernanke can create $500 and loan it to your grandmother to fix the roof." Christina Romer: "The Federal Reserve's creative and aggressive actions last fall to maintain lending will go down as a high point in central bank history."

### CHAPTER 11: International Economics (pp. 286-312)

N211 [MUST KNOW] George Soros breaks the Bank of England (1992): Soros bet ~$10B that the British pound would fall. Britain was in the European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM), pound pegged to 2.95 German marks. Britain was in recession, pound falling as investors fled. Government had two tools to defend the peg: (1) spend foreign currency reserves buying pounds, (2) raise interest rates to attract capital. Both were unattractive — reserves were dwindling, and raising rates during a recession makes for "bad economics and even worse politics." Soros called the government's bluff. On "Black Wednesday" (Sept 16, 1992), Britain withdrew from the ERM, pound fell 10%+. Soros made ~$1 billion in one day.

N212 [MUST KNOW] Purchasing power parity (PPP): Exchange rates should theoretically reflect what the same basket of goods costs in different countries. If $25 buys a basket in the US and 750 rubles buys the same basket in Russia, $1 should be worth 30 rubles. But official exchange rates deviate widely from PPP because 75% of goods/services are nontradable (haircuts, rent, babysitting) — you can arbitrage TV prices across borders but not haircut prices. The Economist's Big Mac Index: Big Mac costs $3.57 in US and 12.5 renminbi in China → PPP rate should be 3.5 renminbi per dollar, but official rate was 6.83 → renminbi massively undervalued. China deliberately keeps currency cheap to boost exports.

N213 [MUST KNOW] Weak vs. strong currency trade-offs: Weak dollar = good for US exporters (Ford Taurus cheaper for Parisians), bad for US consumers (imported Toyota costs more). Strong dollar = opposite. Neither is inherently good or bad. A government deliberately keeping its currency undervalued is "essentially taxing consumers of imports and subsidizing producers of exports." NYT (1992, weak dollar): "The declining dollar has turned the world's wealthiest economy into the Filene's Basement of industrial countries." WSJ (2001, strong dollar): "G.M. Official Says Dollar Is Too Strong for U.S. Companies." Toyota: each 1-yen appreciation against the dollar costs $450M in annual operating earnings.

N214 [MUST KNOW] What drives exchange rates: (1) Economic growth attracts capital (investors must buy local currency) → currency appreciates. (2) Strong exports create demand for the exporting country's currency. (3) Higher interest rates attract capital-seeking return → currency appreciates. (4) Safe-haven effects — US treasuries attracted capital during 2008 crisis, strengthening dollar despite US being ground zero for the crisis. Economists are terrible at predicting exchange rate movements because too many forces act simultaneously. Government intervention (buying/selling own currency) is like "warming up a cold bathtub with one spoonful of hot water" — forex market trades trillions daily.

N215 [SHOULD KNOW] Wheelan's Guangzhou currency trading lesson: Arrived in China 1989, used outdated guidebook for black market exchange rate, made what he thought was a hardball opening offer — trader accepted instantly. The Chinese currency had been losing value since the guide was published. His $100 bought the Chinese equivalent of about $13.50.

### CHAPTER 11: International Economics (pp. 286-312) — continued

N216 [MUST KNOW] Exchange rate regimes: (1) Gold standard — simplest, currencies backed by gold. Advantage: stable exchange rates, no manipulation. Disadvantage: ties monetary policy to gold supply, can't respond to recessions. Robert Mundell argued bungled gold-standard monetary policy in 1920s-30s caused the Great Depression, Nazi revolution, and WWII. (2) Fixed/pegged exchange rate — government declares its currency worth X relative to another (e.g. dollar). Must defend the peg by buying/selling currency or adjusting interest rates. Works until the market doesn't believe the peg anymore (Soros vs. Britain). (3) Floating exchange rate — market determines value. Most major currencies float. Advantage: automatic adjustment (recession → weak currency → cheaper exports → recovery). Disadvantage: volatility makes international business harder. (4) Common currency (euro) — eliminates exchange rate uncertainty within the zone, but surrenders independent monetary policy. One-size-fits-all interest rates can be too high for struggling economies and too low for booming ones.

N217 [MUST KNOW] The euro's fundamental problem: Countries that share a currency can't use monetary policy to address country-specific problems. Greece can't devalue its currency to make exports cheaper and stimulate recovery — it's stuck with the same interest rate as Germany. Ireland's Celtic Tiger boom was partly fueled by interest rates that were appropriate for the broader eurozone but too low for Ireland's overheating economy. When crisis hit, Ireland couldn't devalue. The US works as a common currency area because: (1) labor moves easily between states, (2) federal fiscal transfers cushion regional shocks (struggling states get more federal spending), (3) cultural/linguistic unity. Europe has none of these advantages.

N218 [MUST KNOW] Iceland's spectacular collapse: Iceland (population: 300,000) had GDP per capita among the world's highest. Its three biggest banks had combined assets 10x the nation's GDP. When the financial crisis hit, all three collapsed simultaneously. The government couldn't bail them out — the banks were simply too big relative to the economy. The currency (krona) collapsed. McDonalds pulled out of the country because importing food ingredients became too expensive. The IMF stepped in with a rescue package. Iceland went from poster child of prosperity to cautionary tale virtually overnight.

N219 [MUST KNOW] Capital flight and the "herd of international investors": When investors lose confidence in a country, they sell the local currency and flee — creating a self-reinforcing crisis. Currency drops → imports get more expensive → inflation rises → economy weakens → more investors flee. Asian financial crisis (1997): Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea — capital flowed in during boom years, then fled suddenly when confidence cracked. The IMF's role: lender of last resort for countries (as the Fed is for banks), providing emergency loans with conditions (austerity, reforms). Controversial because IMF conditions can deepen short-term pain.

N220 [SHOULD KNOW] China's deliberate currency undervaluation: China keeps the renminbi artificially cheap by accumulating massive dollar reserves. This makes Chinese exports cheaper globally, fueling export-led growth. But it also means Chinese consumers pay more for imports, and it creates trade tensions with the US. China has accumulated trillions in US treasury bonds — making America and China financially interdependent in ways that constrain both countries' policy options.

### CHAPTER 12: Trade and Globalization (pp. 313-337)

N221 [MUST KNOW] Comparative advantage — the most important concept in international trade: Even if one country is better at making everything, both countries benefit from trade if each specializes in what it does relatively best. Wheelan's example: he could be a better typist and a better writer than his assistant, but it still makes sense to hire the assistant to type because Wheelan's comparative advantage is in writing. The time he'd spend typing is time not spent writing, which is far more valuable. Same logic applies to countries.

N222 [MUST KNOW] Trade creates losers, but the gains exceed the losses: When a textile factory closes because of cheaper imports, those workers suffer real pain. But consumers (including poor ones) benefit from cheaper clothes. The correct response is not to block trade (which makes everyone poorer on average) but to compensate the losers — through retraining, transition aid, education. "Both politics and compassion suggest that we ought to offer a hand to those mowed over by competition."

N223 [MUST KNOW] The sweatshop argument revisited (expanded): Sweatshops are symptoms of poverty, not causes. Closing them doesn't create better alternatives — it eliminates the best option poor workers currently have. Historical evidence: South Korea, Taiwan, Japan all went through sweatshop phases on their way to prosperity. Life magazine story about Pakistani children sewing soccer balls led to outrage and boycotts. Result: children were fired from the factory and many ended up in worse situations (some in more dangerous work). Nike pays Vietnamese workers ~$600/year — twice the average Vietnamese income. The real question is not "are sweatshops good?" but "compared to what?"

N224 [MUST KNOW] Trade protectionism hurts the poor most: Tariffs and quotas raise the price of imported goods. Since the poor spend a larger share of their income on basic goods (clothing, food), protectionism is effectively a regressive tax. Sugar quotas in the US keep American sugar prices at roughly double the world price — benefiting a small number of sugar producers at the expense of every American consumer and every business that uses sugar.

N225 [SHOULD KNOW] The "race to the bottom" argument is wrong: Critics claim globalization forces countries to lower environmental/labor standards to attract business. Evidence doesn't support this — countries with strong institutions and good governance attract more investment, not less. The race is actually to the top: countries that invest in education, rule of law, and infrastructure gain competitive advantage.

N226 [MUST KNOW] Trade and the environment: Wealthy countries have better environmental standards because environmental protection is a luxury good (Ch 1). As trade makes countries richer, they invest more in environmental protection. The relationship isn't linear — there's a "hump" where early industrialization increases pollution, but continued growth eventually leads to cleaner practices (the Environmental Kuznets Curve). Blocking trade keeps countries poor, which is worse for the environment in the long run.

### CHAPTER 13: Development Economics (pp. 338-361)

N227 [MUST KNOW] The wealth gap is staggering and growing: The richest 1% of the world's population earns as much as the bottom 57%. In 1820, the ratio of per capita income between the richest and poorest countries was about 3:1; by 1992, it was 72:1. Some countries have gotten dramatically richer (South Korea, Chile, Botswana); others have gotten poorer (much of sub-Saharan Africa, North Korea). The question of why some nations are rich and others poor is "the most important question in economics."

N228 [MUST KNOW] Institutions matter more than natural resources: Botswana (diamonds, good governance) vs. Sierra Leone (diamonds, corruption and civil war). Resource-rich countries often suffer the "resource curse" — natural wealth creates incentives for corruption, civil conflict, and neglect of other economic sectors. Countries with good institutions (property rights, rule of law, honest government, functioning courts) grow; those without them don't, regardless of natural resources.

N229 [MUST KNOW] Property rights are foundational: Hernando de Soto's research — the world's poor hold trillions in assets (homes, businesses) that they can't leverage because they lack legal title. Without clear property rights, you can't use your house as collateral for a loan, sell it legally, or invest in improvements with confidence. In Egypt, de Soto found that the assets held by the poor (but without title) were worth 55 times all foreign investment ever recorded in the country.

N230 [MUST KNOW] Geography matters but isn't destiny: Tropical countries face disadvantages (disease burden, soil quality, climate extremes). Jeffrey Sachs argues that geographic factors explain much of the development gap and that massive foreign aid is needed to overcome these "poverty traps." Others (William Easterly) argue that institutions and incentives matter more, and that foreign aid often does more harm than good by propping up corrupt governments and distorting local markets.

N231 [MUST KNOW] Foreign aid debate: Sachs (Columbia) advocates a "big push" — massive investment to break the poverty trap. Easterly (NYU) argues aid has been largely ineffective ($2.3 trillion spent over 50 years with disappointing results) because it doesn't address the root causes (bad governance, perverse incentives). Dambisa Moyo (Dead Aid) argues aid to Africa has been "an unmitigated political, economic, and humanitarian disaster." The truth is probably somewhere in between — targeted aid (vaccines, mosquito nets, clean water) can work; big untargeted transfers to corrupt governments often don't.

N232 [SHOULD KNOW] The "Kill Bill" problem of development: Governments in poor countries often have strong incentives to pursue policies that keep them in power rather than policies that promote growth. Kleptocrats steal foreign aid. Dictators prevent the kind of creative destruction that generates growth because it threatens their power base. Development requires not just money but functioning institutions, which are exactly what poor countries lack.

### EPILOGUE: Life in 2050 — Seven Questions (pp. 362-370)

N233 [SHOULD KNOW] Wheelan poses seven unresolved economic questions for 2050: (1) Will we take care of the global environment? Climate change is the ultimate externality — costs are global and deferred, making political action extremely difficult. (2) Will the global economy be more integrated or less? Despite the benefits of trade, backlash against globalization is real and politically powerful. (3) Will income inequality get worse? Human capital premium is rising; those without education are falling further behind. (4) Will the fiscal time bomb go off? US national debt, Social Security, Medicare — the math doesn't add up without painful adjustments. (5) Will the developing world catch up? China and India are growing rapidly, but sub-Saharan Africa continues to fall behind. (6) Will we confront the problems caused by our success? Obesity, congestion, environmental degradation — all byproducts of wealth. (7) Will we be happier? Economic growth hasn't clearly made us happier; perhaps we need to rethink what we're optimizing for.

N234 [MUST KNOW] Wheelan's closing: Economics provides a framework for thinking about these questions, even if it doesn't provide definitive answers. The most important lesson of the book: "Economics is like gravity: Ignore it and you will be in for some rude surprises." Understanding economic principles helps citizens make better decisions and hold politicians accountable. The ideas are too important and too interesting to be left only to economists.

### CHAPTER 12: Trade and Globalization (pp. 313-337) — gap fill

N235 [MUST KNOW] Nicholas Kristof/Sheryl WuDunn (NYT) "Two Cheers for Sweatshops": They reported from Asia that the worst thing that can happen to sweatshop workers is for their factory to close. In Phnom Penh, they found children scavenging in dumps looking for recyclable material — "dangerous, dirty, and sometimes fatal" work. One Cambodian teenager told them her dream was to get a job in a garment factory. "I'd love to get a job in a factory. At least that way, I wouldn't have to work in the dump." The standard of comparison for sweatshops should be the alternatives actually available in developing countries, not Western working conditions.

N236 [MUST KNOW] Outsourcing of services — the new frontier of trade: India doesn't just make physical goods cheaply; Indian radiologists can read X-rays, Indian accountants can do tax returns, Indian programmers can write software — all delivered via the internet. This threatens a whole new category of American workers who previously thought their jobs were safe from foreign competition. The economic logic is the same as manufacturing trade: it makes both countries richer on average while creating concentrated losers.

N237 [SHOULD KNOW] Trade restrictions hurt developing countries more than rich ones: Agricultural subsidies in the US and Europe (especially cotton, sugar) depress world prices, devastating farmers in Africa and Latin America who could otherwise compete. Oxfam estimated that US cotton subsidies cost West African cotton farmers more than the entire US aid budget to Africa. "We give them foreign aid with one hand and take away their livelihoods with the other."

N238 [SHOULD KNOW] Brain drain: The best and brightest from developing countries often emigrate to rich countries where their talents are better rewarded. This deprives poor countries of exactly the human capital they need most. Some economists argue that the possibility of emigration actually incentivizes education (people invest in skills hoping to emigrate, but many don't actually leave — so the country ends up with more educated people overall). The evidence is mixed.

### CHAPTER 13: Development Economics (pp. 338-361) — gap fill

N239 [MUST KNOW] Openness to trade is a key ingredient of growth: Economies that are more open to international trade tend to grow faster. China's explosive growth began after Deng Xiaoping opened the economy in the late 1970s. India's growth accelerated after liberalizing reforms in 1991. Conversely, North Korea (completely closed) and Myanmar (largely closed) have stagnated or declined. However, openness alone isn't sufficient — institutions matter. Russia opened rapidly but lacked the institutional framework (property rights, rule of law) to benefit fully.

N240 [MUST KNOW] Human capital is the ultimate development resource: Countries that invest in education and health see the biggest returns. The East Asian "tigers" (South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Hong Kong) had few natural resources but invested heavily in human capital. South Korea went from poorer than Ghana in 1960 to a wealthy OECD nation. Botswana invested diamond revenues in education and health rather than allowing them to be stolen by elites — the exception that proves the rule in Africa.

N241 [MUST KNOW] Women's education is the single best investment in development: Educated women have fewer children, invest more in each child's health and education, are more productive economically, and are less vulnerable to exploitation. Lawrence Summers (then World Bank chief economist): educating girls yields a higher return than any other investment available in the developing world. The countries with the worst development outcomes are invariably those where women have the least education and fewest rights.

N242 [SHOULD KNOW] The paradox of foreign aid: Aid can create dependency, prop up bad governments, and distort local markets (free food imports can destroy local agriculture). But targeted interventions work: smallpox was eradicated through an international effort, polio is nearly gone, mosquito nets dramatically reduce malaria deaths. The key is whether aid addresses root causes or merely treats symptoms while perpetuating the underlying dysfunction.

N243 [MUST KNOW] Corruption is the single biggest obstacle to development: Transparency International ranks countries by corruption perception. The correlation between corruption and poverty is overwhelming. Corrupt governments steal aid, discourage investment, undermine property rights, and create a culture of rent-seeking rather than wealth creation. Mobutu Sese Seko (Zaire/Congo) reportedly stole $5 billion during his rule while his country became one of the world's poorest.
