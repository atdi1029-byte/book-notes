1. How to Read an Entry

Every entry in this atlas has the same seven parts, and they are in that order for a reason. The name comes first in English with the Portuguese underneath, because the Portuguese is what the Zercher app and your trainer's sheets actually say, and there are movements where the Portuguese name is the only name you have ever seen. The meta line is the fast scan: primary muscle, secondary muscle, movement pattern, how many of the nineteen programs used it, how many sessions you have logged, and your best working weight.

Then the body: what the movement is, and more importantly what makes it different from the three movements next to it that look almost identical. That difference is the whole reason a program picks one over the other. Hits names the muscles and keys them to the little figure beside the entry — red is the muscle doing most of the work, gold is the muscle helping. Purpose is the job the movement does that nothing else in the program does; if you cannot state that, the movement is decoration. Swap with gives ranked substitutions and, crucially, says what changes when you swap, because most swaps are not free. Watch is where it goes wrong.

The frequency pills

Each entry carries a tag for how much of your actual training it has been. CORE means ten or more logged sessions — a movement you genuinely own. REGULAR is three to nine sessions. TRIED is one or two. PRESCRIBED means it appears in a program sheet but never made it into a logged session — either the program predates your logging or you swapped it out every time. That last tag is worth paying attention to: thirty-seven movements carry it.

What each group actually does

Knowing where a muscle sits is half of it. The other half is knowing what it does, because that is what tells you why one exercise trains it and another one does not. Muscles only do one thing: they shorten and pull two bones closer together. Every exercise in this atlas is just a way of loading one of those pulls.

The pectoralis major pulls your upper arm across your chest and forward. That is why a press and a fly both train it even though they look nothing alike — in both cases the arm is travelling toward the midline. It has an upper portion that runs from your collarbone and a larger lower portion that runs from your breastbone, and the upper portion only gets a full share of the work when the arm travels up as well as in, which is the entire reason incline pressing exists as a separate category.

The latissimus dorsi is the big fan across your back and it pulls your upper arm down and back toward your ribs. Pull the bar down from overhead and you train it through that path; row the weight into your stomach and you train it through a different one. The rhomboids and mid trapezius sit between your shoulder blades and squeeze them together, which is a completely different job from the lats and is why a row that stops short of the squeeze trains less than you think. The erector spinae runs either side of your spine and its job is to stop your back from rounding — which means it works hardest on movements where nothing is holding your torso up for you.

The deltoid is three muscles sharing a name. The front head presses overhead and gets heavy involvement in every incline press whether you want it or not. The side head lifts your arm out sideways and is trained almost exclusively by raises, not presses. The rear head pulls your arm backward and is functionally a back muscle that happens to live on your shoulder — it gets trained by rows and reverse flyes, never by pressing. Underneath all of that, the rotator cuff is four small muscles whose job is to keep the ball of your arm centred in the socket while the big muscles move it. They are the reason internal and external rotations appear at the top of sixteen of your nineteen programs.

The biceps bends the elbow and also rotates the palm upward, which is why a curl with a neutral grip trains it differently from a curl with the palm up. It also crosses the shoulder, which is why the position of your upper arm — behind you on an incline curl, in front of you on a preacher curl — changes which part of the range is hardest. The triceps straightens the elbow and its long head also crosses the shoulder, so overhead extensions load a part of it that pushdowns barely touch. That single fact is why your programs consistently pair one overhead triceps movement with one pushdown rather than two of either.

In the lower body the quadriceps straighten the knee, the hamstrings bend the knee and also extend the hip, and the glutes extend the hip. The hamstrings doing two jobs is why leg curls and Romanian deadlifts both count as hamstring work but are not interchangeable — one loads the knee function, the other loads the hip function. The adductors pull your thigh inward and get far more work than people expect from any wide-stance squat or deep lunge. The gastrocnemius points your toes and crosses the knee, so it works best with the leg straight; the soleus beneath it does the same job with the knee bent, which is the whole argument for seated calf raises existing alongside standing ones.

Finally the rectus abdominis curls your ribcage toward your pelvis, the obliques rotate and side-bend your torso, and both of them also work isometrically to stop your spine from extending under load. That last function is what a plank and a dragon flag actually train, and it is a different quality from what a crunch trains, which is why your programs run both.

3. Ten Things Worth Knowing Before the Entries

  1. The movement pattern matters more than the exercise name. A program needs a horizontal press, a vertical pull, a hip hinge and a knee extension. Which specific bar you use to satisfy each of those is mostly negotiable, and that is exactly what makes substitution safe.
  2. Your most-logged movements are not your heaviest ones. Shoulder rotations appear in sixteen of nineteen programs and have eighty-seven logged sessions between them at six pounds. That is not wasted time — it is the cheapest injury insurance in the building.
  3. A machine is not a worse version of a free weight; it is a different tool. The machine holds your torso so the target muscle fails before your stabilisers do. That is a feature at the end of a session and a liability at the start of one, which is why your programs put free weights first almost without exception.
  4. Thirty-seven prescribed movements never made it into a log. Barbell bench press appears in ten programs and has zero logged sessions; Smith bench press appears in one and has ten. You have been quietly running a Smith-machine pressing program for two years. Whether that was a choice is worth knowing.
  5. Swapping the implement is cheap. Swapping the pattern is expensive. Trading a barbell row for a dumbbell row costs you almost nothing. Trading it for a pulldown costs you the horizontal pull entirely, and your mid-back pays for it.
  6. Unilateral work is not just for balance. One arm or one leg at a time lets the working side use a longer range and forces the trunk to resist rotation. It is why a one-arm row and a barbell row are both in the same programs rather than one replacing the other.
  7. The angle is the exercise. Incline, flat and decline pressing are three different movements that happen to share a name, and the program treats them that way. Your own ABC+D blocks went further than your trainer's ever did here, running specific degrees — thirty, thirty-seven, twenty-two.
  8. Isolation movements exist to finish a muscle, not to build it. If a fly is your only chest movement, the chest will not grow much. If a fly comes after two presses, it reaches the fibres the presses left with something in reserve. Position in the session is part of the prescription.
  9. Every exercise has a range where it is hard and a range where it is easy, and they are not the same for movements that look identical. A cable fly is hardest at full stretch; a machine fly is hardest in the middle; a dumbbell fly is nearly free at the top. Pick based on where you want the tension, not on what is available.
  10. If you cannot say what job a movement does that nothing else in the session does, cut it. This is the single most useful test in the atlas and it is the reason the Purpose line exists in every entry below.

Flat presses

Smith Machine Bench Press Supino Reto Smith

Pec major, mid and lower · front delt, triceps · horizontal press · 1 of 19 programs · 10 sessions / 39 working sets · top 150 lbs · CORE

The bar runs on a fixed vertical track, which removes the job of keeping it balanced and hands those reps back to the chest. This is the press you have actually done — ten sessions in ABC+D1 against zero for the free-weight barbell bench across the ten programs that prescribed it. The fixed path also lets you press with your feet up or your torso shifted without the bar wandering, which is why the Smith is the usual home for pause work and pin work.

Hits: The lower and middle pectoral fibres do the bulk of it, with the front deltoid and the triceps sharing the lockout. Because the track is vertical rather than following a natural arc, the front delt takes slightly more than it would on a barbell.

Purpose: Heavy horizontal pressing without a spotter and without spending reps on stabilisation. It is the movement that sets your pressing load for the block, which is why it takes the warm-up sets and the eighty-second rest.

Swap with: Barbell bench press is the closest match and adds the stabiliser demand back — expect to drop ten to fifteen percent. Machine chest press if the shoulder is irritated, since the handles let you pick your own path. Dumbbell bench press if you want the stretch at the bottom, but it costs you top-end load.

Watch: The fixed track will let you press in a line your shoulder does not like. Set the bench so the bar touches the lower chest, not the collarbone, and do not let the elbows flare to ninety degrees at the bottom.

Barbell Bench Press Supino Reto

Pec major, mid and lower · front delt, triceps · horizontal press · 10 of 19 programs · 0 logged sessions · top — · PRESCRIBED

The most prescribed chest movement in your entire history and the one you have never once logged. It appears in ABC01, ABC02, ABC04 and six of the ABCD blocks, always as the first movement of push day, always with two warm-up sets and the longest rest in the session. In ABC02 it became a four-rung pyramid — twelve to fifteen, ten to twelve, eight to ten, five to eight — the first time any program asked you for a set under eight reps.

Hits: Lower and mid pec as the primary, with the front delt and triceps taking a real share. The free bar also loads the rotator cuff and the serratus as stabilisers in a way no machine does.

Purpose: The reference lift. Its job in a program is to be the number that tells you whether the block worked, which is why it always sits first and always gets the warm-ups. Every other chest movement in the session is arranged around it.

Swap with: Smith bench press is what you have actually been doing and it is a legitimate swap — you keep the pattern and the load, you lose the stabilisation. Dumbbell bench press keeps the free-weight demand and adds range. Machine chest press is the last resort; it keeps the pattern and loses most of what makes the lift a reference.

Watch: Elbows tucked to about sixty degrees from the torso rather than flared square. Bar to the lower chest. If the shoulder complains at the bottom, shorten the range before you reduce the weight.

Descending Pin Press Descending Pin Press

Pec major, mid · triceps, front delt · horizontal press, partial range · 1 of 19 programs · 10 sessions / 40 working sets · top 195 lbs · CORE

You press from a dead stop off pins set part-way up, and the pins get lower as the sets progress — so each set starts from a slightly longer range than the one before. Your top load here is a hundred and ninety-five pounds against a hundred and fifty on the full-range Smith press, which is the whole point: a shortened range lets you handle a load your full range cannot yet support, and the dead start removes any help from the stretch at the bottom.

Hits: Mid pec and triceps carry the work, and the triceps share is higher than a normal press because the hardest part of a pin press sits above the sticking point where the arms are already partly extended. Dead-stop starting also loads the front delt hard for the first inch.

Purpose: Overload. This is your ABC+D2 intensification answer to a press that had stopped producing new numbers — same pattern, more weight, less range. The descending element means one exercise covers three different ranges across the session.

Swap with: Board press or a plain pin press at a fixed height if the descending setup is too fiddly. Floor press gives you a similar hard stop with no rack work at all. Paused bench press if what you want is the dead start rather than the overload.

Watch: The dead start is where people jerk the shoulder. Set the tension before the bar leaves the pins and press rather than bounce. Do not chase the descending pins down into a range your shoulder has not earned.

Dumbbell Bench Press Supino Reto com Halteres

Pec major, mid and lower · front delt, triceps · horizontal press · 5 of 19 programs · 1 session / 5 working sets · top 65 lbs · TRIED

Two independent weights, which means each arm travels its own path and the hands can come closer together at the top than a bar allows. That extra few inches of adduction at the top is real chest work that a barbell simply cannot give you. It appears as the fourth chest movement in ABC01 and ABC05 — not the heavy press, but the one that finishes the job the heavy press started.

Hits: Mid and lower pec, with a longer stretch at the bottom than a barbell because nothing stops the hands at chest level. The front delt and triceps assist, and the rotator cuff works hard to control two separate weights.

Purpose: Range and symmetry. In a program that already has a barbell or Smith press, the dumbbell version exists to cover the part of the movement the bar cuts off, and to stop a strong side from carrying a weak one.

Swap with: Flat dumbbell fly if you want even more of the adduction and less of the press. Machine chest press keeps it easy on the shoulders. Hex press — dumbbells pressed together throughout — is the most chest-biased version of this pattern you have logged.

Watch: Getting heavy dumbbells into position is where shoulders get hurt. Sit, place them on your thighs, and kick back one at a time. Do not bring them so low at the bottom that the shoulder rolls forward.

Machine Chest Press Supino Máquina

Pec major, mid · front delt, triceps · horizontal press · 4 of 19 programs · 0 logged sessions · top — · PRESCRIBED

A seated press with a fixed handle path and your back supported. It shows up in ABCD03, ABCD07, ABCD08 and ABCD11, and in every case it sits after a free-weight press rather than before one. That ordering is the whole prescription: the machine is there to take the chest past the point where your stabilisers would have quit first.

Hits: Mid pec almost exclusively, with a modest triceps and front delt share. The back support removes the trunk from the equation entirely, which is exactly why the chest can be driven closer to failure safely.

Purpose: Safe accumulation of pressing volume. When a program wants four chest movements, the fourth cannot be another free-weight press without wrecking the session — so it becomes a machine.

Swap with: Hammer strength press if your gym has one, since the independent arms add the adduction back. Smith bench press if the machine is taken. Push-ups to failure is the zero-equipment version and covers the same “finish the chest” job.

Watch: Set the seat so the handles line up with your mid chest, not your shoulders. Do not let the weight stack slam — the fixed path makes it easy to use momentum and never notice.

Reverse Grip Smith Bench Press Reverse Grip Bench Press Smith Machine

Pec major, upper · front delt, triceps · horizontal press, supinated · 1 of 19 programs · 2 sessions / 6 working sets · top 130 lbs · TRIED

Press a flat bar with your palms facing you. Turning the grip over rotates the upper arm in a way that shifts a surprising amount of the work onto the upper chest — enough that a flat reverse-grip press loads the clavicular fibres about as well as a modest incline does. It appeared in ABCD09 as the second press of the day, right after dumbbell circles and before dips.

Hits: Upper pec more than any other flat press, with the front delt and the long head of the triceps assisting. The supinated grip also brings the biceps in as a stabiliser, which no other press does.

Purpose: Upper-chest loading without an incline bench, and a genuinely different stimulus in a block that was already running an incline press elsewhere. ABCD09 was an accumulation block with only ten percent carry-over — novelty was the point.

Swap with: Incline Smith bench for the same upper-chest target through a more conventional path. Reverse grip barbell bench if you want it free-weight, though unracking it safely needs a spotter. Low-incline press at fifteen to twenty degrees gets you most of the way.

Watch: The grip is the hazard, not the press. Wrists will want to collapse backward under load; keep them stacked. On a Smith this is manageable, with a free bar it is genuinely risky.

Incline presses

Incline Smith Bench Press Supino Inclinado Smith · 30° / 37°

Pec major, upper · front delt, triceps · incline press · 2 of 19 programs · 17 sessions / 59 working sets · top 135 lbs · CORE

Your most-logged chest press, and the one place where your own programs went further than your trainer's ever did: ABC+D1 and ABC+D3 specify the bench angle in degrees rather than just calling it “incline.” That matters more than it sounds. At thirty degrees the upper chest does most of the work; by forty-five the front delt has quietly taken over, and by sixty you are doing a shoulder press with a chest stretch. Thirty to thirty-seven is the window where the clavicular pec is loaded and the delt is still only helping.

Hits: Upper pec as the primary, front delt and triceps assisting, with the mid pec contributing through the bottom half of the range. The fixed bar path keeps the load travelling straight up, which keeps the emphasis honest.

Purpose: Upper-chest thickness, loaded heavily and repeatably. It is the movement that carries your pressing progression in the ABC+D blocks the way barbell bench was meant to in your trainer's.

Swap with: Incline dumbbell press for more range and a harder stabilisation demand, at a lower load. Incline barbell press if you want the free-weight version. Reverse grip flat Smith press reaches similar fibres from a completely different angle if the incline bench is occupied.

Watch: Creeping the bench angle up over a block without noticing. If the front delts are the thing that fatigues first, the bench is too steep. Keep the bar touching high on the chest, not on the throat.

Incline Dumbbell Press Supino Inclinado com Halteres

Pec major, upper · front delt, triceps · incline press · 14 of 19 programs · 11 sessions / 45 working sets · top 80 lbs · CORE

The single most-prescribed chest movement across your whole history — fourteen of nineteen programs — and it survived every transition your trainer made. It is in ABC03 and ABC04, in all eight ABCD blocks from 01 to 08, in ABCD10 and ABCD11, and in your own ABC+D2 and ABC+D3. When a coach keeps one movement through four different training philosophies, that is a vote of confidence.

Hits: Upper pec primary, with a meaningfully larger front-delt share than the Smith version because the dumbbells let the arms travel inward at the top. The stretch at the bottom is deeper than any barred press can give you.

Purpose: Upper chest with range. In programs that already have a heavy barred press, this is the second press — the one that covers the adduction and the deep stretch the bar cuts off. In ABC03's specialization block it was the third incline movement in a row, the point at which the block openly admitted it was an upper-chest specialisation.

Swap with: Incline Smith press for more load and less range. Neutral grip incline dumbbell press if the shoulder complains — palms facing each other is the friendliest version of this movement. Incline dumbbell fly if what you want is purely the adduction.

Watch: Bringing the dumbbells together hard at the top costs you nothing and gains you the best part of the rep, but clanging them together turns it into a rest. Control the bottom; this is the position where an incline press hurts a shoulder.

Incline Barbell Press Supino Inclinado

Pec major, upper · front delt, triceps · incline press · 4 of 19 programs · 1 session / 3 working sets · top 120 lbs · TRIED

The free-weight incline. In ABC03 it led push day at four sets of eight to ten — the first time a program put an incline movement first rather than treating it as the follow-up to a flat press. That reordering was the clearest signal in the whole ABC series that the block was specialising the upper chest.

Hits: Upper pec primary, front delt and triceps assisting, with the stabilisers working harder than on a Smith.

Purpose: Heavy upper-chest pressing that also counts as a reference lift. When it leads the day, the program is telling you the upper chest is the priority for the block.

Swap with: Incline Smith press for the same path with less balance cost. Incline dumbbell press for range. Low-incline guillotine press if you want maximum upper pec and are willing to go very light.

Watch: Unracking an incline barbell is awkward and is where shoulders get tweaked. Get help or use a rack with the pins set high.

Incline Machine Press Supino Máquina Inclinado

Pec major, upper · front delt, triceps · incline press · 3 of 19 programs · 0 logged sessions · top — · PRESCRIBED

The second movement of push day in both ABC01 and ABC02, sitting directly behind barbell bench at four sets of eight to twelve with eighty seconds rest. Its job in that slot is to keep pressing the upper chest after the free-weight lift has already used up your stability.

Hits: Upper pec, with the back support removing the trunk entirely and the fixed path keeping the front delt's share lower than a free incline would.

Purpose: Upper-chest volume that costs the nervous system almost nothing, immediately after a lift that cost it a lot.

Swap with: Incline Smith press is the nearest thing with a fixed path. Incline dumbbell press if you will accept the extra stabilisation. Incline seated cable fly if the goal is just more upper-chest work without more pressing.

Watch: Seat height. If the handles sit above your shoulders the movement becomes a shoulder press with extra steps.

Decline & bodyweight presses

Decline Smith Bench Press Supino Declinado Smith

Pec major, lower · triceps, front delt · decline press · 1 of 19 programs · 6 sessions / 24 working sets · top 150 lbs · REGULAR

Head below hips, bar travelling from the lower chest outward. The decline angle shortens the range and takes the front delt almost entirely out of the movement, which is why the load matches your flat Smith press despite the shorter path. It appeared in ABC+D2 as one of three distinct pressing angles in the same block.

Hits: Lower pec, more cleanly isolated than any other press, with the triceps assisting. The front delt's contribution is the lowest of any pressing movement here, which is the whole argument for including it.

Purpose: Lower-chest work and a pressing pattern that gives the front delts a rest. In a block that already has flat and incline pressing, decline is the third angle that adds stimulus without adding shoulder wear.

Swap with: Weighted dips is the closest functional match and needs no bench at all. High-to-low cable fly for the same fibres with no pressing. Decline machine press if the Smith is in use.

Watch: Getting in and out of a decline bench under load. Set up, then have the bar handed to you or use the pins. People with blood-pressure issues should skip the head-down position entirely.

Dips Paralela

Pec major, lower · triceps, front delt · vertical press · 6 of 19 programs · 6 sessions / 22 working sets · top +35 lbs · REGULAR

The only chest press where you move your body instead of the weight, and the angle of your torso decides what it trains. Lean forward with the elbows flared and it is a lower-chest press; stay upright with the elbows tucked and it becomes one of the best triceps movements there is. Your programs treat it as a chest movement — it sits in the chest block in ABCD04, 06, 08, 09, 10 and 11, usually second or third in the session with a hundred and twenty seconds rest.

Hits: Lower pec and triceps roughly evenly, with the front delt taking a real share at the bottom. As shown here the figure reflects the chest-biased forward-lean version your programs prescribe.

Purpose: Heavy lower-chest and triceps pressing with a deep stretch at the bottom that no barred movement reproduces. It also loads the pattern with your own bodyweight, which is why it carries across six programs without needing a machine.

Swap with: Decline Smith or decline machine press for the same fibres with the range controlled. Dip machine or assisted dip if the bodyweight version is too much. Gironda dip — extreme forward lean, wide elbows — if you want maximum chest and minimum triceps.

Watch: The bottom. Descending until the shoulders roll forward past the elbows is the most reliable way to injure a shoulder in this atlas. Stop when the upper arm is roughly parallel to the floor and earn depth slowly.

Push-ups Apoio / Flexão

Pec major, mid · triceps, front delt, core · horizontal push · 3 of 19 programs · 4 sessions / 12 working sets · bodyweight · REGULAR

In ABC02 these were bi-set with the machine fly and taken to failure — which tells you exactly what they were for. Nobody puts push-ups after a machine fly for strength. They are there because they cost nothing to set up, they can be taken all the way to failure without risk, and the fly has already pre-exhausted the chest so a bodyweight movement is genuinely hard.

Hits: Mid pec, triceps and front delt, plus real isometric work from the abs and obliques holding the plank — a contribution no bench press makes.

Purpose: Failure work at the end of a chest block, with zero equipment and zero risk. When a program wants you to find the true end of a set, this is the safest place to do it.

Swap with: Machine chest press to failure if you want the load controlled. Weighted push-ups once bodyweight stops being difficult. Serratus dip or deficit push-up on handles to get more range.

Watch: The hips sagging as fatigue arrives. The moment the plank breaks, the movement stops being chest work and becomes lower-back work.

Weighted Push-ups Weighted Push Ups

Pec major, mid · triceps, front delt, core · horizontal push · 1 of 19 programs · 1 session / 4 working sets · bodyweight + plate · TRIED

A push-up with a plate on your back, opening ABC+D1's upper day with two warm-up sets and four working sets of eight to twelve. Putting a loaded push-up first in a session rather than last is an unusual choice and a defensible one — it warms the shoulder through a full range under moderate load before anything heavy happens.

Hits: Mid pec, triceps, front delt, with the core working harder than in an unloaded push-up because the plate wants to fold you in half.

Purpose: Loaded pressing that doubles as a shoulder warm-up. It fills the first slot of a day without committing the joint to a heavy barred lift cold.

Swap with: Push-ups plain if there is nobody to load your back. Machine chest press for the same early-session volume with easier loading. Dumbbell bench press if you want a real first press.

Watch: Plate placement — it belongs between the shoulder blades, not on the lower back, and you need someone to set it. Do not let the added load pull the hips into a sag.

Decline Machine Press Supino Máquina Declinado

Pec major, lower · triceps · decline press · 1 of 19 programs · 0 logged sessions · top — · PRESCRIBED

Added in ABC02 purely so that push day had five distinct chest lines. That is the honest reason — the intensification block kept the volume flat and made each set cost more, and a fifth pressing angle was part of how it filled the sheet without adding another heavy lift.

Hits: Lower pec with triceps support, back supported, front delt largely absent.

Purpose: A fifth chest angle at near-zero cost to recovery. Decline is the angle you add when flat and incline are already taken.

Swap with: Weighted dips, decline Smith press, or high-to-low cable fly — any of the three covers the lower chest better than this does.

Watch: Nothing much; it is a machine on a short range. The risk is spending a slot on it that a dip would use better.

Flyes & isolation

Flat Dumbbell Fly Crucifixo Halter Flat · Dumbell Fly (Flat)

Pec major, mid · front delt · chest fly · 4 of 19 programs · 19 sessions / 61 working sets · top 57 lbs · CORE

Arms nearly straight, opening wide and closing in an arc. Logged under two names in your app — the Portuguese in the ABC+D blocks and the English in ABCD09 — which together make it your most-used chest isolation movement by a wide margin. The defining property of a dumbbell fly is that gravity pulls straight down, so the movement is hardest at the bottom where the chest is stretched and almost free at the top where the hands meet. That is the opposite of a cable fly and the reason a program can justify running both.

Hits: Mid pec through a very long stretch, with the front delt assisting and the biceps working isometrically to hold the elbow angle. No triceps involvement at all, which is what separates a fly from a press.

Purpose: Chest work with the triceps removed. After two presses the triceps are usually the limiting factor; a fly lets you keep loading the chest once that is true. The deep stretch at the bottom is also the single best thing this movement offers.

Swap with: Cable fly keeps tension at the top where dumbbells lose it — a genuine upgrade if a cable station is free. Chest fly machine for a fixed path and easy failure work. Pec deck or dumbbell fly on the floor if the bottom position bothers your shoulder, since the floor caps the stretch.

Watch: Going too heavy turns a fly into a bad press — if the elbows are bending to move the weight, it is too much. The bottom is a real shoulder position; lower under control and do not bounce out of the stretch.

primary assist

Cable Fly Crucifixo na Polia

Pec major, mid · front delt · chest fly · 11 of 19 programs · 4 sessions / 12 working sets · top 36 lbs · REGULAR

The most widely prescribed fly in your history — eleven programs, from ABC01 all the way through ABCD08 — and the one where the resistance profile actually favours the part of the movement that matters. A cable pulls horizontally, so tension stays on the chest right through to the point where your hands meet, which is exactly where a dumbbell fly gives up. In ABC02 it picked up rest-pause work, which only makes sense on a movement that keeps loading the muscle at the finish.

Hits: Mid pec, with the emphasis sliding toward the upper or lower fibres depending on whether the cables are set low, level, or high. Front delt assists throughout.

Purpose: Constant-tension chest isolation and the natural home for fatigue techniques — rest-pause, drop sets, twenty-one style partials — because the cable never lets the muscle rest mid-rep.

Swap with: Chest fly machine for the same constant tension with less setup. Flat dumbbell fly if the cables are busy, accepting that you trade top-end tension for bottom-end stretch. Cable crossover if you want to exaggerate the squeeze past the midline.

5. Back

Thirty-three movements, and the most consistent structural rule in your whole history: every single program pairs at least one vertical pull with at least one horizontal pull. They train different things and neither substitutes for the other.

Vertical versus horizontal, and why both

A vertical pull — anything where the weight comes down from overhead — loads the lats through their longest range and builds width. A horizontal pull — anything you row toward your stomach — loads the lats through a shorter range but brings in the rhomboids and mid traps, which squeeze the shoulder blades together and build thickness. Drop the horizontal pull and your mid-back stops developing no matter how many pulldowns you do. This is the single most common structural mistake in self-written back training, and none of your nineteen programs makes it.

6. Shoulders

Seventeen movements, plus the two rotations that live in the prehab section. The deltoid is three muscles, and the structural lesson across nineteen programs is that pressing only trains one of them — which is why every program pairs a press with at least one raise and one reverse fly.

Why a press is not enough

The front delt is trained by every press you do, including every chest press, so it is almost never the limiting factor. The side delt is what makes shoulders look wide and it is trained almost exclusively by raises — a press involves it only partly. The rear delt is trained by rows and reverse flyes and never by pressing at all. So a shoulder day built on presses develops the one head that was already getting work from chest day and ignores the two that were not. Your programs know this: lateral raises appear in eleven, reverse flyes in eight.

7. Biceps

Fifteen movements, and the organising principle is elbow position rather than implement. Where your upper arm sits — behind you, beside you, or in front of you — decides which part of the curl is hardest, and every program picks two curls from different positions rather than two from the same one.

The three elbow positions

The biceps crosses both the elbow and the shoulder, so the shoulder's position changes the exercise. Arm behind the body — incline curl, Meadows curl — puts the biceps on stretch at the bottom and makes the first half of the rep hardest. Arm beside the body — standing barbell or cable curl — is hardest in the middle. Arm in front of the body — preacher and concentration curls — is hardest at the top, near full contraction. Pick two of the three and you have covered the whole strength curve; pick two from the same position and you have done the same exercise twice.

8. Triceps

Eighteen movements, and the organising fact is that the triceps has three heads but only one of them crosses the shoulder. That single anatomical detail explains why every program here pairs one overhead movement with one pushdown instead of running two of either.

Overhead versus pushdown, and why both

The long head of the triceps attaches above the shoulder joint, so it is only fully stretched when your arm is overhead. Put the elbow by your side and press down — a pushdown — and the long head is already slack, so the lateral and medial heads do most of the work. Raise the arm overhead — a French press, a skull crusher, an overhead extension — and the long head is loaded at length, where muscle grows best. Since the long head is the largest of the three, a triceps programme built only on pushdowns leaves most of the muscle under-trained. ABC01 gets this right in its very first session: a rope pushdown for the lateral head, then a kickback.

10. Quads

Nineteen movements. Quad volume is the single most-moved number across your nineteen programs — seventeen sets in ABC02, nine in ABC03, and back up again after — which makes this the section where the programming decisions are most visible.

Why so many squat variations

Every squat pattern trains the quads, but where the load sits changes which muscles share the work. A back squat puts the bar behind you, so the hips travel back and the glutes and lower back take a large share. A hack squat or Smith squat fixes your torso upright, which forces the knees forward and hands the work almost entirely to the quads. A sissy squat goes further still, driving the knees far past the toes with the hips extended, which isolates the quads more than any other pattern. And leg presses remove the spine from the equation altogether. Your programs move between these deliberately: the more quad-specific the block, the more upright the squat.

11. Hamstrings & Glutes

Curls versus hinges

A leg curl loads the hamstring's knee-bending function with the hip fixed. 12. Calves

Four movements. The calf is two muscles with different jobs, and the knee angle decides which one you are training — a fact that explains why a complete calf programme needs both a straight-leg and a bent-leg version.

Straight leg versus bent knee

The gastrocnemius — the visible diamond-shaped muscle — crosses both the ankle and the knee, so it only works properly with the leg straight. Bend the knee and it goes slack, at which point the soleus underneath takes over. That is the entire argument for seated calf raises existing alongside standing ones: standing trains the gastrocnemius, seated trains the soleus, and they are different muscles. Your programs lean heavily on the leg-press version, which is a straight-leg movement.

13. Abs & Core

Twenty-two movements — the largest section in the atlas, and the one with the most logged sessions of any muscle group. Crunch alone has fifty-six. The variety is not random: the trunk does three distinct jobs and your programs cover all three.

The three jobs of the trunk

Flexion — curling the ribs toward the pelvis — is what a crunch trains. Anti-extension — stopping the spine from being pulled into an arch — is what a plank, an ab wheel rollout and a dragon flag train, and it is a completely different quality from a crunch even though the same muscle does it. Rotation and anti-rotation is the obliques' job, trained by cross crunches, side planks with rotation and ball rotations. A program with only crunches trains one third of the trunk. Yours has all three, and in ABC04 abs moved from the last line of the day to the first — which is the standard fix for abs being skipped, and they had been skipped for three programs.

Rectus abdominis · obliques, glutes, front delt · anti-extension, static · 4 of 19 programs · 6 sessions / 6 working sets · 30–40 s holds · REGULAR

The static hold, prescribed in ABC01 as four sets of thirty to forty seconds bi-set with the rower crunch. Pairing a static anti-extension hold with a dynamic flexion movement in one bi-set is a tidy piece of programming — two of the trunk's three jobs in a single line.

Hits: Rectus abdominis isometrically, with the obliques, glutes and front delts all contributing to holding the line.

Purpose: Teaching and training the braced position. It is the entry point to anti-extension work and the regression for everything else in this group.

Swap with: Ab wheel for the dynamic version. Long lever plank with the elbows further forward if thirty seconds is easy. Plank on ball.

Watch: Hips sagging or riding high, and holding for time long past the point where the position has degraded. Thirty good seconds beats ninety bad ones; squeeze the glutes and tuck the ribs.

Plank on Ball Prancha na Bola

Rectus abdominis · obliques, front delt, serratus · anti-extension, unstable · 3 of 19 programs · 12 sessions / 12 working sets · holds · CORE

A plank with the forearms on a stability ball. The instability means the trunk is not just resisting extension but constantly correcting small wobbles in every direction, which recruits the obliques and the serratus far more than a floor plank does.

Hits: Rectus abdominis plus significantly more oblique and serratus work than a static floor plank, with the front delts stabilising the shoulder.

Purpose: Making a plank hard again without adding time. It also trains the serratus, which matters for overhead shoulder health and which almost nothing else in this atlas touches.

Swap with: Plank on the floor if the ball is too much. Ab wheel for a dynamic alternative. Stir-the-pot on the ball for a progression.

Watch: The hips sagging while you concentrate on the wobble. The position rules from the plain plank still apply.

A plank position with the knees driving alternately toward the chest. Prescribed once. It combines the anti-extension demand of a plank with alternating hip flexion, and the alternation adds a mild anti-rotation element as each leg moves.

A crunch angled to one side, or performed lying on the side, so the torso bends laterally rather than straight forward. Twenty sessions across five programs. Cross Crunch Abdominal Cruzado

Obliques · rectus abdominis · trunk rotation · 1 of 19 programs · 19 sessions / 23 working sets · bodyweight · CORE

A crunch that brings one elbow toward the opposite knee, so the trunk rotates as it flexes. Rotation is the obliques' primary function — they run diagonally across the abdomen precisely so they can twist the ribcage against the pelvis — and a straight crunch cannot train it at all.

Hits: Obliques through rotation, with the rectus abdominis contributing the flexion component.

Purpose: Rotational trunk work. Nineteen sessions in a single program means it was doing a job nothing else in that program did.

Swap with: Side plank with rotation for a harder version. Ball torso rotation for standing rotation. Russian twist or cable woodchop for loaded rotation.

Watch: Rotating from the neck rather than the ribcage. The shoulder should travel across, and the elbow reaching the knee is a result, not a target.

Holding a side plank and then rotating the torso, threading the top arm under the body and back out. It combines two things nothing else in the section does together: the lateral bracing of a side plank and active rotation under that brace. Thirteen sessions across four programs.

Hits: Obliques as the clear primary, doing both of their jobs at once, with the rectus abdominis, glutes and front delt holding the side-plank position.

Purpose: The most complete oblique movement in the atlas — lateral stability plus rotation in one rep. It is also the only one where the glutes contribute meaningfully.

Standing or kneeling, rotating a medicine ball from side to side. Standing rotation is a different pattern from lying rotation because the pelvis is free to move, so the movement has to be controlled by the trunk deciding how much of the rotation comes from the hips and how much from the ribcage.

Hits: Obliques through rotation, with the rectus abdominis and erectors working together to keep the spine stacked while it turns.

14. Prehab & Mobility

Two movements, eighty-seven logged sessions between them, sixteen of nineteen programs. They are the most consistent prescription in your entire training history and the cheapest insurance in the building.

External Shoulder Rotation Rotação Externa Ombro

Rotator cuff — infraspinatus, teres minor · rear delt · shoulder external rotation · 16 of 19 programs · 46 sessions / 43 working sets · top 6 lbs · CORE

Elbow at the side, forearm rotating outward against light resistance. It arrived in ABC04 along with its internal counterpart and never left — sixteen of the nineteen programs since, always at the very top of the day, one set of ten at six pounds. The timing of its arrival is the interesting part: ABC04 was the realization block where reps dropped to six to eight and the loads went up. Heavy pressing at six reps is exactly where shoulders start to complain, and the rotations appearing in that same block is not a coincidence.

Hits: The infraspinatus and teres minor — two of the four rotator cuff muscles — with the rear delt assisting. These sit behind the shoulder blade and their job is holding the ball of the arm centred in the socket while the big muscles move it.

Purpose: Keeping the shoulder joint tracking properly under heavy pressing. It builds nothing visible and prevents the single most common injury in this kind of training. Six pounds for ten reps costs ninety seconds and is the highest return per minute in the atlas.

Swap with: Cable external rotation for constant tension. Band external rotation, which is the most convenient version. Face pulls cover the same muscles as part of a bigger movement. Side-lying dumbbell external rotation.

Watch: Using any real weight. The cuff muscles are small and the moment you go heavy enough that the shoulder or the lats start helping, the exercise has stopped working. Six pounds is correct. Keep the elbow pinned to the side.

Internal Shoulder Rotation Rotação Interna Ombro

Rotator cuff — subscapularis · front delt, lat · shoulder internal rotation · 16 of 19 programs · 47 sessions / 44 working sets · top 6 lbs · CORE

The most-logged movement in this atlas after the crunch, and the partner to the external rotation — the two always appear together, one set of ten each, at the top of the day. This one trains the subscapularis, the cuff muscle on the front of the shoulder blade, which is the largest and strongest of the four and the one most often left out of prehab routines because people assume pressing covers it.

Hits: Subscapularis primarily, with the front delt and lat assisting. It sits on the inside face of the shoulder blade, which is why no diagram can show it properly — the figure here marks the cuff region generally.

Purpose: Completing the cuff. Training only external rotation leaves an imbalance; doing both keeps the joint centred in every direction. Together with the external rotation this is the pair that has appeared in sixteen consecutive programs.

Swap with: Cable internal rotation. Band internal rotation. Side-lying internal rotation with a light dumbbell. Dumbbell circles as broader shoulder prep, though it is less specific.

Watch: The same rule as its partner — the load stays light forever. Elbow at the side, forearm rotating across the body, no torso movement.

15. The One-Offs

Thirty-seven movements in your logs have one or two sessions and then stop. Most of them have full entries above, because a movement you tried twice is still a movement you might want to understand. But the pattern of why they stopped is worth reading as a group, because it falls into four clear categories and three of them are not failures.

Replaced by a better version of the same thing

This is the largest and most encouraging group. Skull crushers and triceps rope pushdown each appear in ten programs with one logged session, because you replaced them with the JM press and the barbell extension. Barbell curl and concentration curl gave way to the cable preacher and Arnold variants. Lateral raise became the Lu raise. Leg press became the quad-focused leg press. Bent over barbell row became the Yates row and the T-bar. In every case you kept the pattern and upgraded the execution, which is exactly what someone who understands their own training does.

Tried once, correctly abandoned

Hip abductor at two hundred and fifty-five pounds for one session is a machine being used as a novelty rather than for its actual purpose, which is light high-rep hip stability work. Rack pulls at two hundred and ten pounds appeared once and the movement it substitutes for — the deadlift — was never run either. Reverse grip pulldown and upright row both duplicate work that other movements in the same session already did better.

Logged once because the program only ran briefly

Incline barbell press, dumbbell bench press and machine shoulder press all sit in ABCD01 and ABCD02, which have two and four logged sessions respectively. These are not rejected movements; they are movements from programs that ended before they got a fair run. The same applies to anything tagged from ABC01X or ABCD14.

Same movement, different name

Several apparent one-offs are duplicates. Calf press is logged as three separate exercises, barbell curl as two, dumbbell lunges as two, and machine shoulder press as two. The atlas merges these, which is why some entries carry two Portuguese names in the header. If you want the app's Progress view to be accurate, consolidating these is the single highest-value cleanup available.

Deliberately left out

Four logged movements have no entry in this atlas because their names do not describe their mechanics and guessing would have been worse than omitting them: Cable Fly Estilo GVS, Jayme Lateral Raise, Crucifixo Adutor na Polia (22°) and Zercher Back Curl. These are either named after someone specific, tied to a particular piece of equipment in your gym, or your own variations. You know what they are and this atlas does not, so rather than invent an explanation it leaves them to you. If you tell me how any of them is performed, they can be written up properly.

16. Swap Logic: Substituting Without Breaking the Program

Every entry above has a Swap with line, but the lines are only useful if you know what you are allowed to trade away. Here is the general rule, in order of how expensive each swap is.

Free: changing the implement

Barbell for dumbbell, dumbbell for machine, cable for free weight — as long as the movement pattern and the joint angles stay the same, the swap costs almost nothing. A Smith bench press for a barbell bench press, a T-bar row for a bent-over row, a Hammer press for a machine press. You lose or gain some stabiliser work and the absolute load changes, but the muscle being trained does not. Make these swaps freely whenever a station is busy.

Cheap: changing within the same pattern

Swapping a wide-grip pulldown for a V-bar pulldown, a flat dumbbell fly for a cable fly, a seated curl for a preacher curl. You are still training the same muscle in the same plane; what changes is where in the range it is hardest. Over a single session this barely matters. Over a block it matters, so pick one and keep it for the block rather than rotating week to week.

Expensive: changing the angle

Incline for flat, seated leg curl for lying leg curl, overhead extension for pushdown. These look like small changes and they are not — each one moves the emphasis to a different portion of the muscle. Swapping an incline press for a flat press means the upper chest gets less work that day, full stop. If you make this swap, make it knowingly and check whether anything else in the session covers what you just dropped.

Costly: changing the pattern

A row for a pulldown, a hinge for a leg curl, a press for a raise. These are not substitutions; they are different exercises that happen to be in the same section of the atlas. Trading a Romanian deadlift for a seated leg curl removes hip extension from your training entirely. Trading a bent-over row for a pulldown removes the horizontal pull. If you do this, you are not substituting — you are editing the program, and you should decide whether you meant to.

The three-question test

Before any swap, ask: Does it train the same muscle? — check the Hits line and the figure. Does it use the same pattern? — check the meta line. Does it sit at the same point in the strength curve? — hardest at the stretch, the middle, or the contraction. Three yeses and the swap is free. Two and it is a reasonable variation. One and you have changed the program.

What to do when a joint complains

The most common reason to swap is not a busy rack but a joint that has started talking. The general principle is to keep the pattern and reduce the range or change the grip before you drop the movement altogether. A shoulder that hurts on a barbell bench often tolerates a Smith or a neutral-grip dumbbell press. An elbow that hurts on a straight-bar curl usually tolerates an EZ bar or a rope. A knee that hurts on a sissy squat variant tolerates a leg extension. And the movement that most often fixes a cranky pressing shoulder is not a different press at all — it is adding face pulls and keeping the two rotations that sixteen of your programs already prescribe.
